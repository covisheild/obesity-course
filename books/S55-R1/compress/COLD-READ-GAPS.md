4. Relate: case-control (it starts from the outcome and looks back at exposure).

## Part 3. What the text taught me that would change what I do, say, accept or refuse

1. Refuse to plan from a topic. I would demand one sentence that asks one thing.
2. Write several candidates and reject most of them in writing. I would distrust the first question that arrives, whatever its source (the guide, a paper's closing line, a senior).
3. Fence every population by place, person and time. I would label P/I/E, C and O. If a part cannot be labelled, it is not there. Two outcomes means two questions.
4. Obesity can be the exposure or the outcome; check which.
5. Read the question's verb, then match the design: describe → cross-sectional; relate → cohort or case-control; cause → RCT. I would refuse "causes", "leads to" or "effect of" from a one-visit survey. I would not fix a time-order problem with a bigger sample.
6. Separate value questions from empirical ones. I would leave the "should" decision with its owner.
7. Run the feasibility list in writing. "Should be possible" counts as a fail until asked.
8. Before keeping a question: two plausible answers, a search for an existing answer, and a named action for each answer.
9. Never quote an "uncited" share without the database and what it counted, the publication year, and the window. I would refuse "half of all papers are never cited". Being cited is not being used.
10. Name a reachable role with a real decision. Ask them what they would do with each answer and write down their words and the date. "Interesting" is not an action. The guide and the degree are not waiting parties. Anyone hoping for one result is not waiting. I would be careful with money from a party that gains from one answer.
11. Apply the zero-rule before ranking. Give one checkable line per rejection. I would never quote a percentage difference between scores, and when totals are close I would decide from the items.
