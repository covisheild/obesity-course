# S55-R1-C07 · Who is waiting for this answer, and what will they do differently

**Definition.** The who-is-waiting test asks two things of a candidate research question.

First, name the person or body that needs the answer for a decision they actually face. Examples
are a district programme officer, a clinic, a guideline group, a state nutrition programme,
patients and their families, or a funder. "The literature", "future researchers" and "my thesis"
do not pass this part, because each can be said of any question.

Second, for each answer the study could give, state what that person or body will do. The
question passes only when at least two possible answers lead to different actions. If every
possible answer leads to the same action, the answer changes nothing for them, and nobody is
waiting for it.

The test is a sharper form of criteria in published guides. Jhangiani and colleagues count an
answer's practical implications as one reason a question is worth asking. Ratan and colleagues
ask that a question bear on health decisions made by one or more groups with a stake in them.
Ioannidis calls clinical research useful when it can lead to a better decision. The two-part form,
and the rule that the same action for every answer fails, are this course's.

Chalmers and colleagues, writing in the Lancet in 2014, name clinicians and patients as the
principal users of clinical and epidemiological research. Both, they report, are often frustrated
by mismatches between the uncertainties they want addressed and the questions researchers choose
to investigate. One reason they give is that users of research are only rarely involved in
setting research agendas.

A funder is one kind of waiting party. Money to find an answer is research money. Money to carry a
programme out is implementation money, and it is a different kind of money.

**In plain terms.** The last section showed that being cited is not the same as being used. So judge a question by who
is waiting for its answer, not by whether it will be cited. This section turns that into a test
you can run on paper.

Take one candidate question, written as one sentence the way the section on population, exposure
and outcome taught. Then ask two things.

**Part 1: who needs this answer?** Name a person or a body with a decision in front of them.

Here are some kinds. A district programme officer deciding what goes on a screening camp's checklist. A clinic deciding
whether to phone patients who miss a visit. A guideline group. Patients and their families. A
funder. Name a role you could go and talk to.

Three answers fail Part 1 every time: "the literature", "future researchers" and "my thesis".
Anyone can say them about any question, so they tell you nothing about this one. Your degree does
need a thesis. But it waits for a thesis, not for this answer.

**Part 2: what will they do with each answer?** Write down what the person will do if the answer
comes out one way. Then write what they will do if it comes out the other way. If the two actions
differ, someone is waiting. If they do the same thing either way, nobody is waiting, however
important the topic sounds.

Do not guess Part 2. Go and ask the person. Put it to them as two questions. "If the study found
this, what would you do? And if it found that?" Write down their words.

Chalmers and colleagues report that the users of research are only rarely involved in setting
what gets studied. Clinicians and patients, they add, are often frustrated by the mismatch.

The test is an argument, in the sense of Book 0's section on premise, inference and conclusion.
The premises are "this person faces this decision" and "they told me they do one thing for one
answer and another thing for the other". The conclusion is "they are waiting for this answer".
Check the premises: did they say it, or did you guess it? Check the inference: are the two
actions really different?

Published guides say something close to this. Ratan and colleagues ask that a question bear on
health decisions made by some group with a stake in them. Ioannidis calls clinical research useful
when it can lead to a better decision. The two-part test is this course's sharper version.

A funder is one kind of party that can be waiting. There are two kinds of money. Research money
pays to find out an answer. Implementation money pays to carry a programme out.

You will meet three names for implementation money: company CSR (corporate social responsibility)
funds, programme grants and government tenders. The next book in this subject teaches them. This
book says nothing about the rules for any of them.

**Illustration.** Here is the setting this book uses throughout. It is made up. You are a postgraduate in community
medicine at a medical college in Chhattisgarh. Your guide wants a first research question on
obesity by the end of the month. The people around you are your guide, your department, and the
district officer who runs the non-communicable disease (NCD) programme.

Suppose your first candidate is this one.

"What share of children in classes 6 to 8 at government schools in Raipur city have overweight or
obesity, measured by weight and height, in the 2027 school year?"

Run Part 1. The answer most people write first is "It will add to the literature, and future
researchers can use it." Strike it out. It names nobody.

Try again, and name someone: the district NCD programme officer. Now run Part 2. You go to her
office and ask the two questions. "If the study finds a high share, what will you do? If it finds
a low share?"

Suppose she answers like this. "The school visits for this year are already planned and paid for.
Whatever your number is, the plan stays the same."

Her two actions are the same. So she is not waiting for this answer. That does not make the
question wrong. It means you have not yet found anyone who needs it.

Then she mentions a decision she does face. At the district NCD screening camp, adults are weighed
and measured for height. She is deciding whether to buy tape measures and train staff to measure
waists as well. That adds minutes for every person screened.

That gives you a second candidate.

"Among adults screened at the district NCD screening camp in 2027 whose body mass index is below
the camp's cut-off, what share have a waist measurement above the camp's cut-off?"

Ask the same two questions. Suppose she says: "If it is more than about one in ten, I add the tape
measure. If it is fewer, I leave the checklist as it is." Both her answers and her one-in-ten are
part of the made-up conversation.

Set the two candidates side by side.

```table
| Step | School survey | Camp waist question |
| Who is waiting | district NCD programme officer | district NCD programme officer |
| If the answer is high | same school plan | adds tape measures and trains staff |
| If the answer is low | same school plan | leaves the checklist as it is |
| Different actions? | no | yes |
| Result | fails | passes |
```

Now lay the second one out as an argument. Premise: she decides whether the camp measures waists.
Premise: she said she adds the tape measure if more than about one in ten are missed, and not if
fewer are. Conclusion: she is waiting for this answer.

Check the premises. She said both things herself, so you did not guess them. Check the inference.
Adding tape measures and leaving the checklist alone are different actions, so it carries.

Notice what her one-in-ten gave you. You now know which result would change her decision before
you collect any data. Ask every waiting party for that line.

**Where this picture breaks.** The test is only as good as what the person tells you, and plans change. An officer can be
transferred, or a budget cut, before your results arrive. Ask again before you write up.

A guideline group rarely acts on one study. Ioannidis counts research as useful when it changes a
decision "either by itself or when integrated with other studies". For such a group, ask what they
would do with the pooled evidence your study joins, not with your study alone.

Some people want one answer and not the other. A company that sells tape measures would be glad of
a high share. That person is not waiting for an answer. They are hoping for a result.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to write "this will add to the literature" or "future researchers can use it" in the place of a person. Anyone can say that of any question, so it tells you nothing. Name a role you could go and talk to.
- Ask the person; do not guess for them. Put two questions: "If the study found this, what would you do? And if it found that?" Write down their words, with the date.
- Ask each waiting person which result would change their decision. That line tells you what the study has to be able to tell apart. If they cannot name one, press for it before you start.
- "That would be very interesting" is not an action. When an officer or a head of department says it, ask again: "And what would you do differently?"
- If every answer leads to the same action, drop the question for that party. Or find the party for whom the answers differ. A study nobody is waiting for can be done well and still change nothing.
- Your guide and your degree need a thesis. They are waiting for a thesis, not for this answer. Ioannidis notes that trainees are judged on how fast they publish. Count neither as the waiting party.
- Treat money from a party who gains from one answer with care. That party has a conflict of interest: something to gain from one result. Ioannidis and colleagues note that research is often done by people with conflicts of interest that favour specific results. The screen for this comes with the kinds of money in the next book.
- The test tells you someone would act on the answer. It does not tell you the question can be answered, that the study will be done well, or that the action is a good one. Run it together with the checks for an answerable question and an existing answer.
- A textbook may call a question interesting because it interests the scientific community. That is a fair reason to publish. It does not pass this test, because it names no decision.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A made-up draft protocol for a thesis on obesity in college students says:

"This study will be useful to policy makers and will add to the literature on obesity in young
adults in India."

Lay the claim out as premises and a conclusion. Run the who-is-waiting test on it. Then say what
you would need to find out to rewrite the line.

**Exercise 2** (design). Here is a made-up candidate question.

"Among adults with obesity attending the district hospital NCD clinic in 2027, do those who get a
phone call the day before their follow-up visit come back more often than those who do not?"

Name two people or bodies who might be waiting for the answer. For each, write the two questions
you would put to them. Then say what reply would pass the test, and what reply would fail it.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A first-year resident in your department says: "Every question is useful to someone, so the
who-is-waiting test rejects nothing." You have ten minutes and a whiteboard. Explain why the test
does reject questions, with one example that fails and one that passes.
