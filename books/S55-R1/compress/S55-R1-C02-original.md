# S55-R1-C02 · Population, exposure, outcome: a question in one sentence

**Definition.** A research question can be written as one sentence with up to four parts.

The population is the group the answer is about. It is bounded by place, by person (age, and
any other trait that decides who is in) and by time.

The exposure is what differs between the people being compared: something they have, do or
meet. When a researcher or a programme gives it on purpose, as the thing being tested, it is
called the intervention.

The comparison is the group, or the level of exposure, that the exposed group is set against.
Aslam and Emmanuel call it the control; Morgan and colleagues call it the comparator.

The outcome is what is measured to answer the question. A question has one main outcome.

The four parts are known by their initials. PICO stands for population, intervention,
comparison, outcome, and is used for a question about something given on purpose. PECO stands
for population, exposure, comparison, outcome, and is used for an exposure that people meet in
the ordinary course of their lives.

A question with a population and an outcome, and no exposure or comparison, asks how common or
how large the outcome is in that population. A question with an exposure and a comparison asks
whether the outcome differs between the groups.

**In plain terms.** The last section ended with four candidate questions on the table. Before you can compare them,
each one has to say clearly what it asks. The way to check is to write it as one sentence and
find four parts in it.

**Population** — who the answer is about. Book 0 defined the population as the whole group a
question is about, and the sample as the part of it that is measured.

Here the population needs a
fence round it on three sides. Place: which district, which schools. Person: what ages, and
anything else that decides who is in. Time: which year. "School children" has no fence.
"Children aged 10 to 14 in government schools in Raipur district in the 2027–28 school year" has
all three.

**Exposure** — what differs between the people you compare. It is something they have, do or
meet: walking to school, a night shift, living near a sweet shop. When someone gives it on
purpose, as the thing being tested, it is called the intervention: a text-message reminder the
clinic sends, say.

**Comparison** — the group you set the exposed group against. Children who walk to school are
compared with children who are driven. Without a comparison, "children who walk have obesity
less often" has nothing to be less often than.

**Outcome** — what you measure to answer the question. Obesity judged from measured weight and
height is an outcome. So is coming back for the next clinic visit. In Book 0's section on
probability, "outcome" meant one possible result of a chance process. Here it means the thing
you measure. One question has one main outcome.

The four parts have short names made from their first letters. PICO is population,
intervention, comparison, outcome. Use it when someone gives the thing being tested. PECO is
population, exposure, comparison, outcome. Use it when people meet the exposure in their
ordinary lives.

Not every question needs all four. "What share of these children have obesity?" has a
population and an outcome, and nothing else. It asks how common something is. That is a proper
question. What it cannot do is tell you whether any group differs from any other.

Writing the question this way has a second use, later. When you search for what others have
already found, you build the search from these same parts.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to think "school children" is a population. A population has a fence of place, person and time. Without one you cannot say whom the answer is about, and you cannot draw a sample from it. Refuse to plan a sample until the sentence names all three.
- Write the question as one sentence, then label its population, exposure or intervention, comparison and outcome. If you cannot label a part, the question does not have it. That is fine for a how-common question, and a gap in any question that compares groups.
- Obesity can be the exposure or the outcome. "Do adults with obesity miss more clinic visits?" has obesity as the exposure. Decide which thing differs between your groups at the start, and which thing you measure to answer. Do not let the topic word fill the outcome slot by habit.
- A question with two outcomes is two questions. "Does a games period lower obesity and raise exam marks?" can come out yes for one and no for the other. Split it, and keep the one you would choose if you could only have one.
- Morgan and colleagues report, from an earlier review they cite, that over half (54%) of 313 research studies did not report all four PICO parts. This course has not read that review. So when you read a paper, find its population, exposure, comparison and outcome yourself. Do this before you accept what it says it answered.
- A trainee brings a title like "Prevalence and risk factors of obesity in urban slums". Ask them to read out their one outcome and their one exposure. When they list several risk factors, ask which one they would keep if they could only keep one.
- The four-part check tells you a question is complete and asks one thing. It does not tell you whether the question can be answered, whether the answer is already known, or whether anyone needs it. A perfectly formed sentence can still be the wrong question to ask.

**Exercise 1** (design). Go back to the four made-up candidates in the last section's table. They were about the Raipur
schools, the district screening camp, meal timing at your college, and whether clinic patients
know their own weight. Rewrite each as one sentence with its population fenced by place, person and

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A first-year resident has ten minutes and a whiteboard. Their draft title is "Risk factors of
obesity among college students". Teach them why "risk factors" is not an exposure, and help
them rewrite the title as one question.

**1.** Label the population, the exposure, the comparison and the outcome in this question.

Among adults aged 40 to 60 living in one town in 2025, do those who work night shifts, compared
with those who work day shifts, have a higher average weight?

**2.** Label the parts of each question. Say where a part is missing.

a. Among children aged 6 to 10 in the schools of one block in 2026, what share have obesity?
b. Among patients of one clinic in 2026, does a weekly phone call from a nurse, compared with
   no call, raise the share who come to their next visit?
c. Do people who sleep less weigh more?

**3.** Label the parts of this question. The parts are not in the usual order.

Is the weight gained over one year by first-year students of one college in 2027 greater among
those living in the hostel than among those living at home?

**4.** Take the made-up topic "obesity in school children in Raipur", from this book's setting. Write
a one-sentence how-common question on it. Fence the population by place, person and time, and
say how the outcome is judged. Label the parts.

**5.** Take the made-up topic "what school children eat at lunch", from this book's setting. Write a
one-sentence PECO question on it, with a comparison. Label the parts.

**6.** A made-up report on the district screening camp ends with this sentence:

"Adults who lived within 2 km of the camp site came back for a second check more often than
adults who lived further away."

Write the one-sentence question this sentence answers. Label its parts. Say which part the
report leaves unfenced.

**7.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
question: Among adults aged 30 to 60 attending the district hospital's NCD clinic in 2026, do those with obesity, compared with those without, miss more follow-up visits?
population: adults aged 30 to 60 attending the NCD clinic in 2026
exposure: missing follow-up visits
comparison: adults without obesity
outcome: obesity
```

**8.** Here is a worked answer. Find the steps that broke.

```working
question: Among school children in Chhattisgarh, does a daily games period, compared with none, reduce obesity and raise exam marks?
population: school children in Chhattisgarh
intervention: a daily games period
comparison: no games period
outcome: obesity and exam marks
verdict: all four parts are present, so the question is ready
```
