# S55-R1-C06 · What happens to studies once published: citation, uncitedness and use

**Definition.** A citation is an entry in the reference list of a later published work that points to an earlier
one. A citation database records the reference lists of the journals it indexes. For each paper
it holds, it counts how many of those later works cite it. Web of Science, owned by Clarivate, is
the database behind most of the figures below; Scopus is another. A paper is uncited, in a given
database, when that count is zero.

Every count of uncited papers rests on three choices. The first is the database, and which kinds
of document in it are counted. The second is the set of papers, usually all those published in
one year. The third is the citation window: how long after publication the counting runs.
Golosovsky and Larivière call the share of a set of papers still uncited at the end of the window
the uncitedness ratio. It depends strongly on the length of the window, and it goes on falling as
the window lengthens.

The measurements depend on those choices. A 1990 report in Science found 55% of items published
from 1981 to 1985 uncited 5 years later. It counted letters, corrections, meeting abstracts and
other editorial material, which are rarely cited.

Counting research articles and reviews in Web of Science, Larivière and Sugimoto found 4% of biomedical papers published in 2006 uncited by December
2017. Of 39 million papers from all fields published from 1900 to 2015, 21% were uncited. These
figures come from an analysis done for a news feature in Nature, and the data behind its charts were
not released.

From Sugimoto and Larivière's 2018 book, Golosovsky and Larivière report that 12% of
medical-science papers published in 1990 were uncited after 27 years.

Nicolaisen and Frandsen counted in Scopus. They took seven document types published from 1996 to
2015, among them articles, reviews, letters and notes, and counted citations up to 6 December
2018. So the window is open: a paper of 1996 had about 22 years to be cited, and a paper of 2015
about 3. They give each result as an uncitedness ratio, a share written as a fraction of 1, so
0.23 means 23 in every 100. For medicine, all seven types together, the ratio was 0.23. For
medical articles alone it was 0.18, and for medical notes 0.64. The authors treat such 20-year
totals only as overall indications, because younger papers generally have a higher uncited ratio
than older ones.

A database misses citations: a paper it records as uncited may have been cited in a journal or book
it does not index, or missed through an error. So the true share uncited is probably lower than the
database shows. Counting a paper's citations by its own authors pushes the other way: removing those
raises the share uncited.

A citation records that one work referred to another. It does not record whether the earlier work
was used, and many citations are superficial. An uncited paper may still be read and used.

One study held for this course sorts papers by design.

Patsopoulos, Analatos and Ioannidis took
2646 articles published in 1991 and 2001 from the Science Citation Index in Web of Science. They
sorted them into seven designs by the design named in the title; the second example below shows
five of them. They counted each article's citations to the end of the second year after
publication and gave the median for each design, not a share uncited. Meta-analyses, which
combine earlier studies, had the highest medians. Case reports, on one patient or a few, received
negligible citations. The study has no cross-sectional category. So no held measurement says how
often descriptive cross-sectional studies are cited, or go uncited.

**In plain terms.** The last section asked whether an answer would change anything. You will also hear a quicker test
of a finished study: was it cited? A citation is an entry in a later paper's reference list that
points back to the study. Book 0's section on checking a reference covered what a reference is.
This section is about counting them.

Here is the trap with citation figures. The same word, "uncited", gives numbers from under 1% to
over 50%. That is not because some of them are wrong. It is because each one counted something
different. Every figure rests on three choices.

- **The database, and what it counts.** A database such as Web of Science holds the reference
  lists of the journals it covers, about 12,000 of them. It cannot see a citation in a journal or
  book it does not cover. Some counts include letters and corrections, which are rarely cited.
- **Which papers.** Usually all the papers of one field published in one year.
- **How long you wait.** Counting stops at some date. The longer you wait, the fewer papers stay
  uncited.

So before you use any figure for uncited papers, ask three questions. Which database, counting
what? Papers from which year? Counted until when?

Three more facts change how you read a citation count.

The database misses citations. In one check, a biologist named Heneberg looked at the Web of
Science records of 13 Nobel prizewinners. At first glance about 1.6% of their papers looked uncited.
After about 20 hours of checking by hand, he got it down to 0.3%. The rest had been cited, and the
database had missed it. Papers by Indian authors look less cited in Web of Science than papers from
the United States and Europe. But the database leaves out many regional-language journals, and
counting them would narrow that gap.

Being cited is not being used. Papers in Web of Science with one or two citations outnumber papers
with none, and many citations are superficial. A reference can be one line in a long list.

Being uncited is not being useless. A botanist's short report of a plant found in Texas was cited
once, by the botanist himself. Its information is still used, because it went into plant atlases
and databases. A paper written to improve public-health practice was never cited, and it was
viewed more than 1,500 times.

The outline this course was planned from carried the sentence "most descriptive cross-sectional
studies are never cited". A cross-sectional study, as an earlier section showed, measures a group
once, at one time. That sentence was checked before this book was written. The measurements below
do not support it as written. The one study held that sorts papers by design has no
cross-sectional category, so it cannot test the sentence either. This book does not state it as a
fact.

**Must know points for you.**

- Never quote a share of uncited papers without its three labels. Name the database and what it counted. Name the year the papers were published. Name how long the count ran. In Scopus, for medical papers of 1996 to 2015 counted to 6 December 2018, the uncitedness ratio was 0.18 for articles and 0.64 for notes. Both are true. Without the labels, neither means anything.
- The belief that more than half of all papers are never cited is an error. It goes back to a 1990 count that included letters, corrections and meeting abstracts, over only 5 years. A later count in Web of Science took research articles and reviews only. It found 4% of biomedical papers published in 2006 uncited by December 2017, a figure reported in a news feature. Refuse the "half" figure when you hear it.
- Read "uncited" as "no citation found in this database by this date", not as a fact about the paper. A database misses citations, so the true share uncited is probably lower than it shows. Before you call a paper uncited, search for it in a second database and on the open web.
- Web of Science shows papers by Indian authors as less often cited than papers from the United States and Europe. But it leaves out many regional-language journals, and counting them would narrow the gap. Before concluding that a department's work goes unread, find out whether the database covers the journals it was published in.
- Do not use citation as your test of whether a study was worth doing. A citation can be one superficial line, and an uncited paper can be read and used. Test a question by who needs its answer and what they would do with it, as the last section began to do.
- When someone says a kind of study, such as a descriptive cross-sectional study, is "never cited", ask for the study that counted it. It should name its database, how it sorted papers by design, the years and the window. The one held count by design, of Web of Science articles of 1991 and 2001, has no cross-sectional category. Until you see one that has, the claim is an impression, not a finding.
- A paper's title is not its finding. Golosovsky and Larivière's paper is called "Uncited papers are not useless". It shows that uncited papers follow the same statistical pattern as cited ones. Most of them, it finds, have some chance of being cited given enough time. It does not measure whether anyone used them. Cite it for what its body shows.
- What would overturn these figures is a peer-reviewed count in another database that gave very different shares. It would have to count the same kinds of document over the same windows. Until then, quote them as what they are: counts in Web of Science and Scopus. Several of them were reported through a news feature whose data were not released.
