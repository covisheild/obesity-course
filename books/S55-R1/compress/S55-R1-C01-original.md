# S55-R1-C01 · A research question is a choice

**Definition.** A research topic names an area of interest, such as obesity in adolescents. A research
question is a sentence that asks one thing a study will find out about a topic. A study is
built to answer one main question.

A research question is chosen when it is one of several candidates, set side by side, and the
others are turned down. A question can also reach a researcher without that comparison. It can
be named by a supervisor, or set by the data or the patients already at hand. It can be taken
from the lines on future research at the end of a paper, or picked because it can be finished
quickly. Each of
these routes can supply a good candidate. None of them is a choice until the candidate has
been compared with others.

The question is the first decision in a study. The methods that follow answer the question
that was asked. None of them can turn it into a better question.

**In plain terms.** A topic is an area, like "obesity in school children". A question asks one thing you will find
out, like "What share of children aged 10 to 14 in one district's government schools have
obesity?" You cannot plan a study from a topic. You can plan one from a question, because the
question tells you whom to find and what to measure.

Questions reach people in several ways. A guide names a topic. The department already holds some
records, so the question is whatever those records can answer. A paper ends with a line such as
"future studies should look at meal timing", and that becomes the question. Or a question gets
picked because it looks quick to finish.

None of these routes is wrong in itself. Research methods textbooks recommend two of them. Start
from what is already around you. Read the end of a recent paper. The trouble starts when the first question to arrive is the one you keep, without
setting it beside any other.

Choosing means this. You write down several candidates, put them side by side, and turn most of
them down. Researchers think of many more questions than they ever try to answer. So they need a
way to pick.

The question is the first decision in a study, and the one that matters most. A careful sample,
a good measurement and a correct analysis all answer the question you asked. None of them can
make it the right question to have asked.

**Illustration.** Here is the setting this book uses from start to finish. It is made up. You are a postgraduate
student in community medicine at a medical college in Chhattisgarh. You must propose a first
research question on obesity. Around you are your guide and your department. There is also a
district programme officer for non-communicable diseases (NCDs) — long-term illnesses that do
not pass from one person to another.

Start with what happens when nobody chooses. Suppose your guide says, in your first week, "Do
something on obesity in school children." You write that at the top of a page as your
question. Then you try to plan the study from it.

Ask the page three things. Which children: what ages, which schools, where? What will you
measure: weight, meals, games periods, screen time, or all of them? Whom will you compare with
whom? The page cannot answer any of them. "Obesity in school children" is a topic. Blackstone's
textbook says the same of its own examples: to give a topic as your research question "would
not be correct".

Now suppose, in the same made-up week, three more candidates reach you. Here are all four, each
written as a question, with the route by which it arrived.

```table
| How it reached you | The candidate, written as a question |
| Your guide named a topic | What share of children aged 10 to 14 in government schools in Raipur district have obesity? |
| The department already holds weight and height records from last year's district screening camp | Among adults screened at that camp, did those from the town have obesity more often than those from villages? |
| The last paper you read ended with a line on future research into meal timing | Among students at your college, do those who usually eat dinner late gain more weight over one year than those who eat early? |
| A senior told you to pick something you could finish quickly | What share of patients at the district hospital's NCD clinic know their own weight? |
```

Look at the left column. You chose none of the four. Each one arrived.

That is not a fault in any of them. Blackstone's textbook recommends "starting where you are":
from an interest you already have, or from patterns in your everyday life. Jhangiani and
colleagues recommend the discussion section of a recent paper, where authors suggest future
research. The fault would be to keep the first question that arrives, because it arrived first.

Choosing starts here. Put the four side by side. You do not yet have the tests that decide
between them; the rest of this book builds them. You do have the one move that makes this a
choice rather than an accident. Decide now that most of these will be turned down, and that
you will write down the reason for each one.

**Where this picture breaks.** The four routes are not a complete list. A real question often arrives by two at once: a guide
may name a topic because the department holds data on it. The table sorts candidates by
how they arrived. It says nothing about which one is best. A question handed over by a guide
can win the comparison, and the guide may have reasons you have not heard yet. Ask for them.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to write a topic where the question should be. "Obesity in school children" cannot tell you whom to find or what to measure. Refuse to plan a single step until you have a sentence that asks one thing.
- Before you accept any question, write down how it reached you. Then write at least two other candidates on the same topic and set all of them side by side. A question with no rivals has not been chosen.
- The future-research line at the end of a paper is a good place to find a candidate. It was written for the authors' own field and data, not for your district. Take it as one candidate among several, never as the choice already made.
- A careful sample and a correct analysis answer the question that was asked. If that question was the wrong one, the study is well done and still no use to anyone. Spend your hardest thinking on the question, before the methods, because nothing later can repair it.
- When a trainee says "my guide gave me the topic", do not argue about the guide. Ask them to write three questions inside that topic and say which two they would drop, and why. That turns a topic they were given into a choice they made.
- Knowing how a question arrived tells you only whether it was compared with others. It does not tell you whether the question is good. An assigned or borrowed question can win a fair comparison. Judge it by the tests, not by where it came from.
