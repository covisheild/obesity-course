"Recurring" as defined in the S55-R1-C01 file.

1. **`simplified_explanation`: prevalence defined as a count** (from compression hole r1-H13)
   - **Claim:** "The count is the prevalence — how many people in a group have the condition."
   - **What the source says:** CDC says a cross-sectional study assesses "the presence (prevalence)
     of the health outcome at that point of time". Its examples are shares ("prevalence of smoking").
     Book 0 (B0-R0-C26) defines prevalence with the same words but writes it "P(condition present)",
     a probability, which is a share. Every describe question in this section asks "what share".
     "The count is the prevalence" teaches a number of people, the confusion A4 separates.
   - **Smallest fix:** "The share you count is the prevalence: the share of a group who have the
     condition at that time."
   - **Tag:** error.

2. **`simplified_explanation`, last paragraph: "all the studies" is stronger than the source**
   - **Claim:** "It collects and combines the results of all the studies already done on one question."
   - **What the source says:** Aslam and Emmanuel: "Systematic reviews are rigorous methods of
     collecting and synthesizing the results of many high-quality studies." The source says
     "many high-quality", not "all".
   - **Smallest fix:** "It collects and combines the results of the studies already done on one
     question, in a planned and careful way."
   - **Tag:** error. **Recurring** (claim stronger than its source, SELFCHECK 1 and 4; S36 defects).

3. **`definition.text` and `simplified_explanation`: the systematic review sentence is repeated and has no place in the three kinds** (from compression hole r1-H12)
   - **Claim:** "A systematic review is a study of studies" appears in both fields. It is not in the
     table, the retrieval items or any exercise.
   - **Audit finding:** confirmed. The reader is not told which question a review answers: any of
     the three, by pooling studies of that kind. Nor is it linked to the "reviews" of C05 or the
     "meta-analysis" of C06.
   - **Smallest fix:** cut the definition's copy. After the simplified paragraph (as fixed in item 2),
     add: "A review can answer any of the three kinds, by pooling studies built for that kind."
   - **Tag:** gap.

4. **`definition.text`: when a personal trait is a describe category and when it is an exposure** (from compression hole r1-H9)
   - **Claim:** a descriptive question asks "in which people it is commoner. It names no exposure."
     C02 defines the exposure as "something they have, do or meet".
   - **Audit finding:** confirmed. By C02's words an age group is "something they have", yet exercise
     1 question 5 (age groups) is marked describe with "no exposure you are testing". CDC section 6
     puts age, sex and other personal traits under "person" in *descriptive* epidemiology. That
     supports the answer, but the text never says so.
   - **Smallest fix:** add to the definition: "Counting the outcome by age, sex or place is still
     describing. A trait becomes an exposure when the question sets two groups side by side to ask
     whether the trait goes with the outcome." Cite the CDC section 6 "person" passage.
   - **Tag:** gap.

5. **Exercise 1 answer 2; `simplified_explanation`: nothing says a cohort starts from people without the outcome** (from compression hole r1-H14, residual)
   - **Claim:** question 2 asks which children "develop obesity over two years". The answer says
     "weigh the children at the start, and weigh them again in two years."
   - **What the source says:** CDC: the cohort investigator "tracks the participants to see if they
     develop the disease of interest". To see who develops the outcome, you leave out those who
     already have it at the start. Neither the text nor the answer says this.
   - **Smallest fix:** in the cohort bullet: "You start with people who do not yet have the outcome."
     In the answer: "leave out children who already have obesity at the start".
   - **Tag:** gap.
   - The rest of r1-H14 is withdrawn: two worked illustrations, each with a limits block, now exist.

6. **`definition.text`, `simplified_explanation`, illustration 1: one question type, three names**
   - **Claim:** C02 names it "a how-common question". C03 calls it "a descriptive question"
     (definition), "describe" (table, exercises) and "a how-common question" (illustration). The
     same pattern repeats for relational/relate and causal/cause.
   - **What is wrong:** style sheet §10 rule 2 (one term, same words). The reader has to work out
     that C02's how-common question is C03's descriptive question. Point 5 of the audit brief asks
     for exactly this check.
   - **Smallest fix:** the definition's descriptive sentence begins "A descriptive, or describe,
     question — the how-common question of the last section — asks ...". Give relate and cause
     their short names in the same way.
   - **Tag:** style. **Recurring** (synonym drift across sections; SELFCHECK 7).

7. **`simplified_explanation`: "It cannot tell you which came first" against the definition's "usually cannot"**
   - **Claim:** the definition says a cross-sectional study "usually cannot show which of the two
     came first". The simplified explanation drops the "usually".
   - **What the source says:** Blackstone 5.2's own survey example: gender is recorded at the same
     time as gadget use, yet gender plainly came first. An exposure fixed before the outcome (sex,
     birth order) leaves no doubt about order even in a one-day survey.
   - **Smallest fix:** "It usually cannot tell you which came first. The exception is an exposure
     fixed long before, such as sex."
   - **Tag:** style.

8. **Exercise 3 prompt: the press release is not marked as made up**

   - **What is wrong:** every other illustrative case in C01 to C03 is marked made up. This one
     reads as a real release.
   - **Smallest fix:** "A journalist has three minutes. Their press release, made up for this
     exercise, says ..."
   - **Tag:** gap. **Recurring** (made-up material not said to be made up, SELFCHECK 3).
