# S58-R1-C23 · One finding, from the fact sheet to a paragraph a colleague can restate

**Definition.** In this book, a finding's journey is the order of work that takes one result from its source
to a results paragraph a reader can restate after one reading. Each step uses what the step
before produced.

1. The question, and the data read from the source with every limit the source states.
2. The one message: the claim in one or two sentences, false if the data had come out
   otherwise.
3. The results paragraph, rewritten from an account of what was done into the claim and its
   evidence.
4. The numbers: units, counts where the source gives them, differences in percentage
   points, and no more digits than the data hold.
5. A readability score taken as a trip-wire, and a cut.
6. One home for the numbers, chosen by what the reader has to do with them. A table is made
   to stand alone. A figure is made by the procedure of the last section, and its axis is
   checked with the Lie Factor. The paper keeps one of the two, never both.
7. A numbered reference to the source, formatted from the rules for that kind of source.
8. A one-pass test with a reader who does not know the work, and a revision.
9. A statement of what the finding does not mean.

**In plain terms.** Each earlier section taught one tool. Real work uses them one after another, on one result.
This section takes one real result and carries it all the way. The result is the share of
Indian adults with overweight or obesity in two national surveys.

You start with a question and the two rows of a table. You write the one sentence the result
says. You turn a diary-like paragraph into an argument, fix its numbers, score it and cut it.
You build a table and a figure, and keep only one. You cite the source, on the pattern NLM
gives for a report read on the Internet.
You give it to a reader and fix what they misread. Last, you write down what the result does
not mean.

**Must know points for you.**

- Work in this order: the message, then the paragraph, then the table or figure, then the reference, then the reader. Each step is checked against the one before. A figure drawn before the message chooses the claim for you, and a paragraph polished before the outline may be cut.
- Give every number one home. The ICMJE Recommendations ask you not to duplicate data in graphs and tables, and to summarise in the text only the most important observations. Decide by what the reader has to do with the numbers, then cut the other homes.
- The diary-style draft scored 61.6, above Flesch's Plain English minimum of 60, and was the worst of three drafts. A passing score never tells you a paragraph works. Read it for its claim before you look at the number.
- Check the axis of every bar chart a program draws for you. Starting at 18, the program's chart of these rows drew the men's rise {{n:nfhs_men_lf_axis18}} times its true size. Work out a ÷ (a − s) with the smaller bar; if it is above {{n:tufte_lf_threshold}}, start the bars at zero.
- Use one number of decimal places for the same figures in the text and the table. When Cole's rules pull apart, as they do here between four totals and eight values, choose and keep to it. Two copies of one number with different digits look like two different numbers.
- When the source gives no counts, say so in a footnote. Never make one by multiplying a percentage by the survey's total sample. NFHS-5's {{n:nfhs5_women_interviewed}} women are those it gathered information from, not the women weighed for this row.
- Format each kind of source from its own chapter of Citing Medicine. A fact sheet read online is not a journal article: it follows the chapter on titles on the Internet. Where you have only an example to work from, say the entry is modelled on it. Leave a marked gap for any element you have not found. A reference made up to look finished is worse than one marked unfinished.
- A reader's restatement that adds something, such as "obesity" for "overweight or obesity", or a comparison you did not make, is what readers will repeat. Find the words in your paragraph that invited it and change them, then test again with a new reader.
- This finding covers adults aged 15 to 49, counts overweight and obese together, and shows a rise between two surveys, not its cause. It describes 2019-21, not now. Say each of these in the discussion, before a reader or a journalist widens it for you.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory, list the steps of a finding's journey in order, from the question to what the
finding does not mean. For each, name one check you make before moving on.

**Exercise 2** (critique). A newspaper headline reads: "Obesity in Indian women up 17%, says national survey". Decide
what was computed to get 17, compute it, and say what the headline gets wrong.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A journalist rings: "What does the new national survey say about obesity in India?" You have
three minutes. Give her one sentence she can quote, and say how sure you are.

**Exercise 4** (design). Take one finding from the section you have been building for this book. Run it through the
nine steps of the journey, on one sheet of paper.

Write down the limits of the data and the one
message. Show the paragraph before and after, with its numbers checked, its score and its cut.
Say which home the numbers get, and why. Write the reference. Compare a reader's sentence with
yours. End with what the finding does not mean.
