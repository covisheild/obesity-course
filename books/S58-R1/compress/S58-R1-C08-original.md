# S58-R1-C08 · Words: the everyday word, one term for one thing, abbreviations that cost the reader

**Definition.** Four practices govern the choice of words in a scientific paper.

The everyday word. Of two words that carry the same meaning, the familiar, short and concrete
one is used. A technical term is used where no everyday word carries its meaning, and it is
defined at first use.

One term for one thing. Once a concept has a name, the same name is used for it throughout. A
different word makes the reader wonder whether a different thing is meant.

Abbreviations. An abbreviation is a shortened form of a word or phrase. The International
Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) asks for standard abbreviations only, and none in
the title. At first mention it asks for the full term followed by the abbreviation in
brackets, except for a standard unit of measurement. The US Federal Plain Language Guidelines advise no more than
three abbreviations in a document, and preferably two.

Statistical words. "Random", "normal", "significant", "correlation" and "sample" have exact
meanings in statistics. ICMJE asks authors not to use them in their everyday senses.

How fast abbreviations are spreading has been measured. Barnett and Doubleday (2020) counted
acronyms, which they defined as words in which half or more of the characters are capital
letters, in 24,873,372 titles and 18,249,091 abstracts in PubMed published from 1950 to 2019.
In titles, acronyms per 100 words rose from 0.7 in 1950 to 2.4 in 2019. In abstracts, they
rose from 0.4 in 1956 to 4.1 in 2019. Of 1,112,345 different acronyms, 30% were used only once
and 49% two to ten times. Just over 2,000 (0.2%) were used more than 10,000 times.

The study counted acronyms in titles and abstracts. It did not test whether readers understood
less. What would overturn the trend: a count of later years, or of full texts, finding that
the rise has stopped or reversed. What would overturn the case against acronyms: reading tests
finding that readers understand text dense with acronyms as well as the same text spelled out.

**In plain terms.** Three habits with words do most of the work.

Use the word a reader would say out loud. "Many", not "a number of". "Now", not "at this point
in time".

Once you have named a thing, keep the name. If the paragraph says "overweight or obesity" in one
sentence and "excess weight" in the next, the reader wonders whether they are two groups.

Spell out a short form the first time you use it. Then ask whether it earns its place. "HR"
for "heart rate" saves two letters of ink and nothing a reader would notice.

Some words are traps because they have two meanings. In statistics, "significant", "random",
"normal" and "correlation" each mean something exact. Used in their everyday sense, they make a
claim you did not mean to make.

Is the short-form habit getting worse? Someone counted. In medical abstracts, acronyms went from
0.4 in every 100 words in 1956 to 4.1 in 2019. Most acronyms were hardly ever used again. The
count shows that they are spreading. On its own, it does not show how much they slow a reader
down.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Using a different word each time makes writing less repetitive, and so better" is the error. A reader who meets a new word assumes a new thing. Name a concept once and keep the name, even when the paragraph starts to sound repetitive.
- Do not write "obesity" for the NFHS-5 row that reports "overweight or obese". The fact sheet's adult rows give the two together, at a body mass index of {{n:nfhs5_ow_ob_bmi_cutoff}} kg/m^2 or more, and none gives obesity alone. "Obesity rose to {{n:nfhs5_women_ow_ob_pct}} per cent" makes a claim the survey did not report, and a journalist will repeat it.
- In PubMed abstracts, acronyms rose from 0.4 per 100 words in 1956 to 4.1 in 2019, about ten times as dense (Barnett and Doubleday, 2020).
- Put three questions to every abbreviation in your draft. Is it standard for the reader of this paper? Does it save the reader anything but ink? Will it appear often? Keep no more than three, spell each out at first mention, and use none in the title. A long name used often can become a short plain one, such as "the centre".
- "Significant", "random", "normal" and "correlation" make a statistical claim when a reviewer reads them. If you ran no test, used no chance process, or computed no correlation, use other words, or state the number itself.
- The acronym count measured use, not understanding, and only in English-language titles and abstracts. It cannot tell you how much an acronym slows your own reader. When it matters, as in a report that ASHAs or patients will read, give the text to one such reader and watch where they stop.
- An abbreviation every Indian community medicine reader knows, such as ASHA, may be unknown to a journal's reviewer abroad. Spell it out at first mention in anything written for a journal, even when your department never does.
