# S58-R1-C12 · Cutting: deleting the words, sentences and paragraphs that do no work

**Definition.** Cutting is deleting words, sentences or paragraphs that do no work for the reader, so that
what remains says the same thing in fewer words. A unit does no work when deleting it loses
nothing the reader needs. It may restate what is already said, serve no claim of the piece, or
use several words where fewer say the same.

Cutting runs from the largest unit to the smallest: paragraphs, then sentences, then words.
The test for a paragraph or a sentence is whether it serves the piece's one message, from
`S58-R1-C03`. The test for a word is whether the sentence means the same without it.

Cutting is not compressing. Compressing keeps everything and packs it into fewer, longer
sentences. Cutting removes what does no work and leaves each remaining sentence carrying one
idea, as before or shorter.

**In plain terms.** Cutting is deleting what the reader does not need. Start big. First ask of each paragraph
whether it serves your one message. Then ask it of each sentence. Only then go through the words.

Big cuts first, because polishing a sentence you later delete is work thrown away. Most of the
words you lose will go in whole sentences and paragraphs, not in single words.

Do not cut by squeezing two sentences into one. That saves a few words and makes a sentence the
reader has to read twice.

**Must know points for you.**

- Cut in order: paragraphs first, then sentences, then words. Do not polish a sentence until you know it will stay.
- Test each paragraph against your one message with a reverse outline. Where a margin note serves no part of the message, delete that paragraph. Or move it to the section whose message it does serve.
- "Fewer words is better, so join short sentences into one" is the error. That is compressing. It trades words for difficulty: each sentence now carries several ideas, and its score on `S58-R1-C11`'s formula drops. Cut whole units instead, and keep one idea a sentence.
- Cutting has no test for truth. A qualifier such as an age range, a year, a place or "or obese" looks like padding. Before you delete any word, ask whether the claim gets wider without it. If it does, the word stays.
- Before you cut, open a second file and paste every deleted passage into it. This is this book's advice, not a finding. Deleting is easier when nothing is lost for good, and you will rarely open the file again.
- Delete the opener that delays the subject: "It was observed that", "It is important to note that", "In the present study". Sometimes such a phrase was holding a fact, as "at this point in time" held the survey round. Then put the fact back in its plain form.
- When you correct a trainee's thesis chapter, mark the paragraphs and sentences to delete before you touch a single word. Corrections to words in a passage that should go teach the wrong lesson and waste both your time.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory: in what order do you cut, and what question does each unit have to fail before
you delete it? Then say how cutting differs from compressing.

**Exercise 2** (critique). Cut this methods paragraph from a district survey by deleting words only. It is invented. Do
not rewrite or join sentences. Say what you cut and why, and count the words before and after.

> The present study was conducted in the Department of Community Medicine of a tertiary care
> centre in central India. A cross-sectional study design was used for the purpose of this
> study. The study was carried out over a period of six months, from January to June 2025.
> The study participants were adults aged 18 years and above who were residing in the field
> practice area. Each and every participant was weighed with a digital weighing scale, and the
> weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg.

**Exercise 3** (design). Take the manuscript or thesis section you chose for this book's build. Write its one message
