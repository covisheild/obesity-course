# S58-R1-C19 · Colour: for meaning only, readable by everyone, and in grey

**Definition.** In a data figure, colour does one of three jobs. It separates groups that have no order, using
a qualitative scale: a small set of colours that look clearly different from each other and
equally strong. It stands for a value, using a sequential scale, which runs from light to dark.
For a value that can lie either side of a meaningful middle, such as zero, it uses a diverging
scale: two sequential scales joined at a light middle colour placed at that value. Or it picks
out one element against others drawn in grey. Colour that does none of these jobs is ornament.

A colour scale shows values fairly when it is perceptually uniform: the same step in the data
looks like the same step in colour anywhere along the scale. Its lightness should also change
in one direction only, so that the order of the values survives when the colour is removed. The
rainbow scale fails both. Crameri, Shephard and Heron (2020) report that its distortion can
exceed seven per cent of the range of the data shown.

Colour-vision deficiency is a reduced ability to tell certain colours apart, most often red
from green. The general estimate cited worldwide is that it affects 8 per cent of men and 0.5
per cent of women. In India, one school screening of 74,986 boys aged 11 to 17 in Kanchipuram
district, Tamil Nadu, found it confirmed in 2.76 per cent. That is a figure for boys in one
district, not a rate for India.

A greyscale copy of a figure keeps only the lightness of each colour, so two colours of the
same lightness print as the same grey. A colour-vision-deficiency simulator is a program that
redraws an image as a reader with a given deficiency would see it.

What would overturn this: perception experiments in which readers judge values on a rainbow
scale as accurately as on a perceptually uniform one. For the Indian figure, a survey of a
random sample of Indian adults, women and men, would replace one district's boys as the number
to quote.

**In plain terms.** Colour in a chart should have a job. It can tell groups apart, stand for a number, or point at
one thing. If it does none of these, take it out and draw in black or grey.

Some of your readers cannot tell red from green. Some copies of what you write will be printed
or photocopied in black and white. A grey copy keeps only how light or dark each colour is. So two
colours of the same lightness turn into the same grey, and the reader loses what they meant.

So test every colour figure twice before it leaves you. Look at it in grey. Then run it through a
program that shows it as a reader with colour-vision deficiency sees it. If the meaning survives
both, the colour is doing its job.

**Must know points for you.**

- "More colours make a figure clearer" is the error. Colour with no job gives the reader a puzzle to solve. Before you colour anything, ask the question Rougier and colleagues print: "Is there any reason this plot is blue and not black?" If you cannot answer, draw it in black.
- Name the job before you choose a colour: separating groups with no order, standing for a value, or picking out one element against grey. Each job takes its own kind of scale. A rainbow fits none of them.
- Red against green is the tempting pair: good and bad, urban and rural, before and after. At similar lightness it fails twice: in a grey copy and for readers with red-green colour-vision deficiency, the commonest kind. Change one of the pair, or make them differ in lightness.
- The general estimate cited worldwide is 8 per cent of men and 0.5 per cent of women with colour-vision deficiency. It is an estimate Crameri and colleagues cite, not one they measured.
- The one Indian measurement here found 2.76 per cent of 74,986 school boys in Kanchipuram district, Tamil Nadu. It is boys in one district, with block figures from 1.12 to 3.40 per cent. Quote it with those words attached, never as "the Indian rate".
- Test every colour figure twice before it leaves you: in grey, by printing or photocopying it in black and white, and through a colour-vision-deficiency simulator. Write "urban" on the urban bar so that nothing depends on colour at all.
- A palette sold as colour-blind safe stops being safe on thin lines and small points. Wilke shows colours are harder to tell apart there, and harder still under colour-vision deficiency. So test the finished figure at its printed size, not the palette.
- When you review a colleague's map or heat map, run Crameri and colleagues' checks aloud. Is there a colour bar? Does the scale get steadily lighter or darker? Are red and green at similar lightness? Is it a rainbow? A no, yes, yes or yes is a request for a redraw, not a matter of taste.
