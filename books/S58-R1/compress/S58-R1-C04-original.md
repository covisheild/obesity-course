# S58-R1-C04 · The argument across sections: from the gap to the answer and its limits

**Definition.** In a paper that reports original research, each of the four main sections does one job in
the paper's argument.

The introduction moves from what is known to what is not yet known, the gap. It ends on the
study's specific purpose: the question it asks, its research objective, or the hypothesis it
tests (a statement the study is designed to support or refute).

The methods say how and why the study was done, in enough detail for a reader to judge it and,
with the data, to reproduce the results. They hold only what was known when the plan or
protocol was written. Everything learnt during the study belongs in the results.

The results are a sequence of claims, in the order the argument needs them, each supported by
its data, with the main findings first.

The discussion opens by stating the main findings briefly, as the answer to the introduction's
question. It then explores possible explanations and places the findings among the other
evidence. It states the study's limitations: the ways its design or data could make the answer
wrong, or narrower than it looks. Last, it says what the findings mean for research, practice
or policy, claiming no more than the data support.

In this book, an outline is the paper's argument written before the prose: one sentence for
each planned paragraph, each sentence the claim that paragraph will make.

**In plain terms.** Think of the paper as one argument handed along four sections. The introduction says what is
known, then what is not known, and ends on your question. The methods say what you did and why,
so that a reader can judge it and could repeat it. The results answer the question in steps, one
claim at a time, each with its numbers. The discussion gives the answer in its first lines, then
what might explain it, what could make it wrong, and what it means.

Plan this before you write a paragraph. Write one sentence for each paragraph you intend, and make
each sentence the point that paragraph will make. Then read those sentences alone, top to bottom.
If they do not carry the argument, polishing the paragraphs will not make them carry it.

**Must know points for you.**

- Write the outline before the prose: one sentence for each planned paragraph, each sentence the claim that paragraph makes. Then read the sentences alone. If they do not take a reader from your question to your answer, fix them there. Fixing a sentence takes a minute. Fixing a finished section means throwing away paragraphs you worked hard on.
- An introduction that reviews everything known about obesity buries the gap. Keep only the references that lead to your question; ICMJE asks for directly pertinent references only. A reader should be able to underline the sentence that names the gap, and the one that states your question.
- The two sources this book follows disagree on how an introduction ends. Mensh and Kording's last paragraph summarises the results. ICMJE says to state the study's purpose and not to include data or conclusions from the work being reported. For a medical journal that follows ICMJE, end on the question or research objective. Then read the journal's instructions, which decide.
- The methods hold what you knew when you wrote the protocol. How many people declined, who was excluded once the data came in, and anything you changed along the way are results. Keep them there. A reviewer needs to see what you planned separately from what happened.
- Give the results subheadings that state findings, not topics: "Overweight or obesity was commoner in urban than in rural women", not "Urban-rural comparison". Open each results paragraph with the question it answers and end it with the answer. A reader who doubts one conclusion can then find the one paragraph to check.
- "The discussion begins with background and other people's studies" is the error. Begin with your main finding, briefly, as the answer to your question. Explanations, comparison with other evidence, limits and meaning follow, in that order.
- Claim no more than your data carry. A prevalence survey cannot say that a programme will save money unless the paper contains cost data and their analysis. A new idea the data suggest is written as a hypothesis, and labelled as one. When you sit on a committee, read a paper's last paragraph against its results before you quote it.
- "Stating limitations weakens the paper" is the error. A limitation you leave out is still there. A reviewer who finds it will ask about it, and Mensh and Kording's sign of a weak discussion is a reader left holding unanswered criticisms. Name each limit, say which way it could push the answer, and say what would settle it.
- The structure shows the logic; it cannot add evidence. If an outline sentence has no data behind it, either cut the claim or get the data. Better paragraphs will not fill that gap, and a clear paper makes a missing step easier for a reviewer to see.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). Without looking back: give the one job of each of the four main sections in a sentence each.
Then say in which section the number of people who declined to take part belongs, and why.

**Exercise 2** (critique). Here is the first paragraph of the discussion in an invented paper on a survey of
overweight among adults in one rural block.

> Obesity is a major public health problem in India and the world. Several studies from
> different states have reported different levels of overweight. Our study was conducted in a
> rural block of Chhattisgarh. Table 2 shows the distribution of body mass index by age group.
> Further research is needed.

Say what is wrong with it. Then rewrite it so that it does the first paragraph's job. Where
you need the study's own results, write a labelled blank rather than a number.

**Exercise 3** (design). Take the manuscript or thesis section you chose to rewrite for this book. Write its outline:
one sentence for each planned paragraph, each sentence the claim the paragraph will make.
Then read the sentences alone and answer three questions. Where is the gap? Which sentence
answers the question? Which claims have no data or reference behind them yet?

**Exercise 4** (teaching). A first-year resident shows you the introduction to her draft paper. It runs to four pages and
reviews obesity from its definition to its complications, worldwide and in India. Ten minutes,
with a whiteboard: explain what an introduction is for, and how she should cut hers.
