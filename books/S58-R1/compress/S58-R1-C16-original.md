# S58-R1-C16 · Show the data: when a bar of means hides what the points would show

**Definition.** A measured quantity, such as minutes of activity or hours of sleep, can take any value in a
range; Weissgerber and colleagues call such values continuous data. A bar chart of a measured
quantity draws each group as one bar, the height of its mean. It usually adds an error bar for
the standard deviation, or for the standard error of the mean (`B0-R0-C29`). It reduces every
group to two numbers.

Many different sets of values give the same bar and the same error bar. The values may be
spread evenly, or bunched with one far from the rest, or split into two clusters. There may be
only three of them. Each can give the same mean and much the same standard deviation. The summary cannot tell
them apart, and the reader cannot either.

Showing the data means drawing every value. A strip chart puts each value as a dot along the
value axis, one column of dots per group; Weissgerber and colleagues call it a univariate
scatterplot. Where dots would sit on top of each other, they are spread a little sideways,
which is called jittering. With more values, a box plot shows the median, a box around the
middle half of the values, and whiskers beyond it, with far values drawn as single dots. A box
plot is still a summary, and it hides two clusters.

How common the bar chart is was measured in one systematic review. It covered all 703 original
research papers in the top quarter of physiology journals over three months of 2014. Of these,
85.6% had at least one bar chart. 13.4% had at least one univariate scatterplot, 5.3% a box plot and 8.0% a
histogram. Most bar charts showed the mean and the standard error. In the median paper, the
smallest group in any figure had four values.

What would overturn this for your field: a count of recent papers in the journals you read,
showing that most figures of measured quantities already draw the values.

**In plain terms.** A bar chart of a measured quantity shows one number per group: the average. It hides
everything else. Eight people can reach an average of 30 minutes in very different ways. All
may sit near 30. Seven may sit near 25 with one far above. Or four may sit near 15 and four near
45. The bar is the same
height each time.

So when your groups are small, draw every value as a dot. The reader sees how many people there
were, how they spread, and whether one person is pulling the average. With larger groups, a
box plot or a histogram summarises the spread, but even these can hide two clusters.

**Must know points for you.**

- "The bar chart summarises the group" is the error. Very different sets of values give the same mean and the same error bar: an even spread, one far value, two clusters. A reader shown only the bar cannot tell which, and neither can you.
- When each group has only a handful of values, draw every value as a dot, with a short line at the median or mean. Say in the caption which the line is, and how many values each group has.
- A box plot is a summary too. It can draw two clusters as one wide box with a median where nobody is. With small groups, show the dots; with large ones, check a box plot against a histogram or violin before you trust it.
- When the same people are measured twice, never draw the two occasions as two bars. Join each person's two values with a line, so the reader sees who went up, who went down and who stayed the same.
- A systematic review covered 703 papers in leading physiology journals from early 2014. Of these, 85.6% had at least one bar chart, and only 13.4% had a chart showing every value. Expect to meet bar charts of small groups in most papers you review.
- When you review a colleague's draft with bars of means for small groups, ask for the values as dots. If they object that bars look cleaner, ask them to read their own claim off the dots and see whether it survives.
- That review counted physiology journals over three months of 2014. It says nothing directly about community medicine journals in India. Leaf through a recent issue of the journal you will submit to and count for yourself before you assume its habits.
- Showing every value shows the shape and the count. It does not tell you whether a gap between groups is real or large enough to matter. And it is for measured quantities. A share of people with a condition is one number per group, and a bar or dot is right for it.

**Exercise 2** (interpretation). A made-up student survey compares hours of sleep a night in two hostels, six students each. The
draft shows two bars of equal height and says, "Sleep did not differ between hostels."

```table
| hostel | each student's hours of sleep a night |
| hostel A | 6.5, 7.0, 7.0, 7.5, 7.5, 8.0 |
| hostel B | 5.0, 5.5, 5.5, 6.0, 6.0, 15.5 |
```

Work out each hostel's mean and median. Sketch the values as a strip chart. Say what the bars
hid and what you would do before writing the sentence again.
