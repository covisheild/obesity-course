# S58-R1-C21 · Making a table that stands alone

**Definition.** A table in a paper sets values in rows and columns, as Book 0 described. A table stands alone
when a reader can understand it without going back to the text. It is numbered in the order it
is first cited, and the text cites it. Its title is short but self-explanatory, and sits above
the table: what was measured, in whom, where and when.

Each column has a short heading with
the unit. Explanations, exclusions and nonstandard abbreviations go in footnotes below the
table, marked with letters or symbols, not in the headings. A percentage has its count beside
it, and any measure of variation is named: standard deviation, standard error or confidence
interval. A source line says where data taken from elsewhere came from.

A table is the right place for a result when the reader needs exact values, or will look up
particular rows. A pattern among many values is better shown in a figure. The same numbers are
not given in both a table and a figure, and the text does not repeat the table. It states the
most important observation and cites the table.

The layout follows a few rules. There are no vertical lines and no lines between data rows; a
line under the column headings, and lines above and below the whole table, are enough. Text
columns are aligned left. Number columns are aligned right and carry the same number of
decimal places all the way down. Each heading is aligned like the column under it.

Data taken from another published or unpublished source is acknowledged in full, and the
owner's permission is obtained before it is used.

**In plain terms.** A table is for the reader who wants exact numbers, or wants to find one row. It has to make
sense on its own, because a reader may look at the tables before reading the text.

So the title above it says what was measured, in whom, where and when. Each column heading is
short and gives the unit. Anything longer goes in a footnote under the table. Every percentage
has its count beside it.

Keep it plain. No grid of lines, no lines between rows. Numbers line up on the right and keep the
same number of decimal places. And never print the same numbers in a table and in a figure too.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Put the numbers in a table and in a figure, to be safe" is the error. ICMJE says do not duplicate data in graphs and tables, and do not repeat all of a table in the text. Choose one home for each result. The text then states the most important observation and cites it.
- Write the title above the table, short and self-explanatory: what was measured, in whom, where and when. Test it by covering the text. If a reader cannot say whom the rows describe, the title is not finished.
- Keep headings short, with the unit in brackets. Put exclusions, definitions, cut-offs and every nonstandard abbreviation in footnotes under the table, marked with letters or the symbols your journal asks for.
- Give every percentage its count, and name every measure of variation. A column of "mean ± value" is unfinished until its heading or a footnote names the value. It may be a standard deviation, a standard error or a confidence interval.
- When your source gives no counts, as the NFHS fact sheet does not, say so in a footnote. Never work a count back from a percentage and a total you guessed, and never leave the gap silent.
- Delete the word processor's default grid. No vertical lines, no lines between rows; a line under the headings and one above and below the table. Align numbers right with the same decimal places, and text left. Wilke found most of his word processor's ready-made table styles break these rules.
- "Many rows, so a table" is a half-truth. A table is for exact values and for a reader who looks up rows. For a pattern among many values, ICMJE offers a graph instead. Decide by what the reader has to do.
- Keep the same decimal places down a column of like values, such as the per cents in one table: that is Wilke's rule. Cole adds a warning. In his words, "tables ought not to be restricted to columns of numbers with fixed decimal places". A column of ratios or P values may need more digits in some rows than in others, by the rules of `S58-R1-C09`. Give each value the digits it needs, and line the column up on the decimal point.
- Acknowledge every table's outside data in a source line, every time. Before you use unpublished data, such as a colleague's register or another department's survey, get the owner's permission. ICMJE asks for both.

**Exercise 2** (critique). A resident's thesis draft has the table below, made from the real NFHS-5 India Fact Sheet
numbers for anaemia; the layout is made up for this exercise. Haemoglobin, written Hb, is the
oxygen-carrying protein of the blood, measured in grams per decilitre (g/dl). The table has a
border round every cell, and every number is centred.

The table has no title. Its first column heading reads "Prevalence of anaemia among non
pregnant women (Hb<12) and men (Hb<13) as per NFHS 5 survey conducted in 2019-21 in India
(capillary blood)". Its rows are:

```table
| | Urban | Rural | Total |
| Women | 54.1% | 58.7 | 57.2 |
| Men | 20.4% | 27.4 | 25 |
```

Under the table, the draft reads out all six numbers in a sentence: "Anaemia was 54.1% in
urban women, 58.7% in rural women ..." and so on. A bar chart of the same six numbers follows.
List what you would change.
