# A4 · Percentages, and the percentage of a percentage

**Definition.** A percentage is a fraction whose denominator is one hundred. Per cent means per hundred, so 24
per cent is 24/100, which is 0.24.

A percentage change is the change divided by the value it started from, multiplied by one
hundred.

A percentage point is the arithmetic difference between two percentages. A per cent change is
that difference divided by the first percentage.

A percentage of a percentage is the product of the two fractions: 40 per cent of 30 per cent is
0.40 times 0.30, which is 0.12, or 12 per cent of the original whole.

**In plain terms.** Per cent means per hundred. Twenty-four per cent is twenty-four out of a hundred, which is 0.24.

The hard half is what the hundred was.

A percentage change is worked out against the number you started from. Because the starting
number sits underneath, going up and coming back down are not the same percentage.

If something goes from 2 per cent to 3 per cent, it has gone up by one percentage point. It has
also gone up by 50 per cent, because one is half of two.

**Illustration.** Go back to the two rows you read in the National Food Security Act, 2013. A lower primary meal
is set at 450 calories. An upper primary meal is set at 700.

How much more is the upper primary standard?

Work out both.

The plain difference first.

```working
    700 minus 450 = 250 calories more
```

Now as a percentage. The rule is: the change, divided by what you started from, times a hundred.

```working
    250 divided by 450 = 0.5555...
    0.5555... times 100 = 55.6 per cent      (rounded to one place, as the last section had it)
```

Now turn it round, because this is where people slip. Start at 700 and come down to 450.

```working
    250 divided by 700 = 0.3571...
    0.3571... times 100 = 35.7 per cent
```

The lower primary meal is about 36 per cent less. Not 56. The number sitting underneath changed,
so the percentage changed with it.

Ask which end it was measured from, every single time.

Here is a sentence of the kind that reaches a front page.

> The share of children affected rose by fifty per cent in a decade.

Suppose the real figures behind it are two in a hundred rising to three in a hundred.

```working
    3 minus 2 = 1 percentage point
    1 divided by 2 = 0.5, so 50 per cent
```

Both are true of the same change. The writer picked the fifty.

Percentage points subtract. Per cent divides.

Ask for the count as well as the percentage, always.

Suppose 30 per cent of schoolchildren in a district eat a packaged snack on a school day. Of
those who do, suppose 40 per cent buy it within a hundred metres of the school gate. Multiply
the fractions.

```working
    0.40 times 0.30 = 0.12, so 12 per cent of all schoolchildren
```

Those two percentages are made up, to show the move. A percentage of a percentage is smaller
than either.

**Where this picture breaks.** Percentages hide the size of the thing they are a percentage of, and they hide it by design.

A percentage change and a percentage point look alike on the page and are different quantities.
When a source says "per cent" you often cannot tell which it meant, and you cannot repair it by
reading harder. Go back to the two original numbers, or say in your own writing that you could
not.

**Must know points for you.**

- A percentage point and a per cent are different quantities. Whichever is quoted at you, ask for the other.
- For any percentage in front of you, ask two questions before you use it: per cent of what, and how many people is that.
- A rise and the fall that undoes it are different percentages, because the starting value sits underneath. Say which end you measured from whenever you quote a change.
- A percentage of a percentage multiplies, so it is always smaller than either.
- In a committee a relative change will be offered when it is the larger number and an absolute one when it is the smaller. Bring both to the meeting yourself.
- When a journalist gives you a percentage on the phone, do not react to it. Ask what it is a percentage of and what the two underlying counts were.

**Exercise 2** (teaching). Explain to a journalist, in three minutes and without jargon. The claim is that obesity in this
group rose by fifty per cent. Say why that may be a smaller story than it sounds, and why it may
also be a larger one. Give them one sentence they can print.

**1.** Write each of these as a decimal, and then as a fraction over one hundred.

```working
    7 per cent      40 per cent      125 per cent      0.5 per cent
```

**2.** Work out 35 per cent of 840. Then work out 840 as a percentage of 1,200.

**3.** A figure goes from 80 to 92. Express the change as a percentage. Then express the step from 92
back to 80 as a percentage.

**4.** Work out 40 per cent of 30 per cent. Then work out 5 per cent of 60 per cent. Give each answer
as a percentage of the whole you started with.

**5.** Section 3(2) of the National Food Security Act, 2013 says entitlements shall extend up to 75
per cent of the rural population. For the urban population the figure is up to 50 per cent. A
district has 18 lakh rural and 6 lakh urban residents. Work out the ceiling on the number
covered in each.

**6.** In a survey, 45 per cent of respondents buy a packaged snack in a week. Of those who do, 20 per
cent buy it at a shop next to a school. What share of all respondents is that? Then say how
many people that is, out of 2,400 respondents.

**7.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a figure rises from 4 per cent to 6 per cent
    6 minus 4 = 2
    so the rise is 2 per cent
```

**8.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    in a survey, 30 per cent of respondents buy a packaged snack on a school day
    of those who do, 40 per cent buy it at a shop by the school gate
    30 plus 40 = 70
    so 70 per cent of all respondents buy a snack at a shop by the school gate
```

**9.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a budget of 100 is cut by 20 per cent
    the following year it is raised by 20 per cent
    20 minus 20 = 0, so the budget is back to 100
```
