# S58-R1-C09 · Numbers in a sentence: the unit, the count behind the percentage, and no more digits than the data hold

**Definition.** A number in a sentence is reported in full when the reader can check it. A measurement
carries its unit. A percentage carries the two counts it was worked out from: the numerator,
how many had the thing, and the denominator, how many were counted.

A difference between two percentages is stated in percentage points. A relative change
divides that difference by the starting percentage, and is stated in per cent.

How finely a number is stated is shown by its significant figures. Cole calls them
significant digits. A number shows false precision when it carries more digits than the data
can support. Every digit is kept through the calculations, and the number is rounded once,
when it is reported.

Cole's rules for how many digits to report, offered as recommendations, not requirements:

```table
| statistic | how many digits to report |
| mean | enough decimal places to give the standard deviation (SD) two significant figures, or the standard error (SE) one |
| percentage | whole numbers, or one decimal place for values under 10% |
| risk ratio or odds ratio | two significant figures if the first digit that is not zero is 4 or more, otherwise three: the rule of four |
| P value | one significant figure, never 0.000 |
```

The SAMPL guidelines (Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature) are by
Lang and Altman. They ask for P values as equalities, to one or two decimal places. The
smallest that need be reported is P < 0.001. They ask for a mean and its SD in the form
mean (SD).

When n people are counted, one person moves a percentage by 100 ÷ n percentage points.

**In plain terms.** The last two sections worked on the sentence and its words. This one works on the numbers
inside it. A reader cannot check a number that has lost its unit or its count. Nor can they
trust one that carries more digits than the study could measure.

So ask three questions of every number you write. In what unit? Out of how many? How finely do
the data let you say it?

**Must know points for you.**

- More decimal places look more careful, and that is the error. Each digit claims a fineness of measurement, and a digit the data cannot support is a small false statement. Keep every digit while you calculate. Round once, when you write the sentence, to the digits Cole's rules give.
- Put the count before the percentage: "15 of 40 (38 per cent)". The reader can check the arithmetic and see at once how far one person moves it. ICMJE and SAMPL both ask for the counts behind every percentage, so a reviewer will ask for them if you leave them out.
- A change between two percentages has two sizes. For women in NFHS it is {{n:nfhs5_women_ow_ob_change_pp}} percentage points, or about {{n:nfhs_women_ow_ob_rel_rise_pct}} per cent of the starting figure. Say the points first. If you give the per cent change as well, label it, so nobody reads it as points.
- When fewer than 100 people are counted, one person is worth more than a percentage point. In a small study the count is the result. Give it every time, and let no decimal place suggest a finer measurement than the people allow.
- Cole and SAMPL give different rules for P values. Above 0.01 they agree. Below it, two decimal places cannot show 0.0026, and one significant figure gives 0.003. Neither allows 0.000. Use the rule your journal names, or say which you used, and keep to one rule through the paper.
- Rounding rules are for the page, not for the working. A figure rounded early and then used in a later sum carries its rounding into the answer. So never compute a new result from the rounded figures in a published table when the full ones are in your own data.
- These rules decide how many digits you print. They cannot tell you whether the number is right. A mean printed to the correct digits from a sample that was not random can still be far from the district's mean. Check the sample first.
- The NFHS fact sheet prints percentages with no counts. Its rows for women leave out pregnant women and women who gave birth in the two months before. Quote the percentage with the survey, its years, the age range and the cut-off. Never make a count by multiplying the survey's {{n:nfhs5_women_interviewed}} women by the percentage.

**11.** Cole cites a review that gives a hazard ratio of 0.03, rounded to two decimal places. A
hazard ratio compares how fast an event happens in two groups; here the event was updating
a reference work's citations. Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
hazard ratio = 0.03
turned the other way round: 1 divided by 0.03 = 33.33
so the other reference work updates its citations 33.33 times as fast
```
