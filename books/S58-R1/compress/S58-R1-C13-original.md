# S58-R1-C13 · Showing a draft to a reader: the one-pass test

**Definition.** The one-pass test checks whether a draft carries its argument to a reader. It has five steps.

1. Before the test, write your argument in one or two sentences, your one message from
   `S58-R1-C03`, and put it away where the reader cannot see it.
2. Give the draft to a reader who does not know the work. They read it once, at their normal
   pace, without going back, and mark each place where they stopped or read a passage again.
3. Without looking at the draft, the reader writes in one or two sentences what it argues.
4. Compare the reader's sentences with yours, part by part: whom or what the claim is about,
   what was found, compared with what, and how far it reaches.
5. Revise the paragraphs that the comparison and the marks point to, and test again with a
   new reader.

The draft passes when the reader's sentences carry every part of your message and add no part
of their own. The wording need not match. A reader can take the test only once on a given
argument, because after one reading and one conversation they know it.

**In plain terms.** You cannot tell whether your draft is clear by reading it yourself. You know what it means, so
you read that meaning into it.

So write your argument in a sentence and hide it. Give the draft to someone who does not know
your work. Ask them to read it once, mark where they got stuck, and then write down what it
argues. Put their sentence next to yours. Where they lost a part, or added one, the draft has a
hole, and their marks show you where to look.

**Illustration.** Start with the test that is not a test. You finish the discussion section of your thesis
chapter on the NFHS figures and give it to your guide. Two days later it comes back: "Looks
good. Go ahead."

It tells you almost nothing. Your guide planned the analysis with you and
knows the argument before reading a word. Mensh and Kording say the writer is "the world's
least qualified person" to judge a draft as a new reader would. The same holds for anyone
who already knows the work.

Now the test. The case is invented; the steps are the ones you will use.

First, write your one message and seal it in an envelope, or in a file the reader will not
see.

> Among women aged 15 to 49 in India, overweight or obesity was commoner in NFHS-5 than in
> NFHS-4, and commoner in urban than in rural areas. These survey data cannot say why.

Next, find a reader outside the work: a resident in Physiology, say, who knows what a survey
is and nothing about yours. Hand over the four pages with a pencil and these instructions.

> Please read this once, at your normal speed, without going back. Put a mark in the margin
> wherever you stop or read something twice. When you finish, turn it face down and write one
> or two sentences: what is this section arguing?

She hands back one mark, beside paragraph 3, and this sentence.

> Obesity is increasing among Indian women because of urbanisation.

Now open the envelope and compare, part by part.

```table
| part | your message | her sentence | verdict |
| about whom | women aged 15 to 49 in India | Indian women | age range lost |
| what was found | commoner in NFHS-5 than in NFHS-4 | increasing | kept |
| compared with what | urban higher than rural | because of urbanisation | a difference turned into a cause |
| how far it reaches | the data cannot say why | nothing | lost, and contradicted |
```

The added cause is the worst failure. Your draft never claims one, and a reader of your paper
could still leave with it and repeat it. Find where it came from. Her mark sits beside
paragraph 3. You open it: the urban and rural figures come straight after two sentences on
diet and work changing in Indian cities. The order invites the reader to join them.

Revise only what the test pointed to. Move the urban and rural figures into the paragraph that
reports the findings. Give the possible explanations a paragraph of their own, opening with
the sentence that these data cannot test them. Put the age range into the first sentence of
the discussion, where the claim is made, and not only in the methods.

Then test again, with a new reader. The resident from Physiology now knows your argument: her
second summary would test her memory, not your draft. A resident in Microbiology reads it
once, makes no mark, and writes this.

> Overweight among women of 15 to 49 went up between the two surveys and is higher in towns.
> The survey can't tell us why.

Every part is there and nothing is added. The words differ from yours, and that does not
matter. The draft passes.

**Where this picture breaks.** One reader is one reader. A resident from another department reads like the colleague the
gate of this book describes. An examiner or a journal reviewer in your field will read more
closely and know more. A pass with one reader does not promise a pass with them.

The test shows that the argument gets across. It does not show that the argument is right.
If the analysis is wrong, a clear draft carries the wrong claim clearly.

**Must know points for you.**

- Never count your guide's or co-author's approval as the test. They know the argument already, and they read it into the draft. Choose a reader outside the work for the one-pass test.
- Write your own one-message sentence and put it away before the reader starts. If you write it after reading theirs, you will find their sentence closer to yours than it is.
- "Did you like it?" and "Any comments?" are the wrong questions. They bring back general praise or general doubt. Mensh and Kording read non-specific feedback as a sign that the reader did not get the story. Ask for the one sentence and the marks.
- Treat a part the reader added as worse than a part the reader lost. An added cause, a wider group, a stronger verdict: readers will repeat these. Find the paragraph whose order or wording invited it, and change that paragraph.
- Use a new reader for each round. A reader who has read the draft once and talked to you about it cannot be new to it again. In return, read your colleagues' drafts the same way when they ask.
- A pass shows that one reader, minutes after reading, could state your argument. It does not show that a reader will remember it a year later, which is Mensh and Kording's own measure. It also does not show that the argument is true or the methods sound. That is the examiners' and reviewers' job, and a clear draft makes their job possible, not unnecessary.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory, list the five steps of the one-pass test. Then say why the reader must not know
your work, and why each round needs a new reader.

**Exercise 2** (build). This book's build: one section of a manuscript or thesis, rewritten so that each paragraph
carries one idea, and shown to a reader. Use the section you have been working on since the
start of the book.

1. Write the section's one message at the top of a separate page, and put the page away.
2. Make a reverse outline. Rewrite until each paragraph has one point, stated in its first
   sentence.
3. Cut it in three passes, paragraphs, sentences, words, keeping the cuts in a second file.
4. Score each paragraph for readability with one program, and reread the longest sentences of
   any paragraph that scores far harder than the rest.
5. Run the one-pass test with a reader outside the work. Fill in the four-part comparison.
6. Revise what the test pointed to, and run it again with a new reader until the draft passes.

Keep the dated versions, the reverse outlines, the readers' sentences and their marks.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A first-year resident hands you her thesis introduction and asks, "Can you read it and tell me
if it's OK?" You have ten minutes and a whiteboard. Explain what you will do instead of reading
it for her, and why.
