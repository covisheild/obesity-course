# S58-R1-C03 · The one message: your argument in one or two sentences, written first

**Definition.** A paper's one message is its central claim, stated in one or two sentences: what the study
found, and why that matters. Mensh and Kording's first rule is to focus a paper on a single
central contribution and to carry it in the title. The contribution may have several parts,
provided one statement holds them together.

The message is written before the draft, and it is a test. If you cannot state it, the paper
is not ready to draft. It passes only if it is a claim, which would be false had the data come
out differently, and if it answers the reader's "so what?".

The title is the message in its shortest form. ICMJE's Recommendations describe the title as a
distilled description of the complete article. The abstract is the argument in small: the
context and the gap, what was done, the main result, and what it means. ICMJE asks the
abstract for the study's context, purpose, basic procedures, main findings and principal
conclusions. Title, abstract and text must agree, and ICMJE notes that abstracts often differ
from the text.

**In plain terms.** Before you write the paper, write one or two sentences that say what you found and why it
matters. That is your one message. Everything else in the paper is there to support it.

Test it two ways. First, would the sentence be false if your data had come out differently? If
not, it says nothing yet. "This study is about obesity" is true whatever you found. Second, does
it answer "so what?" for someone who reads only that sentence?

Then the title is the message cut as short as it will go. The abstract is the message with its
reasons: what was known, what was missing, what you did, what you found, and what it means.
Check that the title, the abstract and the paper all say the same thing.

**Must know points for you.**

- Write your one message before you draft any section. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to draft. Go back to your results until you can say what they show.
- "This study is about..." is a topic, not a message. Test any candidate by asking whether it would be false had the data come out differently. If it would still be true, it claims nothing, and neither will a title or abstract built on it.
- Make the title the message in its shortest form, with no abbreviations, which ICMJE's Recommendations ask authors to avoid there. Use the words a searcher would type, and name the study design if the journal asks for it.
- Before anyone else reads your draft, list every number in the title and abstract and find each one in the results. ICMJE notes that abstracts often differ from the text. The abstract is the only part many readers read, so a wrong number there is the one most people take away.
- Use the same message for every other form of the work: the talk, the poster, the answer to a journalist. Mensh and Kording's test of success is that readers can still state your main contribution a year later. A message you can say in one breath is the one they can repeat.
- A clear message is not a true one. Mensh and Kording ask for a claim as simple as the data and logic support, but no simpler. Writing the message does not test whether the data carry it. Check that separately, and if they carry less, cut the message to fit them.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory: what are the two tests a one-message sentence must pass? And what are the parts
of an abstract's argument, in order?

**Exercise 2** (critique). Three residents offer the one message of their theses. All three are made up. Say which is a
message, what is wrong with the other two, and what the message does not show.

> (a) This thesis studies waist and body mass index in adults who come to a rural health centre.

> (b) We measured waist, body mass index, blood pressure and fasting blood sugar in 300 adults.
> We report each by age and sex.

> (c) In 300 adults at a rural health centre, waist put more people above its cut-off than body
> mass index did. So screening by body mass index alone would miss some of the people waist
> would flag.

**Exercise 3** (design). Write the one message of the section you chose for this book's build, in one or two sentences.
Test it both ways. Then write a title from it, and an abstract of five sentences, one for each
part of the argument.

**Exercise 4** (teaching). A journalist rings and asks: "Is obesity rising in India?" You have three minutes and the
NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 figures. Give the one quotable sentence, and say how sure the evidence lets
you be.
