# S58-R1-C14 · A figure makes one claim

**Definition.** A figure in a paper is drawn to make one claim: one statement about the data that a reader
should be able to repeat after looking at it. The claim is written as a sentence before the
kind of chart is chosen. One figure carries one claim. A second claim gets a second figure, or
goes into the text.

A result belongs in a sentence when it rests on a few numbers. It belongs in a table when the
reader needs exact values, or will look up one row among many. It belongs in a figure when the
claim is a pattern among many values, such as a trend, a gap or a ranking, that words would
take too long to carry. The same numbers are not shown in both a table and a figure.

The caption is the text printed with a figure; journals call it the figure legend. Its first
sentence states the finding, not the topic. Titles and explanations go in the caption, not on
the drawing.

A figure is made for one reader and one medium. A figure on a printed page can be studied for
as long as the reader likes. A figure on a slide is seen for a few seconds, from across a room,
while someone talks. The two are made separately.

**In plain terms.** Before you draw anything, write down the one sentence you want a reader to take away from the
figure. That sentence is the figure's claim. If you cannot write it, you do not have a figure
yet. If you write two, you have two figures, or one figure and a sentence in the text.

Then ask whether the claim needs a picture at all. Four numbers read well in a sentence. Forty do
not. A reader who wants to find one district's value wants a table. A reader who has to see
that one group sits above another, all the way along, wants a figure.

Last, put the sentence where the reader meets it first: the opening line of the caption.

**Figure.** In NFHS-5 (2019-21), a larger share of urban than of rural adults aged 15-49 had overweight or obesity, in women ({{n:nfhs5_women_ow_ob_urban_pct}}% against {{n:nfhs5_women_ow_ob_rural_pct}}%) and in men ({{n:nfhs5_men_ow_ob_urban_pct}}% against {{n:nfhs5_men_ow_ob_rural_pct}}%). Bars show the per cent with a body mass index of 25.0 kg/m^2 or more. Source: NFHS-5 India Fact Sheet, indicators 88 and 89.

*What the figure shows:* Two pairs of bars. Women: urban 33.2%, rural 19.7%. Men: urban 29.8%, rural 19.3%. In each pair the urban bar is the taller.

**Must know points for you.**

- Write the figure's claim as one sentence before you open any program that draws charts. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to draw. If you write two, make two figures, or put one claim in the text.
- "A result looks more serious as a figure" is the error. A few numbers belong in a sentence, where the reader takes them in without stopping. The ICMJE Recommendations ask for only the tables and figures the paper's argument needs.
- Never show the same numbers in a table and in a figure. Editors will ask you to cut one, and the ICMJE Recommendations say so. Decide by what the reader has to do: look a value up, use a table; see a pattern, use a figure.
- Make the first sentence of every caption the finding, not the topic. "Overweight and obesity, NFHS-4 and NFHS-5" names a topic. "A larger share of urban than of rural adults had overweight or obesity" is a finding. Put titles in the caption, not on the drawing.
- Do not lift a figure from your paper onto a slide. Make the slide version again, with fewer elements, thicker lines and bigger text, because it will be seen from a distance for a few seconds.
- The one-claim rule lets you cut data that are off to the side of the claim. It never lets you cut data that would weaken it. Before you drop a group or a year, ask whether a reader who saw it would doubt the claim. If so, it stays, or the claim changes.
- Wilke recommends three to six figures for a scientific paper, with the rest moved to an appendix or supplement. That is one author's advice. The journal's instructions to authors set the actual limit; read them first.
- A clear figure persuades whether or not its claim is true. When a press release shows you a figure with one obvious message, the clarity tells you the maker knew what to show. It does not tell you the data or the analysis are sound.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory: when does a result belong in a sentence, when in a table, and when in a figure?
Where does a figure's claim go, and what must never be shown twice?

**Exercise 2** (critique). A colleague's clinic audit has this caption over a figure of three panels. The audit is made
up.

> Figure 2. Clinic attendance, BMI categories and waist circumference, by age group and sex,
> April 2024 to March 2025.

Panel A shows monthly attendance as a line. Panel B shows the share of patients in each BMI
category, by age group, as stacked bars. Panel C shows mean waist circumference by sex, as two
bars. The text says the audit's main finding is that the share of patients with a BMI of 25.0
kg/m^2 or more rises with age. Say what is wrong and what you would do.

**Exercise 3** (design). Take the manuscript or thesis section you are rewriting for this book. List every result in
it. For each, write the one-sentence claim, then decide: sentence, table or figure. For each
figure, write the first sentence of its caption. Check that no numbers appear in both a table
and a figure.

**Exercise 4** (teaching). A first-year resident wants to keep all eight NFHS bars in one figure, "so the reader has
everything". Ten minutes, with a whiteboard. Explain why one claim per figure, and what
happens to the numbers she takes off.
