# S58-R1-C07 · The sentence as the unit of clarity

**Definition.** A sentence is clear when a reader takes from it, on one reading, the point the writer meant,
and gives each part of it the weight the writer meant. Readers look for each kind of
information in a fixed place in the sentence. Five principles put it there. They come from
Gopen and Swan's reader-expectation approach (1990) and the US Federal Plain Language
Guidelines (2011), and both sources call them principles, not rules.

1. One idea per sentence. Each sentence makes a single point. A sentence is too long when it
   holds more pieces of information that deserve emphasis than it has places for emphasis.
   Its word count does not decide it.
2. The subject close to its verb. Readers expect the verb straight after the subject. They
   read anything long between the two as an interruption, and so as less important.
3. The action in the verb. A hidden verb is an action turned into a noun, such as
   "increase", "assessment" or "measurement", carried by an empty verb such as "was
   observed", "was done" or "make". It is turned back into a verb.
4. Old information first, new information last. The start of a sentence, the topic position,
   holds the information that links back to what the reader already has, and names whose
   story the sentence tells. The end, the stress position, holds the new information the
   reader should weigh most.
5. The doer as the subject where the doer matters, in the active voice, with "we" for the
   authors' own actions. The passive is kept where the doer does not matter, or where the
   sentence carries on the story of the thing acted on.

A sentence that breaks the first principle is split into two or more sentences. It is not
compressed: its ideas are not fused into a string of nouns.

**In plain terms.** A reader reads your sentence once and decides what it said. Five habits make that decision go
your way.

Say one thing in each sentence.

Put the verb soon after the subject. The reader is waiting for it, and everything in between
feels like a delay.

Put the action in the verb. Write "the proportion rose", not "an increase was observed".

Start with what the reader already knows, and end with what is new. The end is where a reader
puts the most weight, so put there the thing you want remembered.

Name who did it when that matters. If it was you, write "we".

When a sentence breaks these habits, make it two sentences. Do not squeeze it shorter by piling
nouns together.

**Must know points for you.**

- Find the subject and the verb, and count the words between them. Does the reader have to wait through a list, a bracket or a clause before the verb arrives? Then move that material to the end of the sentence, or into a sentence of its own.
- "Long sentences are the problem, so cut words" is the error. Gopen and Swan report 10-word sentences that are hard to get through and 100-word sentences that are easy. The test is how many points in the sentence deserve weight. More than one, and the sentence needs splitting, however short it is.
- Search your draft for the empty verbs that carry hidden actions: "was done", "was carried out", "was observed", "was performed". Then look between "the" and "of" for a noun ending in -tion or -ment. Each one is an action hiding from its verb. Turn it back into a verb.
- End a results sentence on the number or finding you want remembered. A reader puts the most weight on the end of a sentence. A sentence that ends on "was observed", or on its source, spends that weight on nothing.
- In a methods section, name the doer of every step a reviewer would judge: who measured, who interviewed, who entered the data. Write "we" for your own steps if the journal allows it. Keep the passive only where the result is the same whoever did it.
- Split a sentence with several points into several sentences. Do not compress it into a string of nouns. A chain of three or more nouns makes the reader guess how they relate.
- These are principles, and they stop working when followed blindly. Gopen and Swan warn that slavish use of them does no better than slavishly preferring the active voice. If a rewrite makes a sentence worse for its paragraph, as "Bees disperse pollen" would in a paragraph about pollen, keep the original. If no rewrite will come, the trouble may be the thought, not the sentence: go back to the one claim you are making.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). Without looking back: where in a sentence does a reader put the most weight? Where does a
reader look for the link to what came before? What should you put in each place?

**Exercise 2** (critique). This sentence is made up, from a clinic audit at a primary health centre.

> Assessment of the adherence of the patients attending the hypertension clinic to their prescribed medicines by pill count was done at every visit by the staff nurse.

Find the subject's main word and the verb, and count the words between them. Name the hidden
verb and the doer. Then rewrite the sentence so that the doer is the subject and the action is
the verb.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A first-year resident brings you this pair from her thesis methods. She says you told her
always to use the active voice, so she changed the first into the second.

> Each blood sample was centrifuged within one hour of collection. Each blood sample was labelled with the participant's code.
>
> The laboratory technician centrifuged each blood sample within one hour of collection. The laboratory technician labelled each blood sample with the participant's code.

You have ten minutes and a whiteboard. Explain when the active voice helps here and when it
does not.

**Exercise 4** (critique). Take five sentences from the results part of the manuscript section you chose for this book's
build. For each, write down its subject, its verb, the number of words between them, and the
last three words of the sentence. Rewrite any sentence whose gap is long, whose action hides
in a noun, or whose last words are not the new information.
