# S58-R1-C01 · What a research paper is: its parts and what each part is for

**Definition.** An original research article is a journal paper that reports a study for the first time. The
International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) is a group of medical journal
editors whose Recommendations, updated January 2026, many journals follow. They say the text
of such an article is usually divided into Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion
(IMRAD). The IMRAD structure is named from the initials of the four sections.

The Recommendations give each section its job. The introduction gives the context of the study
and states its purpose or the hypothesis it tested; it holds no data or conclusions from the
study itself. The methods say how and why the study was done, in enough detail for others with
the data to reproduce the results, and hold only what was known when the protocol was written.
The results give the findings in a logical order, the most important first. The discussion
summarises the main findings, sets them among the other evidence, and states the study's
limitations and what follows from it.

Around the text sit a title page, which carries the title, an abstract and a numbered list of
references.

Other kinds of article are shaped differently. Case reports, narrative reviews and editorials
may be less structured or unstructured, and meta-analyses may need other formats. The journal
an author sends the paper to sets the final format.

IMRAD is a convention that editors adopted, not a fixed law of science. In four leading
general medical journals it was absent from original articles in 1935, reached 80 per cent of
them in the 1970s, and was the only pattern by the 1980s (Sollaci and Pereira, 2004).

**In plain terms.** A research paper reports one study. Most medical journals want it in the same four parts, in
the same order.

The introduction says why the study was needed and what question it asked. The methods say
what was done, so that someone else could check it or do it again. The results say what was
found. The discussion says what the findings mean, and where they stop.

Before these four come a title and an abstract, a short summary of the whole paper. After them
comes the list of references, the sources the paper leans on.

The four-part shape has a name, IMRAD, from the first letters of the parts. It is a habit that
editors spread in the twentieth century, not a rule of nature. So the journal you send your
paper to has the last word on its shape. Read its instructions before you start.

**Must know points for you.**

- Before you draft, open the instructions for authors of the journal you are aiming at. Note its article types, its abstract format and its word limits. ICMJE leaves the abstract's format to the journal, and a paper shaped for the wrong journal is reshaped before anyone reads its science.
- The two commonest misplacements are your own result in the introduction and a number you obtained during the study in the methods. ICMJE's Recommendations bar the first and send the second to the results. A reader who meets a result before its method cannot judge it.
- "Papers have always been written this way" is wrong. In four leading medical journals the IMRAD structure was absent in 1935. It passed 80 per cent of original articles only in the 1970s, and editors get much of the credit. ICMJE calls it a reflection of how discovery works. Both can be true. Neither makes it a rule for every kind of article.
- Do not force a case report, a narrative review or an editorial into the four parts. ICMJE says such articles may be less structured or unstructured, and that a meta-analysis may need a different format. Check the journal's instructions for that article type.
- Readers jump to the section where they expect to find a thing. So put it there. The sample size, how it was chosen and how things were measured go in the methods; every number the study produced goes in the results. A fact in the wrong section is a fact some readers will never find.
- The four parts tell you where each kind of sentence goes. They do not tell you what the paper claims, or whether the data carry that claim. A draft can be in perfect IMRAD order and still say nothing; check the claim separately.
- When you turn a thesis chapter into a paper, read the journal's instructions afresh. The thesis followed your university's rules. The paper follows the journal's, and its sections, abstract and length may all change.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). From memory, name the four sections of an original research article in the IMRAD structure,
and the question each one answers for a reader. Then name the three parts that sit around
them.

**Exercise 2** (critique). A colleague's draft has these four sentences in its methods section. The numbers are made up.

> (a) Adults aged 30 and over who came to the diabetes and blood pressure clinic on Mondays
> were eligible. (b) Waist was measured with a tape that does not stretch, at the level set in
> the protocol. (c) Of 260 adults who were eligible, 214 were measured. The rest said no or left
> first. (d) Waist was above the clinic's cut-off in 58 per cent of those measured, more than we
> expected.

Say which sentences belong in the methods, where the others go, and why.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). For a first-year resident, ten minutes with a whiteboard. She asks why journals insist on
four fixed sections when a paper would read better as a story. Answer her, and tell her the
one case where she should not use the four sections.
