# S58-R1-C20 · Words on a figure: axis titles with units, direct labels, and a caption that stands alone

**Definition.** The words on a figure let a reader understand it without the text of the paper. An axis title
names the quantity an axis shows and, for a measured quantity, its unit, as in "body mass
index (kg/m^2)". An axis of categories, such as sex or place of residence, needs no unit. Tick
labels are the values printed along an axis. A legend is the key that says which colour or
mark stands for which group; a direct label puts the group's name beside its data instead.
Every letter and number must stay legible at the size the figure is printed.

The caption is the text printed with a figure, and journals call it the figure legend. Its
first element is the figure's title, which states the finding. It then says what is shown, in
whom, where and when; how many independent people or units were measured, written n; what any
error bar shows; and where the data came from. With its caption, a figure should be readable
on its own.

An error bar is a line drawn through a value, usually a mean, to show a range around it.
Descriptive error bars show how the data are spread: the range, from the lowest value to the
highest, or the standard deviation (SD). Inferential error bars show how precisely the mean is
estimated: the standard error of the mean (SE), which is the SD divided by the square root of
n, or a 95% confidence interval (CI). A 95% CI is a range worked out from the sample so that,
if the study were repeated many times, 95 per cent of such ranges would contain the true mean
of the population. When n is 10 or more, it is approximately the mean plus or minus 2 SE. The
two kinds of bar are drawn the same way, so a bar means nothing until the caption says which it
is.

In n, independent means separate people or units. Measuring one person three times gives
three replicates and an n of 1.

**In plain terms.** A reader may look at your figure before reading a word of your paper. So the figure has to
explain itself. Each axis says what it measures and in what unit. Each group has its name
written beside it. The caption under the figure says what the finding is, who was measured, how
many, and where the numbers came from.

Error bars need special care. The same line through a mean can show how spread out the people
are, or how sure you are of the mean itself. These are different things, and they look the
same. So the caption has to say which one it is, every time.

**Figure.** One set of data, three error bars. Cumming, Fidler and Vaux's three measurements, 28.7, 38.7 and 52.6, have a mean of 40.0. One arm of each bar, as it would be drawn above and below that mean: 12.0 for the standard deviation, 6.93 for the standard error, and 27.7 for an approximate 95% confidence interval (4 times the standard error, their rule for three values). A reader cannot tell these apart unless the caption names the bar.

*What the figure shows:* Three bars of different heights for the same data: 12.0 for the standard deviation, 6.93 for the standard error, and 27.7 for an approximate 95% confidence interval.

**Must know points for you.**

- "An error bar shows the error" is the error. The same mean of 40.0 can carry an arm of 12.0, 6.93 or 27.7 from the same three numbers. Write in every caption whether a bar is the SD, the SE or a 95% CI, and give n beside it.
- Title every axis with the quantity and its unit: "body mass index (kg/m^2)", not "BMI". An axis of categories needs no unit. If a reader must guess, the axis is not finished.
- Write the caption in a fixed order. The finding comes first. Then what each mark shows; who, where and when; n; what any error bar is; and the source. Check it by covering the paper's text and reading only the figure and its caption.
- n counts separate people or units, not measurements. Weigh 40 children three times each and you have 120 readings and an n of 40. An error bar worked from all 120 readings looks narrower than the evidence allows.
- "Legend" means two things. To a journal, the figure legend is the caption: ICMJE asks for "legends for illustrations" on a separate page. On the figure itself, a legend is the key to the colours. When an author's instructions ask for legends, send captions.
- To show how spread out people are, draw the SD or, better, the points themselves. To show how precisely a mean or a prevalence is known, draw a 95% CI. ICMJE asks for measures of uncertainty such as confidence intervals, not P values alone.
- Before a figure leaves you, shrink it to {{n:figure_check_width_low_cm}} to {{n:figure_check_width_high_cm}} cm wide on screen and read every label. Most programs' default text is too small at that size.
- Naming the bar lets a reader read it; it does not license every comparison. Bars on the same group measured at different times cannot show whether the group changed (Cumming's Rule 8). For that, the change in each person has to be worked out and its own interval drawn.

**Exercise 2** (interpretation). The numbers here are made up. A figure shows mean waist circumference in two groups, 92 cm and
88 cm. Each mean has an error bar whose arm is 3 cm long. The caption ends: "Values are mean ±
SEM; n = 40 per group." SEM is the standard error of the mean.

What does a 3 cm arm tell you about how much waist circumference varies among the people? What
does it tell you about how precisely each mean is known?

**Exercise 3** (critique). A draft thesis figure, made up for this exercise, has a side axis titled "BMI", no title on the
bottom axis, and this caption:

> Figure 3. Graph showing BMI of participants in the two groups. Error bars represent
> variation. *p<0.05.

List everything a reader cannot tell from it, and rewrite what you can.

**Exercise 4** (teaching). A first-year resident shows you two slides made from the same data. On one, the error bars are
