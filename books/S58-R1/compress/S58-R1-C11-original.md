# S58-R1-C11 · Measuring a draft: words per sentence, a readability score, and what they cannot show

**Definition.** A readability formula estimates how hard a passage is to read from three counts: words,
sentences and syllables. A syllable is one beat of a spoken word: "row" has one, "maintain"
two, "dictionary" four.

The counts give two averages. The average sentence length (ASL) is the number of words
divided by the number of sentences. The average syllables per word (ASW) is the number of
syllables divided by the number of words.

The Flesch Reading Ease score (FRE), published by Rudolf Flesch in 1948, is 206.835 minus
1.015 times ASL minus 84.6 times ASW. Flesch wrote the second term as 0.846 times the
syllables per 100 words, which gives the same score. A higher score means easier text. For
almost all ordinary prose the score falls between 0 and 100, but the formula can go below 0
or above 100. Flesch chose its numbers so that it predicted the school grade of children who
could answer three-quarters of the questions on a set of reading test passages. He then
reversed the signs so that a higher score means easier.

Kincaid and colleagues (1975) gave a grade-level formula: 0.39 times ASL, plus 11.8 times
ASW, minus 15.59. Its result is a school grade level. The report calls the result GL, for
grade level. Later writers call the formula the Flesch–Kincaid grade level (FKGL). Its
numbers were fitted to the reading-test results of 531 enlisted personnel of the United States
Navy, on 18 passages from Navy training manuals. Fitted means chosen so that the formula's
predictions came as close as they could to those results.

Both formulas count lengths. Neither reads meaning, order or the logic between sentences.

Measured this way, scientific abstracts have grown harder to read. Plavén-Sigray and
colleagues (2017) scored 709,577 abstracts from 123 journals, published from 1881 to 2015.
The yearly average FRE fell steadily. In 1960, 14% of the abstracts scored below 0; in 2015,
22% did. More general scientific jargon accounts for part of the fall. A study showing that
readers understand recent abstracts as well as older ones would show the fall to be in the
score and not in understanding.

**In plain terms.** A readability formula reads your draft with a ruler, not with a mind. It counts how long your
sentences are, in words, and how long your words are, in syllables. Long sentences and long
words push the score towards hard. You can work it out by hand in five minutes, and most word
processors print it for you.

Use it as a trip-wire. If one paragraph of yours scores far harder than the rest, read its
longest sentences first, and split or cut them as `S58-R1-C07` showed.

Never use it as a target. A row of four-word fragments scores as very easy, and it can still
leave out the one thing the reader needed. The score cannot see that, because it never reads
a word for its meaning.

**Must know points for you.**

- "The score went up, so the text is clearer" is the error. The formula counts lengths and nothing else. Chopping a sentence into fragments raises the score and can lose the meaning, as Rewrite B did. Plavén-Sigray and colleagues warn that changing a text only to raise its score "does not automatically make a text more understandable".
- Before you compare two scores, find out how each counted figures. Flesch in 1948 read them aloud, Flesch in 1979 counted each as one syllable, and one large study dropped them. On a sentence thick with figures, the same words scored 38.5 or 1.1.
- Use the score as a trip-wire on your own draft, never as a target. Score each paragraph with the same program. Where one scores far harder than the rest, read its longest sentences and rewrite them for the reader. Then read the result; do not rewrite to reach a number.
- A grade level is carried over from the people the formulas were fitted on: Flesch's schoolchildren and Kincaid's 531 Navy personnel. Flesch wrote that his score tracks a school grade only up to about seventh grade. Your reader may be far from those groups: a specialist, say, or a patient in an Indian clinic. Then treat the number as a rough guess, and test the text on real readers: `S58-R1-C13` sets out one way.
- Do not score a Hindi or Tamil translation of a leaflet with these formulas. Their numbers were fitted on English passages, and a syllable count in another language means something else. For a health leaflet in any language, give it to a few people from the audience and ask them what it tells them to do.
- Mind the unit of the syllable term. Flesch's 1948 article multiplies syllables per 100 words by 0.846; his 1979 book multiplies syllables per word by 84.6. Mix the two and the score comes out in the thousands below zero.
- When someone says scientific writing is getting harder to read, the measured fact is narrower. In one study of 709,577 abstracts, the share scoring below 0 on the FRE rose from 14% in 1960 to 22% in 2015. That is a change in a length-based score, partly from more general scientific jargon. It does not show that specialists understand the newer abstracts less well.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A journalist rings you: "Is it true scientific papers are getting harder to read?" You have

**1.** A paragraph has 120 words, 6 sentences and 180 syllables. Find its average sentence length

**2.** A passage has an average sentence length of 20 words and an average of 1.5 syllables per

**3.** A passage averages 1.6 syllables per word. What average sentence length, to the nearest

**4.** In 1948 Flesch scored an issue of the New Yorker magazine from October 1946. His counts were

Flesch–Kincaid grade level for the same counts.

**5.** Flesch (1979) gives this sentence a score of 32.

Count its words and syllables, and compute its Flesch Reading Ease score to one decimal
place. Then work out how far one syllable more or less in your count moves the score.

**6.** Suppose the 43-word sentence of the last problem could be split into three sentences. No word

**7.** Score this results sentence twice: once taking each figure as one syllable, and once counting

**8.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
a paragraph: 200 words, 10 sentences, 300 syllables
ASL = 200 divided by 10 = 20
syllables per 100 words = 300 divided by 200 times 100 = 150
FRE = 206.835 minus 1.015 times 20 minus 84.6 times 150 = -12503.465
so the paragraph is unreadable
```

**9.** Here is a student's worked score of a two-sentence paragraph. Find the step that broke.

```working
the paragraph has 60 words and 90 syllables
it has 2 full stops and 3 more clauses joined by "and", so 5 sentences
ASL = 60 divided by 5 = 12; ASW = 90 divided by 60 = 1.5
FRE = 206.835 minus 12.18 minus 126.9 = 67.755
a score above 60, so it is Plain English
```
