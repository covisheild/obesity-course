# S58-R1-C06 · The parts of a sentence a writer works with

**Definition.** A sentence is built from clauses. A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. A
main clause contains a subject and a predicate and can stand alone as a sentence. A
subordinate clause also has a subject and a verb. It opens with a word such as although,
because, when, if, since or while, and it cannot stand alone.

The verb expresses an action, an occurrence or a state of being. The predicate is the verb
together with everything that completes it.

The subject names what the clause is about: the thing the verb is said of. A subject may be
one word or a long phrase. Its main word is the noun the verb is said of, and the rest of the
phrase describes that noun.

The object is the noun that receives the action of the verb. In "The investigator measured
each woman's weight", the object is "each woman's weight".

The doer is whoever or whatever performs the action, whether or not it is the subject.

Voice says which of them is the subject. In the active voice, the subject performs the action
of the verb. In the passive voice, the subject receives the action: the active sentence's
object becomes the subject. The verb then takes a form of "to be" (is, was, were, has been)
and a past participle, the form of the verb that follows "has" or "was" (measured, taken,
given). The doer moves into a phrase beginning "by", or is left out.

Voice is not tense. Tense places the action in time. "Measured" and "was measured" are both
past: the first active, the second passive.

**In plain terms.** To fix a sentence, you have to be able to point at its parts. Four words do most of the work,
and each is easy to find once you know what to ask.

The verb is the word for what happens: measured, rose, is. Find it first.

The subject is what the sentence is about. Ask "who or what" in front of the verb: who
measured? What rose? The answer is the subject.

The object is what the action is done to. In "We measured the weight", the weight is the
object.

The doer is whoever did the action. In "We measured the weight", the doer is "we", and "we" is
also the subject. That is the active voice.

Turn it round: "The weight was measured". Now the thing measured is the subject, and the doer
has gone. That is the passive voice. Its sign is a form of "to be" with a verb ending, usually,
in -ed.

A clause is a subject with its verb. A main clause can stand as a sentence on its own. A clause
that starts with "because", "when" or "although" cannot.

**Must know points for you.**

- "The passive voice is the past tense" is the error. Voice says whether the subject did the action or received it. Tense says when. "We measure" and "Weight is measured" are both present. When an editor tells you to remove the passive, check voice, not tense.
- Find the parts in a fixed order. Verb first. Then ask "who or what" in front of it to find the subject. Then look for "by", or ask who did it, to find the doer. Done in this order, the parts of even a long sentence come out the same way every time.
- A passive sentence with no "by" hides who did the work. In a methods section that is the question a reviewer asks first: who measured, who interviewed, who entered the data? Before you submit, find each passive verb and ask whether the reader needs the doer. If yes, name it.
- The two signs of the passive, a form of "to be" and an -ed word, are a quick test that can mislead. Both may be missing from a passive sentence, and both can appear in one that is not passive. When the test is unsure, ask whether the subject did the action or received it.
- Put the point of a sentence in its main clause. A reader takes the main clause as the main idea and a clause beginning "because" or "although" as the lesser one. If your finding sits in the subordinate clause, the reader will weigh it less than you meant.
