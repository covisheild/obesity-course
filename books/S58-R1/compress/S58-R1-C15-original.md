# S58-R1-C15 · Choosing the mark: what the eye reads accurately, and the shape of the data

**Definition.** A mark is anything drawn that stands for one number (`B0-R0-C40`). The reader gets the number
back by a judgment the eye makes about the mark. Cleveland and McGill (1984) called these
judgments elementary perceptual tasks, and named ten. They are position along a common scale,
position along scales that are not lined up, length, direction, angle, area, volume,
curvature, shading and colour saturation. A common scale is one axis that every mark being compared is read
against.

They proposed an order, from the judgment people make most accurately to the least. First
comes position along a common scale, then positions along scales not lined up. Length,
direction and angle share third place, then area. Volume and curvature come next, and shading
and colour saturation last. Their rule for
design follows: a graph should use judgments as high in this order as it can.

The order is a hypothesis. Their two experiments tested two parts of it, position against
length and position against angle. Each person judged what per cent a smaller marked value was
of a larger one, and 51 people's judgments were analysed in each experiment. Position won both
times.

Heer and Bostock (2010) repeated the position-against-length experiment with 50 people
per chart, recruited online, and added angle and area judged the same way. The order of
position and length held. Area did worse than angle, and both did worse than position. Angle
did not do worse than length, which the hypothesis predicted.

The mark is chosen by the shape of the data as well. Amounts for a set of groups take bars, or
dots where the bars would end. Many values from each group take a chart of their distribution.
Parts of one whole take side-by-side bars, stacked bars or, for a few parts, a pie. One measured
quantity against another takes a scatterplot. Change over time takes a line.

What would overturn this: experiments in which people judge areas or angles as accurately as
positions, on the questions a paper asks of its reader. The experiments so far asked one
question, what per cent one value is of another.

**In plain terms.** Your eye reads some marks better than others. It is best at comparing where two points sit
against the same ruler. It is worse at comparing lengths that do not start from the same line,
and worse again at comparing angles and areas. So put the numbers a reader must compare on one
shared axis, side by side.

Then match the mark to what the numbers are. Amounts for a few groups take bars or dots. Many
values from one group take a chart of how they spread, which the next section is about. Parts
of one whole can take a pie, though side-by-side bars compare better. Change over time takes a
line.

The order of the marks rests on a handful of experiments, all asking one question: what per
cent is this value of that one? It is good advice, not a law.

**Figure.** Cleveland and McGill (1984), first experiment. Position judgments were 60% of all judgments but drew 22% of the large errors; length judgments were 40% of judgments and drew 78%. Per judgment, a large error was 5.3 times as frequent on length as on position.

**Must know points for you.**

- Put the values a reader must compare on one shared axis, next to each other: bars from one shared start, or dots. Avoid making the reader compare pieces stacked inside bars, or slices of a pie. Those are lengths without a shared start, and angles.
- In Cleveland and McGill's experiments, large errors were 5.3 times as frequent per judgment on length as on position. On angle they were 7.3 times as frequent as on position. The pie beat the bar chart in only 3 of 40 comparisons.
- "The ranking of marks was proved by experiment" is the error. The full order of ten judgments is Cleveland and McGill's hypothesis. Their experiments tested only position against length and against angle. Heer and Bostock added area, which came last of the four, but found angle no worse than length.
- Before you draw a pie, check that its numbers are parts of one whole: shares of the same group of people, adding to 100 per cent. Prevalences in different groups are not. Neither are conditions a person can have together, such as stunting and underweight in the same child.
- When a trainee shows you a pie chart or a stacked bar chart, ask for the same numbers as dots or grouped bars on one axis. Cleveland and McGill concluded that pies and divided bar charts should not be used, because better forms exist.
- Name the shape of your numbers before you choose the chart. Is it amounts for groups, many values per group, parts of a whole, one quantity against another, or change over time? The shape narrows the choice to two or three kinds of chart.
- When the value axis cannot start at zero, as on a logarithmic axis, draw dots, not bars. A bar's length then measures the distance from an arbitrary starting point, not the value.
- The evidence covers one task: judging what per cent one marked value is of another, on bar and pie charts. It does not tell you whether a reader sees that parts make a whole, or remembers the chart next week. Use the order as a guide, as its authors did, not as a rule that settles every choice.
