# A1 · Counting, place value, and what a calculator is doing

## Exercises

**E1.** Five lakh: `5,00,000` (Indian) / `500,000` (international). Twenty-five thousand: `25,000` in both — the Indian rule (last three digits together, pairs above) and the international rule (threes) give the same commas below a lakh. Ceiling ÷ proviso = 500,000 ÷ 25,000 = **20 times larger**.

**E2.** The first sentence is right: section 53's ten lakh *is* one million. The break is at the second sentence's conversion. One million is ten lakh, which is a *tenth* of a crore. Ten crore is `10,00,00,000` — a hundred times the stated figure. The step that broke: reading "million" onto the Indian ladder at the crore rung.

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** 9 = ninety thousand, 0 = no thousands, 8 = eight hundred, 0 = no tens, 7 = seven. Five digits.

**P02 — worked.** Two lakh `2,00,000` / `200,000`. Twelve lakh `12,00,000` / `1,200,000`. Three crore `3,00,00,000` / `30,000,000`.

**P03 — worked.** `9,900,000` is larger. Stripped of commas: 9090000 against 9900000; same digit count, so compare from the left — 9,0 against 9,9.

**P04 — worked.** Ten lakh `10,00,000` / `1,000,000`. Fifty lakh `50,00,000` / `5,000,000`. Five times larger.

**P05 — worked.** One crore `1,00,00,000` / `10,000,000`, eight digits. Ten crore `10,00,00,000` / `100,000,000`, nine digits.

**P06 — worked.** 100,000 × 450 = 45,000,000. `4,50,00,000` / `45,000,000`. Four crore fifty lakh; forty-five million.

**P07 — worked.** "Fifty lakh = 5,000,000" is right. "or five crore" is the broken step: five crore is 50,000,000, ten times more. 5,000,000 is fifty lakh, or half a crore.

**P08 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the meaning of decimal notation — that "1.8 crore" denotes a quantity between one crore and two crore.** A1 never writes a number with a point in it. I settled it by digit count instead (18,00,000 has seven digits, one crore has eight, so the total is below one crore and cannot be 1.8 of them). Total is eighteen lakh. The broken step is the naming of 18,00,000 in crore; the addition is right.

**P09 — worked.** Ten million is one crore. Section 53's ceiling is ten lakh, which is one million. The press line is ten times too large. It does not establish whether ten lakh is a large or small penalty, whether it is ever imposed, whether some other section applies, or whether the Act has been amended since.

**P10 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the value of a billion.** A1 names billion once and never says what it is worth. Assuming a thousand million: 400 crore = 4,000,000,000 = 4 billion, correct. 4,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 4,000 rupees each, correct. Neither figure establishes that the money reaches those people, that 4,000 rupees is adequate, that the reach figure is real, or that cost-per-head is the right measure at all.

**A1: worked 1-7, 9; guessed 8, 10**

## What A1 taught me that changes what I would do

- A lakh is not a million; the two ladders step by two and by three powers of ten and never line up.
- Count digits, not commas, and when they disagree trust the digits.
- Say the number out loud in the naming system you are writing in, before pressing equals.
- A calculator will divide money by children without objecting; the arithmetic being right is not the same as the question being right.
- Refuse "a lot" as an answer to how big a crore is — give ten million, a hundred lakh, and the digit count.

## Gaps

- **Billion is never defined.** *"Most other places count thousand, million, billion."* The section gives values for lakh, crore and million and stops. **Blocks P10 (level 10)** for a reader who cannot supply it, and P10 is the problem where the whole check hangs on that one conversion.
- **"How many times larger" is never named as an operation.** *"Type one million, then divide by five hundred thousand, and it returns 2."* The text never says what the 2 means. E1 and P04 both ask for "how many times larger" and the reader must infer that this is what division answers.
- **Decimal multipliers of a unit appear before decimals exist.** P08 hands the reader "1.8 crore" and P10 "4 billion". Decimals are not introduced until A3. **Hits P08 (level 8).**
- **Comparing two numbers of equal digit length is never demonstrated.** The section's whole method is *"count the digits"*, which ties for P03. The leftmost-digit comparison I used is nowhere in the text. **Hits P03 (level 3).**
- **The place-name ladder below a lakh is never listed.** The reader is told only that each place is ten times the one right of it, and is shown "five hundred" and "five". **P01 (level 1)** asks what each digit of 90,807 is worth, and the names (ten thousands, thousands, hundreds, tens) have to be built by the reader.
- **Compound naming is never shown.** P06 wants 45,000,000 named in both systems. The text names single rungs (one lakh, one crore) and never composes one (four crore fifty lakh). **Hits P06 (level 6).**
- **Read twice — "Here the last three digits go together and everything above that is grouped in pairs: 10,00,000."** "Here" points at nothing. The preceding example was 4,505, which has no lakh in it, and 10,00,000 arrives with no introduction. I had to read forward to line 34 to learn this was the Indian grouping rule.
- **Read twice — "Ten lakh, they think, and lakh sounds like million."** "They" has no antecedent; the paragraph begins mid-anecdote. Nothing before it introduces a person making a mistake.
- **Read twice — "So count the digits, not the commas."** The "So" follows a paragraph about whether a penalty is large, which does not lead to it.
- **E2 asks "say which step it happened at" and the section never decomposes a sentence into steps in front of the reader.** The illustration shows an error but presents it as a single wrong belief, not a numbered chain.

# A2 · Fractions

## Exercises

**E1.** 500/800 → divide both by 100 → 5/8. It is the take-home ration for a child of six months to three years expressed as a fraction of the ration for a malnourished child of six months to six years. It is not a fraction of what any child needs in a day, nor of what any child actually receives.

**E2.** The arithmetic (12/20 = 3/5) is right; the whole has been swapped. Three fifths is the lower primary *standard* as a fraction of the upper primary *standard* — the Act says nothing about a child's protein requirement, so "three fifths of their protein" has no denominator behind it. Defensible rewrite: *"The lower primary protein standard, at 12 grams, is three fifths of the upper primary standard of 20 grams. Neither figure states what a child requires, and neither states what a child receives."*

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** 6/8 ÷2 → 3/4. 20/50 ÷10 → 2/5. 36/48 ÷2 → 18/24 ÷2 → 9/12 ÷3 → 3/4.

**P02 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the method for choosing a common denominator (multiply the two denominators together) and the fact that this is what "put them over the same denominator" means.** 3/5 = 24/40, 5/8 = 25/40, so five eighths is larger.

**P03 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the rule that once denominators match you add the numerators and leave the denominator alone.** 2/5 = 6/15, 1/3 = 5/15, total 11/15. You cannot add tops and bottoms because fifths and thirds are different-sized parts; adding the bottoms changes how big each part is, which the section's own rule forbids.

**P04 — worked.** 600/700 → ÷100 → 6/7.

**P05 — worked.** 12/20 ÷4 → 3/5. 450/500 ÷10 → 45/50 ÷5 → 9/10.

**P06 — worked.** 240/400 ÷10 → 24/40 ÷8 → 3/5. The whole is the 400 households in that block in that month — not households in the district, and not people.

**P07 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the addition rule again.** The broken step is the first: the denominators were added. 1/4 + 1/4 = 2/4 = 1/2. I could smell the error without the rule (adding something to a quarter cannot leave a quarter), but I could not have stated the right answer.

**P08 — worked.** 450/700 reduces to 9/14, near enough two thirds, and the section grants the arithmetic. What broke is the whole: 9/14 is the lower primary standard as a fraction of the *upper primary standard*, not of a child's daily energy. Neither row of the Act states a daily requirement.

**P09 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the addition rule, and the reading of "half" as the fraction 1/2** (the text never writes a half). Where the nine tenths came from: 2/5 + 1/2 = 4/10 + 5/10 = 9/10. The addition is invalid because the two fractions are cut from different wholes — anganwadi centres and schools. What to compute instead: the count of centres reporting, plus the count of schools reporting, over the total count of facilities. What that does not establish: what "a shortage" meant, how long it lasted, how severe it was, whether the two facility types were assessed the same way, or whether the reporting was complete.

**P10 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the common-denominator method again.** 3/5 = 6/10 and 1/2 = 5/10, so the move is one tenth of the standard. What it does not establish: which standard, for which age group, and therefore what the whole is; whether any child received the meal; whether provision changed or measurement changed; how many children sit behind each fraction; or whether one tenth of a standard is a difference that matters.

**A2: worked 1, 4, 5, 6, 8; guessed 2, 3, 7, 9, 10**

## What A2 taught me that changes what I would do

- A fraction is a division not yet carried out; numerator counts parts, denominator says how many the whole was cut into.
- Multiply or divide top and bottom by the same thing and the value holds; change one alone and it is a different number.
- A fraction is always a fraction of something, and two fractions cut from different wholes cannot be compared or added even though the arithmetic will still run.
- A fraction on its own is not a finding. Ask for the denominator every time, of my own numbers first.
- Refuse to answer "what fraction of a child's need does this meal cover" with whatever fraction I happen to be holding.

## Gaps

- **The single largest gap in the eight files: the section tells the reader to compare and add fractions and never once does either.** *"To simplify a fraction, divide the top and the bottom by the same number. To compare or add two, put them over the same denominator first."* That is the entire treatment. The illustration only simplifies. There is no worked example anywhere in the section of putting two fractions over a common denominator, of choosing which denominator, or of what to do with the numerators once they match. **This blocks P02 (level 2), P03 (level 3), P07 (level 7), P09 (level 9) and P10 (level 10) — five of ten, spanning the whole ladder.** P07 is the sharpest case: it is a find-the-broken-step problem about an addition, and the correct procedure appears nowhere in the section.
- **Finding a common divisor is never taught, only performed.** The illustration picks divisors with two cues — *"both ended in a zero"* and *"both are in the five times table"*. P01's 36/48 fits neither. A reader with no remembered multiplication tables has no method, only three worked instances.
- **"Times table" is used as a known term.** *"both are in the five times table"*. Nothing in A1 or A2 explains what a times table is.
- **"A half" is used but never written as a fraction.** P09 and P10 both require treating "half" as 1/2 and putting it over a common denominator.
- **Anganwadi is used without explanation** (P09). Also "take-home ration", "lactating mother" and "hot cooked meal" arrive in the illustration as if already defined.
- **Read twice — "This section tells you how to handle a fraction once you have one. It cannot tell you what the whole was. It comes from the document, the table heading or the person who handed you the number."** The "It" in the third sentence is the whole; the "It" in the second sentence is the section. The referent flips mid-paragraph and I read it twice before I saw which way round it went.
- **Read twice — "Cut a roti into fourteen equal pieces and take nine of them. Now cut the same roti into seventy pieces and take forty-five."** The conclusion ("exactly the same amount") is asserted without the step that shows 9 and 14 each multiplied by five. The reader must reconstruct the link to the rule stated in the next paragraph.
- **The section says a fraction is "a division that has not yet been carried out" and never carries one out.** 9/14 is never turned into a decimal or a percentage, so the reader is never shown what the division would produce. P08 asks the reader to accept that 9/14 "is about two thirds" — a comparison of two fractions, which is precisely the untaught move.

# A3 · Decimals, rounding, and significant figures

## Exercises

**E1.** Six people × 5 kg = 30 kg per household per month. Daily: 30 ÷ 31 = 0.968, 30 ÷ 28 = 1.071. So **about 1 kilogram a day**, somewhere between 0.97 and 1.07. I stopped there because the 5 is exact (it is written into an Act) but the number of days in a month is not — it is a choice, and the answer moves across that whole range depending on which month.

**E2.** I would ask what the 4.8732 was computed from and how many beneficiaries were in the denominator. Four decimal places on a kilogram is a tenth of a gram per person per month. I expect the answer to be that it came from a total tonnage divided by a headcount, that the tonnage was weighed to no better than the nearest quintal, and that the digits after the first two are what the calculator produced rather than anything the register can support. The defensible figure is 4.9 kg.

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** 0.3849 → 0.38 (next digit 4). 12.0761 → 12.08 (next digit 6). 0.0955 → 0.10 (next digit 5). 7.2950 → 7.30 by the rule as stated (next digit 5) — but this is precisely the case the section flags and then does not resolve.

**P02 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the convention for where to stop counting significant figures.** Answers given: 450 → 2 (the trailing zero taken as a placeholder); 0.0072 → 2; 6.030 → 4 (the trailing zero after a point taken as significant); 1,200 → 2, and genuinely ambiguous as written. Note that the section's own words — *"counted from the first digit that is not zero"* — say where to *start* and never where to *stop*. Read literally they give 450 → 3, 6.030 → 4, 1,200 → 4, which contradicts how the section itself treats 450 elsewhere. I answered against the section's practice rather than its stated rule.

**P03 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the procedure for rounding to a number of significant figures**, as distinct from rounding to decimal places. 17 ÷ 7 = 2.428571429 on the display; to three significant figures, 2.43.

**P04 — worked.** 5 ÷ 4 = 1.25 kg per week. I would report **about 1.2 kg a week**, because the "4 weeks in a month" is a chosen approximation, not a fact — a month is nearer 4.3 weeks, which gives 1.16 — so the second decimal place is not earned.

**P05 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **a decision the section's own rules make impossible.** 450 × 196 = 88,200. The section says an Act figure is exact ("Five kilograms in an Act is exactly five") and 450 is an Act figure; 196 is a count of days, also exact. By the section's rule the answer is exactly 88,200 and the significant-figure question does not arise. If instead 450 is treated as a 2-significant-figure measurement the answer is 88,000. I cannot tell from the text which the question wants.

**P06 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **the trailing-zero convention again.** 1,850 ÷ 387 = 4.7803. I would report **4.8 kg per household**. What fixed the precision: the 1,850, whose trailing zero suggests it was recorded to the nearest ten kilograms; the 387 is an exact count and constrains nothing.

**P07 — worked.** The break is at step two: 8.64 was rounded in the middle of the working, not at the end. The section's rule is to keep full precision and round once, and its own worked example shows what rounding early costs (0.17 × 30 = 5.1 against an exact start of 5). Here it happens not to change the final answer — 8.64 ÷ 4 = 2.16 → 2.2 either way — which makes it the more dangerous habit, not the less.

**P08 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **what a per cent is** (A3 does not define it; A4 does). Six significant figures on a coverage figure cannot come from two register counts unless the registers hold hundreds of thousands of households. Report it as about 65 per cent, and ask for the two counts.

**P09 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **percentage change.** 5 − 4.8732 = 0.1268; 0.1268 ÷ 5 = 0.02536, so 2.536 per cent — the arithmetic checks. Both figures carry false precision; the defensible statement is 4.9 against 5, a shortfall of about 3 per cent. What it does not establish: whether any single household received less than its entitlement (a mean hides the whole distribution), why the gap exists, whether the register is accurate, or whether 3 per cent is a difference anyone would feel.

**P10 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **percentage change, and the idea of measurement error in a mean.** 2,014.6 − 1,987.3 = 27.3; 27.3 ÷ 2,014.6 = 0.01355, so 1.36 per cent. I would not publish it. Five significant figures on a mean dietary intake is not a precision any dietary measurement supports, and a 1 per cent move is far below the error on such a measurement. Report it as roughly 2,010 to roughly 1,990, a fall of about 1 per cent, and only with the spread and the sample size beside it. It does not establish that anyone's intake changed, that the change is real rather than noise, what caused it, or that it matters.

**A3: worked 1, 4, 7; guessed 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10**

## What A3 taught me that changes what I would do

- Calculator digits are not precision; they are what the division produced.
- Keep full precision through the working and round once, at the end, to what the weakest input earned.
- Ask whether a number is exact (written into an Act) or measured (read off a scale) before deciding how to round it.
- A per-month entitlement converted to a per-day figure is where false precision enters; give a range and say where the spread came from.
- Say so at the time when a number I am handed carries more digits than its source could support, and refuse to quote it in public even when it came from above.

## Gaps

- **There is no rule for where the significant-figure count stops.** *"The significant figures of a number are the digits that carry information about the quantity, counted from the first digit that is not zero."* This defines the start only. Trailing zeros — the entire difficulty — are never mentioned. **Blocks P02 (level 2) outright, and contaminates P05 (level 5), P06 (level 6) and P08 (level 8),** all of which require counting significant figures in a number with a trailing zero (450, 1,850, and the implied register counts).
- **Rounding to significant figures is never demonstrated, only rounding to decimal places.** *"Pick the place you want to stop at and look at the very next digit."* Every worked instance is a decimal place. **P03 (level 3) asks for three significant figures** and P05 asks how many the answer "deserves"; the operation appears in this section only inside P07, which is the deliberately broken example.
- **Per cent is used in three of the ten problems and is not defined until A4.** P08 ("64.8135 per cent"), P09 ("a shortfall of 2.536 per cent") and P10 ("a fall of 1.36 per cent") all require computing or checking a percentage. **This removes the entire top band, levels 8 to 10, for a reader taking the files in order.** It is a sequencing error, not a content one: swapping A3 and A4 would fix it.
- **The exact/measured rule and the significant-figure rule contradict each other and the section does not notice.** *"Five kilograms in an Act is exactly five."* and *"A quantity calculated from other quantities cannot carry more significant figures than the least precise of them."* P05 sits exactly on the seam: 450 is an Act figure, hence exact, hence 88,200 carries as many figures as you like — which is surely not the intended answer.
- **The flagged rounding edge case is raised and abandoned.** *"When the digit you are dropping is exactly five and nothing follows it, the rule above always pushes up."* The sentence stops there. No alternative is given, no reason why always pushing up is a problem is stated, and **P01 (level 1) contains 7.2950, which lands on it.** The reader is told there is a trap and not told what it is or how to get out.
- **The two-ends method is shown once and never named.** Dividing by 31 and by 28 to bracket an answer is the most useful move in the section. It is performed once and appears in no must-know point, so a reader has no cue to reach for it in E1 or P04 — where it is exactly what is wanted.
- **Read twice — "You have 0.1666666667 and you want two places after the point."** This arrives *after* the section has already concluded that the honest answer is 0.16 to 0.18. On first reading it looks like a retraction of the range. It is in fact a separate demonstration of the rounding rule using the same number, but nothing says so.
- **Significant figures are defined in the opening and never used in the illustration.** The worked example is entirely about decimal places and ranges. The third of the three things the section names in its title gets a definition, a warning, and no demonstration at all.

# A4 · Percentages, and the percentage of a percentage

## Exercises

**E1.** 12 to 20: change 8, over the starting value 12 → 0.6667 → **66.7 per cent more**. 20 back to 12: change 8, over 20 → 0.4 → **40 per cent less**. Neither belongs in a note on its own. I would write "12 grams against 20 grams" and give the percentage only with the direction and the base named — "the lower primary standard is 40 per cent below the upper primary standard" — because the absolute figures are small, memorable and not open to being read from the wrong end.

**E2.** Fifty per cent is a statement about the size of the change relative to where it started, and it tells you nothing about where it started. If the share went from two in a hundred to three in a hundred, that is a rise of fifty per cent and also a rise of one person in a hundred — a smaller story than it sounds. It can also be a larger one: one in a hundred across a population of a crore is a lakh of additional people, and nothing in the percentage tells you which case you are in. I would ask for the two underlying percentages and the two headcounts before saying anything. One sentence to print: *"The share rose from two per cent to three per cent — one percentage point, or a rise of fifty per cent on the earlier figure — which in this district means about [N] more people."*

## Practice

**P01 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **that a percentage may exceed a hundred and may itself be fractional.** 7% = 0.07 = 7/100. 40% = 0.40 = 40/100. 125% = 1.25 = 125/100. 0.5% = 0.005 = 0.5/100 (or 1/200). The last two strain the definitions given: A2 defined a numerator as a count of parts, and A4 defines a percentage as a fraction over a hundred, which leaves "a numerator of 0.5" and "more parts than the whole was cut into" unexplained.

**P02 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **taking a percentage of a quantity, and expressing one quantity as a percentage of another.** Neither is demonstrated in the section. 0.35 × 840 = **294**. 840 ÷ 1,200 = 0.7 → **70 per cent**.

**P03 — worked.** 80 to 92: 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15 → 15 per cent. 92 back to 80: 12 ÷ 92 = 0.1304 → 13.0 per cent.

**P04 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **percentage of a quantity, as in P02.** 0.75 × 18,00,000 = **13,50,000** rural (13.5 lakh). 0.50 × 6,00,000 = **3,00,000** urban (3 lakh).

**P05 — worked.** 500 to 800: 300 ÷ 500 = 0.6 → 60 per cent. 800 back to 500: 300 ÷ 800 = 0.375 → 37.5 per cent.

**P06 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **percentage of a quantity for the second half.** 0.20 × 0.45 = 0.09 → **9 per cent of all respondents**. 0.09 × 2,400 = **216 people**.

**P07 — worked.** The break is at the last line. 6 minus 4 is two *percentage points*, not 2 per cent. As a per cent the rise is 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5, a rise of **50 per cent**. The section states the distinction outright and demonstrates it on the same shape of numbers.

**P08 — worked.** The break is the last line, and more precisely the assumption that the two 20 per cents are the same quantity. The cut is 20 per cent of 100, which is 20, leaving 80. The rise is 20 per cent of 80, which is 16, giving **96**. They do not cancel because the second percentage sits on a smaller base.

**P09 — worked.** What to compute: I cannot compute anything until I have the two underlying percentages and the two headcounts. Sixty per cent is consistent with 5 per cent rising to 8 per cent (three percentage points) and equally with 30 per cent rising to 48 per cent (eighteen points). So: get both end values, compute the percentage-point change, and compute the number of women behind each. What the answer does not establish: whether the definition of overweight or the measurement method changed between the two surveys; whether the same women were measured; whether the district's population or its age structure changed; whether the two samples are comparable; or what caused any of it.

**P10 — worked.** What to compute: check that the two sentences describe the same change — 12 minus 8 is four percentage points, and 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5, so 50 per cent. They do. Then compute the headcounts: 8 per cent and 12 per cent of how many people. The second sentence goes in the brief, because it carries the two levels and the base; the first is true and unreadable without them. What that does not establish: whether the rise is real rather than a change in how coverage was recorded, whether "coverage" means the same thing in both years, who the remaining 88 per cent are, or whether coverage translated into anything reaching anyone.

**A4: worked 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10; guessed 1, 2, 4, 6**

## What A4 taught me that changes what I would do

- Percentage points subtract; per cent divides. Whichever is quoted at me, ask for the other.
- For any percentage: per cent of what, and how many people is that.
- A rise and the fall that undoes it are different percentages, because the starting value sits underneath — so always say which end the change was measured from.
- A percentage of a percentage multiplies and is therefore always smaller than either.
- In a committee the relative change will be offered when it is the larger number and the absolute when it is the smaller; bring both myself.
- Do not react to a percentage a journalist reads down the phone. Ask what it is a percentage of and what the two counts were.

## Gaps

- **Taking a percentage of a quantity is never performed.** The only multiplication in the section is a percentage of a percentage: *"0.40 times 0.30 = 0.12, so 12 per cent of all schoolchildren."* Both factors are percentages, and the product is reported as a percentage. Turning a percentage into a count of things is the single most common use of a percentage and it appears nowhere. **Blocks P02 (level 2), P04 (level 4) and the second half of P06 (level 6)**, and is needed for P08 and for E2's printable sentence.
- **Expressing one quantity as a percentage of another is never performed either.** The section gives percentage *change* (change over starting value) and nothing else. **P02 (level 2)** asks for 840 as a percentage of 1,200, which is a different operation, and the reader has to build it by analogy.
- **Percentages above 100 and below 1 are never mentioned.** *"A percentage is a fraction whose denominator is one hundred."* Under A2's definition of a numerator as a count of parts of the whole, neither 125 per cent nor 0.5 per cent can be written. **Hits P01 (level 1)** on two of its four items.
- **The two invented percentages are flagged as invented and then reasoned from anyway.** *"Those two percentages are made up, to show the move."* Good practice — but the section never returns to say what you would have to know to use real ones, and the must-know list does not carry the point.
- **Read twice — "Go back to the two original numbers, or say in your own writing that you could not."** This is the closing sentence of "Where this picture breaks" and it is a fragment; there is no preceding clause it completes. I read the paragraph three times looking for the missing half.
- **"The hard half is what the hundred was."** This is the section's best line and it is never cashed out. No worked example in the section ever misidentifies the hundred and gets caught — the misidentification examples are all about which end a change was measured from, which is a different error.
- **No must-know point covers the percentage-of-a-percentage warning that it only applies when the second percentage is of the first group.** The illustration is careful — *"Of those who do"* — but the rule as stated in the must-knows is just "a percentage of a percentage multiplies", which would also authorise multiplying two unrelated percentages.

# A5 · Ratios, rates and proportions

## Exercises

**E1.** Priority: 1,200 households × 5 people = 6,000 people; × 5 kg = **30,000 kg per month**. Antyodaya: 300 households × 35 kg = **10,500 kg per month**. Block total **40,500 kg per month**. Per household per month: priority 30,000 ÷ 1,200 = **25 kg**; Antyodaya **35 kg**. Antyodaya is higher on that denominator — but only because the priority households average five people; at seven they would be equal, and above seven priority would be higher.

**E2.** The missing step is that offtake is not coverage. Tonnes lifted and people covered are different quantities with different denominators, and the 11 per cent is a percentage change in tonnage being reported as a percentage change in something else. More grain could mean more households lifting, the same households lifting more, larger households, or a change in the entitled list. I would ask for: the number of households entitled and the number who lifted, in both months; and what the presentation means by "coverage" — over what denominator.

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** 18 to 24 ÷6 → 3 to 4. 45 to 15 ÷15 → 3 to 1. 200 to 500 ÷100 → 2 to 5.

**P02 — worked.** 90 lift, so 250 − 90 = 160 do not. Ratio of lifting to not lifting 90 to 160 → ÷10 → 9 to 16. Proportion of all households 90 ÷ 250 = 9/25 = **0.36**.

**P03 — worked.** 15 per 1,000 per year × 100 = **1,500 per 100,000 per year**. 15 ÷ 1,000 = **0.015 per person per year**.

**P04 — worked.** 4 × 5 = **20 kg per household per month**; × 12 = **240 kg per household per year**. Denominators: kilograms per household per month, and kilograms per household per year.

**P05 — worked.** 35 ÷ 5 = **7 people**. At a household of seven the two are identical; below it the Antyodaya household does better per person, above it worse.

**P06 — worked.** Block A: 48 ÷ 3,200 = 0.015 → **15 per 1,000 per year**. Block B: 160 ÷ 12,500 = 0.0128 → **12.8 per 1,000 per year**. A is higher, although B has more events in absolute terms — which is the point.

**P07 — worked.** The break is the comparison itself: the two rates have different denominators. Put both on 100,000: block A is 1,500 per 100,000, block B is 40 per 100,000. **A is nearly forty times worse**, the opposite of what was concluded.

**P08 — worked.** The first three lines are sound (2 kg per person per week, four weeks, 8 kg per person per month, with "a month is 4 weeks" an approximation worth marking). The break is the last line: the denominator was switched from per person to per household without multiplying by the five people. It should be 8 × 5 = **40 kg per household per month**.

**P09 — worked.** Compute: 640 − 590 = 50; 50 ÷ 590 = 0.0847, so about **8.5 per cent** — the percentage change in tonnage is roughly right. But the sentence says coverage, and tonnage has no people in its denominator. What to compute instead: the number of entitled households and the number who lifted, in each month, and coverage as one over the other. What the answer does not establish: how many people were reached, whether the same households lifted more or more households lifted, whether the entitled list changed size between the months, whether the grain reached anyone after it was issued, or whether a single person's consumption changed.

**P10 — worked.** Compute: rate × population for each district, to get the number of cases in each. Twice the rate on half the population is the same number of cases; twice the rate on the same population is twice the cases. Until I have both denominators I cannot say which. What the answer does not establish: what resources a single case actually requires; what resources each district already has; whether the two rates were measured the same way, over the same period, with the same case definition; how the cases are distributed within each district — the section's own warning that one rate can hide blocks differing several times over; or whether resources should be allocated in proportion to cases at all rather than to need, cost or capacity.

**A5: worked 1-10**

## What A5 taught me that changes what I would do

- Two rates with different denominators cannot be compared, and dividing one by the other will still produce a number.
- Say the denominator out loud before using any rate: per what, and over how long. Missing either half means I do not have a quantity.
- Read the whole provision including the proviso before taking a number out of it, because Indian scheme figures move between per person, per household and per beneficiary without warning.
- When two schemes are stated on different denominators, find the point at which they are equal and quote that point.
- Converting a per-household figure to a per-person one using an average household size assumes every household is average.
- Refuse to answer "which programme delivers more" with the larger number; ask what each is divided by.
- A rate per thousand says nothing about which thousand.

# A6 · Powers, roots and scientific notation

## Exercises

**E1.** One lakh = 1 × 10^5. Thirty-five thousand = 3.5 × 10^4. Ten lakh = 1 × 10^6. One crore = 1 × 10^7. One thousandth = 1 × 10^-3. Lakh in a crore: 10^7 ÷ 10^5 = 10^(7−5) = 10^2 = **100**. Thousand in a million: 10^6 ÷ 10^3 = 10^3 = **1,000**.

**E2.** Step one: 2.4E+05 = 2.4 × 10^5 = 240,000, and one lakh is 10^5, so "about 2.4 lakh" is **right**. Step two: the note gives no multiplier. If annual means 365 days, 2.4 × 10^5 × 365 = 8.76 × 10^7, so 8.8E+07 is **right**. Step three: one crore is 10^7, so 8.8 × 10^7 is **8.8 crore** — also **right**. Every arithmetic step checks. What is not right is the hidden assumption: the annual figure assumes production on all 365 days of the year, which the note never states and which no plant does.

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** 10^2 = 100. 10^5 = 100,000 (1,00,000). 10^0 = 1. 10^-2 = 0.01.

**P02 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **arithmetic with negative numbers.** 10^4 × 10^3 = 10^7. 10^8 ÷ 10^5 = 10^3. 10^3 × 10^-1 = 10^(3 + −1) = 10^2. The section demonstrates 5 + 2 and 7 − 5 and never adds a negative exponent to a positive one.

**P03 — worked.** 6,200 = 6.2 × 10^3. 93,00,000 = 9,300,000 = 9.3 × 10^6. 0.0047 = 4.7 × 10^-3. 1,00,00,000 = 10,000,000 = 1 × 10^7.

**P04 — worked.** One crore = 1 × 10^7; ten crore = 1 × 10^8. From the exponents alone, 8 − 7 = 1, so 10^1 = **ten times larger**.

**P05 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **multiplying the mantissas of two numbers in scientific notation.** 450 = 4.5 × 10^2. 1.8 × 4.5 = 8.1; 10^5 × 10^2 = 10^7. Answer **8.1 × 10^7 calories**.

**P06 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **mantissa multiplication again, plus adding a negative exponent.** 2.5E+04 = 25,000; 3.0E-02 = 0.03. 2.5 × 3.0 = 7.5; 10^4 × 10^-2 = 10^2. Answer **7.5 × 10^2**, that is 750. (Checked the long way: 25,000 × 0.03 = 750.)

**P07 — worked.** The break is the second line: when you multiply powers of the same base you **add** the exponents, you do not multiply them. 10^6 × 10^3 = 10^9, not 10^18.

**P08 — worked with a guess.** Supplied: **that the ordering of powers of ten continues downward through zero into negative exponents.** The section's ladder starts at 10^1 and rises; nothing shows that 10^-3 sits above 10^-6. The broken step is the third line's reasoning and its conclusion: 10^-3 is 0.001 and 10^-6 is 0.000001, so 10^-3 is the **larger**, and 5 × 10^-3 is therefore the larger concentration, not the smaller. The premise "−3 is greater than −6" is true; the inference drawn from it is backwards.

**P09 — worked.** 1.2E+06 = 1,200,000 tonnes a year. Three lakh a quarter is 3 × 10^5 × 4 = 1.2 × 10^6 a year. The two match exactly, so the quarterly figure is simply the annual figure divided by four — it says nothing new. What it does not establish: that 3 lakh a quarter is anywhere near capacity. No capacity figure appears anywhere in the sentence, so "close to capacity" has nothing under it. It also does not establish that production was even across the four quarters, or that a tonne of output means the same thing in each.

**P10 — worked.** 2 crore = 2 × 10^7 people. One rupee each is 2 × 10^7 rupees, which is **2 crore rupees, not 2 lakh** — the speaker has dropped two powers of ten by treating crore and lakh as the same rung. The cost is a hundred times what was claimed. What it does not establish: whether all 2 crore are genuinely reached, whether a rupee a head buys anything, or whether the delivery cost is inside or outside that figure.

**A6: worked 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 10; guessed 2, 5, 6, 8**

## What A6 taught me that changes what I would do

- A negative exponent does not make a number negative; it makes it small.
- Whenever a figure contains an E or a raised number, write it out in full before using it.
- The Indian ladder steps by two powers of ten (lakh 10^5, crore 10^7); the international by three (million 10^6, billion 10^9).
- Write any large figure as a digit or two times a power of ten before comparing it with another.
- Checking a colleague's calculation, check the exponents first and the digits second.
- "The exponent is the number of zeros" is true of powers of ten and of nothing else.

- **Roots are in the title and nowhere in the section.** *"A6 · Powers, roots and scientific notation."* The word "root" does not appear again in the file. No definition, no example, no exercise, no practice problem. Nothing was blocked by it — but a reader who arrives at A7 expecting to have met roots has not.

# A7 · Logarithms

## Exercises

**E1.** A straight line at a constant angle on an axis marked 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 with evenly spaced marks means the quantity is **multiplying by the same factor in each equal interval of time** — not increasing by the same amount. Equal distances up that page are equal multiples. On an ordinary scale the same data would not be a straight line at all: it would be almost flat near the bottom left, then bend upward and climb ever more steeply, with the early years indistinguishable from zero.

**E2.** Largest 10,00,000, smallest 25,000. By division: 1,000,000 ÷ 25,000 = **40 times larger**. By subtracting logarithms: 6.0 − 4.4 = 1.6 — and here I stop. Turning 1.6 back into a factor requires raising ten to a power that is not a whole number, and nothing in A6 or A7 defines what that means or how to do it. The section asserts the answer (*"1.6 powers of ten is a factor of about forty"*) without showing the step. The logarithm of ten crore: ten crore is 10^8, so its logarithm is **8**.

## Practice

**P01 — worked.** log 100 = 2. log 10,000 = 4. log 1 = 0. log 10^9 = 9.

**P02 — worked.** 3 → 1,000. 6 → 1,000,000. 0 → 1. −2 → 0.01.

**P03 — worked.** 4,800 lies between 1,000 (10^3) and 10,000 (10^4), so its logarithm lies **between 3 and 4**. It is closer to **4**, because closeness on this scale is a matter of multiplication, not of subtraction: 4,800 is 4.8 times 1,000 but 10,000 is only 2.1 times 4,800, so the shorter step is the one up to 10,000. Worth flagging that the obvious method gives the wrong answer here — by subtraction, 4,800 is 3,800 above 1,000 and 5,200 below 10,000, which would say "closer to 3".

**P04 — worked.** Ten lakh = 10^6 → **6**. One crore = 10^7 → **7**. Ten crore = 10^8 → **8**.

**P05 — could not attempt.** I can get as far as the subtraction: log 300,000 = 5.48, log 25,000 = 4.40, difference **1.08**. I cannot carry out the step the problem requires next, which is turning 1.08 back into a multiplying factor. That needs ten raised to a fractional power, and A6 defines a power as *"repeated multiplication of one number by itself... the exponent, which counts how many copies of the base are multiplied together"* — a count of copies, which excludes 10^1.08 entirely. A7 does not extend the definition, does not name an inverse operation, and does not say which calculator key performs it. (By the route the problem forbids, 300,000 ÷ 25,000 = 12.)

**P06 — worked.** 30,000 ÷ 300 = 100 = 10^2, so the two points are **2 apart in logarithms** and one value is **100 times** the other. (This one comes out whole, so no antilogarithm is needed.)

**P07 — worked.** The break is at the third line, and it is the rule being applied to the wrong operation. Adding logarithms corresponds to **multiplying** the numbers, not adding them: 10^4.8 is about 63,000, which is 200 × 300, not 200 + 300. The answer to 200 + 300 is 500, and logarithms are no help in getting it.

**P08 — worked.** "Steady" is wrong because one mark on this axis is a multiplication by ten, not an addition of a fixed amount. If the line rose one mark in each five-year period, the quantity was multiplied by ten and then by ten again — so the increase in the second period was ten times larger in absolute terms than in the first. It was steady only in the sense of a constant rate of multiplication, which is exactly the sense a reader of the word "steady" will not take.

**P09 — worked.** What to work out: how many observations are in the dataset against how many appear on the figure, and what values the missing ones take — in particular whether any are zero or below, since a logarithmic axis cannot show either and those observations have not been dropped for a stated reason. Also what the axis minimum is, and what share of the data falls under it. What the figure cannot establish: anything at all about the distribution below the minimum; whether the omitted values are a handful or most of the data; whether zeros exist and what they would mean; and — because a log axis squashes differences among the large values — how big the differences at the top of the chart actually are.

**P10 — worked.** What to work out: the actual numbers behind both charts — the starting value, the ending value, the difference between them, and the ratio between them. Both charts are honest; they answer different questions. If the briefing is about how large the change is, the ordinary axis is the right one, because a log axis makes a large difference look modest and would be working against the argument. If the briefing is about a range spanning several powers of ten, or about a rate of growth, the log axis is right. What that does not settle: whether the change is real rather than an artefact of measurement; whether a difference of that size matters to anyone; what the audience will take away from either picture; or which of the two questions the briefing is actually for — which is a judgement about the decision being made, not about the data.

**A7: worked 1-4, 6-10; could not attempt 5**

## What A7 taught me that changes what I would do

- A logarithm is an exponent: the power to which ten must be raised to give the number.
- On a logarithmic axis equal distances are equal multiples, not equal amounts.
- Before reading any figure, look at the axis and decide which kind it is — 1, 10, 100 is multiplication; 1, 2, 3 is addition.
- A log axis cannot show zero or anything below it, so ask what happened to those observations.
- A log axis makes a large difference look modest; if the argument is about the size of an effect rather than its existence, that axis is against me.
- Steps of one lakh, ten lakh, one crore are a log scale wearing words. Read the steps, not the words.

A6 defined an exponent as a count of copies of the base being multiplied — under which 10^1.6 means nothing. A7 neither extends that definition nor names the operation nor says how to perform it. **This is the one outright could-not-attempt in eighty problems: P05 (level 5). It also makes the second half of E2 unanswerable, and it makes the section's own headline finding ("about forty times") unverifiable by the reader.**

# A8 · Orders of magnitude and the back-of-envelope check

## Exercises

**E1.** 2 crore meals = 2 × 10^7. At 450 calories, rounded to 5 × 10^2:
`2 × 10^7 × 5 × 10^2 = 10 × 10^9 = 10^10 calories`.
A person eats about 2 × 10^3 a day (**a guess**) over about 4 × 10^2 days (365 rounded), so about 10^6 calories a year. 10^10 ÷ 10^6 = **10^4** — about ten thousand people's annual eating.
Is 2 crore meals possible for a state? At about 200 school days a year (**a guess**), 2 × 10^7 ÷ 2 × 10^2 = 10^5, so **about a lakh children**. Held against a state, that is low — the section's own illustration guesses a *district* at a lakh children. So either the figure covers only part of the state's schools, or it is a district figure mislabelled, or the meals are not daily. It is possible, but it implies a programme about the size of one district.
Assumptions, all marked: 2,000 calories a day per person; 365 days rounded to 400; 200 school days; and a state having many times a district's children — which I have no figure for from any of these eight files.

**E2.** 40 crore = 4 × 10^8 rupees. 12 crore = 1.2 × 10^8 children. 4 × 10^8 ÷ 1.2 × 10^8 ≈ **3 rupees per child per year**. What the check establishes: that the two figures in the sentence cannot both be right as described. ## What A8 taught me that changes what I would do

- An order-of-magnitude estimate is not a rough version of the right answer; it is a tool for deciding whether someone else's number is possible.
- Round every input to one digit and a power of ten, do the arithmetic on the exponents, and claim the answer only to the nearest power of ten.
- The comparison against a quantity whose size I already know is the step that does the work — an estimate that stops before it is not an estimate.
- Write every number I supplied myself into the working and label it a guess, where someone can argue with it.
- The method catches a factor of ten and never a factor of two, so it can never be used to argue that an effect is big enough to matter.
- Comparing against another estimate gives two guesses agreeing with each other, which feels like confirmation and is not.
- Asked to check a calculation under time pressure, do not redo it — estimate it independently and see whether the two land on the same power of ten.

## Gaps

- **The method's load-bearing step requires a stock of known quantities that the course never supplies.** *"The method only works if the thing you compare against is one you actually know."* The eight files give the reader almost nothing to know: no population of India, no population of a typical state or district, no price of anything, no typical household size other than the ones inside individual problems. The illustration slips one in — *"Hold that against a district population of a few lakh"* — in passing, unmarked, in the middle of a different point. **This is what stopped me on P09 (level 9) and P10 (level 10), the two problems where the comparison is the whole task, and it weakened E1 and P05.** The section teaches the procedure and withholds the reference points that make the procedure work.
- **A tonne is never defined in any of the eight files**, although tonnes appear in A5-E2, A5-P09 and are required as an output by **A8-P06 (level 6)**.
- **The mixed-rounding case is never handled.** *"Round three numbers down and your answer is low by a factor of two or three before you start."* **P02 (level 2)** asks whether an estimate is above or below when one input was rounded down and one up, which needs the relative size of each rounding — a step the section never performs.
- **The definition of an order of magnitude and the procedure for estimating disagree, and the text slides between them.** *"The order of magnitude of a quantity is the power of ten nearest to it"* — by which 450 would be 10^3, since 450 is 4.5 times 100 but only 2.2 times below 1,000. The illustration instead writes *"450 calories, to one digit: 5 times 10^2"*. Both are used in the section and they are different operations. **P01 (level 1)** asks for one and **P03 (level 3)** for the other, without the difference ever being named.
- **P04 asks the reader to "mark every input you were not given" and then gives every input.** Either the problem is a null case or the intended answer is the attendance assumption hidden in "receives", which nothing in the wording signals.
- **"Is this number possible" is the section's stated question and it is never answered in the negative on a real case.** The illustration's error check is a typo the estimator himself makes; the two exercises and P09 and P10 all ask the reader to deliver a verdict on someone else's figure, which the section never models start to finish.
- **Read twice — "Somebody asks you, in a meeting, roughly how much food energy a district's school meal programme puts out in a year. Do it anyway, on paper, in front of them."** "Anyway" answers an objection that has not been raised. The sentence that presumably said "you will want to say you cannot" is missing, and the paragraph reads as though it starts halfway through.

## Which file left me least able to do what it asked

**A3.** Seven of ten problems needed something the section did not give, and the failures are spread across the whole ladder rather than bunched: level 2 (counting significant figures, where the section gives a starting rule and no stopping rule), level 3 (rounding to significant figures, an operation the section never performs), levels 5 and 6 (the same trailing-zero hole, plus a contradiction between "Act figures are exact" and "no more significant figures than your weakest input"), and then the entire top band — levels 8, 9 and 10 — all three of which require percentages that are not defined until A4.

A3 is also the file where the failure is most invisible. Nothing in it reads as hard. The definition of a significant figure is a single clean sentence, and it is the omission inside that sentence that takes out four problems.

**Runner-up: A2**, which is worse in kind if not in count. Its must-know list tells the reader to compare and add fractions and the section does neither, anywhere, once. Five of ten problems depend on that missing demonstration, including P07 — a find-the-broken-step problem about an addition whose correct procedure has never appeared.

**A7-P05 is the only outright blockage in eighty problems**, and it is a clean one: the section asserts that a difference of 1.6 in logarithms is a factor of forty and never gives the operation that turns the one into the other — an operation A6's own definition of a power positively excludes.

## The gap I would fix first

**A2: add four lines showing two fractions put over a common denominator and added.**

Not because A2 is the worst file, but because this is the cheapest fix with the largest reach. It is one short worked example — pick a denominator, multiply each fraction up to it, add the numerators, leave the denominator alone. That single insertion:

- unlocks A2-P02, P03, P07, P09 and P10 — half the practice set, spanning levels 2, 3, 7, 9 and 10;
- removes the contradiction of a find-the-broken-step problem (P07) about a procedure the reader has never been shown;
- and repairs a prerequisite the rest of the course leans on. A4 defines a percentage as a fraction over a hundred and asks the reader to compare percentages; A5 defines a proportion as a part over a whole; A7-P03 asks which of two logarithms a number is nearer to. All of them assume the reader can put two fractions on a common footing, and none of them teaches it either.

Second in line, and nearly as cheap: **give A3 a rule for where the significant-figure count stops** (one sentence on trailing zeros, with 450 and 1,200 as the examples), and **move A4 before A3** — or strip the percentages out of A3-P08, P09 and P10. Those two changes between them recover six more problems.

Third: **give A8 its reference quantities.** The section teaches a method whose final step is "compare against something you already know" and then never tells the reader anything they could know. A short table — population of India, of a large state, of a typical district, a household size, a daily calorie intake, a few prices — would turn A8-P09 and P10 from guesswork into the exercise they are meant to be, and would retrospectively make A1-P10, A6-P10 and A5-P10 sharper too.
