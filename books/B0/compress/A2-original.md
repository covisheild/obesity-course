### A2 · Fractions

*B0-R0-C02 · derivable*

**Definition.** A fraction is a division that has not yet been carried out. The number below the line, the
denominator, states how many equal parts the whole was cut into. The number above the line, the
numerator, states how many of those parts are being counted.

Multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same amount, or dividing both by the same
amount, leaves the value of the fraction unchanged. Two fractions written differently can
therefore be one number. 45/70 and 9/14 are.

Two fractions may be compared or added only once they stand over the same denominator, because
a part is defined by the whole it was cut from. The same fraction of two different wholes is, in
general, two different amounts.

**In plain terms.** A fraction is a sum you have not done yet. Write nine fourteenths and you are saying: take nine,
divide it by fourteen, and leave the dividing until later.

Two numbers, two different jobs. The bottom one — the denominator — says how many equal parts the
whole was cut into. The top one — the numerator — says how many of those parts you are holding.

Cut a roti into fourteen equal pieces and take nine of them. That is nine fourteenths. Now cut the
same roti into seventy pieces and take forty-five. You are holding exactly the same amount of
roti. Both numbers in the second fraction are five times the numbers in the first, so the two
fractions are one number written twice.

That gives you the only move fractions really need. Multiply the top and the bottom by the same
thing, or divide both by the same thing, and the value stays put. Change one of them on its own
and you have a different number.

Now the part that outlives this section. A fraction is always a fraction of something. Half a roti
and half a district are both one half, and they are nothing like the same amount. So before you
compare two fractions, or add them, check that they were cut from the same whole. If they were
not, the arithmetic still works and the answer still means nothing.

**Illustration.** Open `sources/nfsa_2013.txt`, which is the National Food Security Act, 2013. Search inside it
for the words NUTRITIONAL STANDARDS. Two hits this time, and both are in the same place: a table
near the back of the Act. It is the second schedule, and it sets what a meal under these schemes
has to deliver.

Read two of its rows. The standard for a lower primary hot cooked meal is 450 calories and 12
grams of protein. The standard for an upper primary one is 700 calories and 20 grams. The Act
sets what a meal has to deliver. It does not say what any child receives.

A word about those labels before the arithmetic. Calories count food energy and grams count
weight. This section does not need you to know what a calorie is. It needs you to do the
arithmetic and keep the label attached to the answer. A number with its label stripped off is
not a number any more.

Now a question you could be asked in a meeting. What fraction of the upper primary energy
standard is the lower primary one?

Write it as a fraction, energy over energy, and work it down.

```
    450 over 700
    = 45 over 70        (both ended in a zero, so divide both by ten)
    = 9 over 14         (both are in the five times table, so divide both by five)
```

Check it backwards. Nine times five is forty-five. Fourteen times five is seventy. Nothing
changed except the way it is written.

Now the mistake, and it is a bad one. Somebody hears that and says: a lower primary child gets
nine fourteenths of what a child needs.

They have swapped the whole. Nine fourteenths is the lower primary standard as a fraction of the
upper primary standard. Both are standards for one school meal. Neither is what a child needs in
a day, and those two rows of the Act say nothing whatever about a day's requirement. The
fraction is right. The sentence built on it is not.

Do the protein column yourself now, the same way. Twelve over twenty. Divide both by four and
you have three fifths.

Compare the two answers. Nine fourteenths is a little under two thirds. Three fifths is a little
over half. The two columns do not shrink by the same fraction. Whoever set these standards did
not simply scale one meal down to make the other.

**Where this picture breaks.** Cutting a roti makes the parts look obviously equal, and that is the part of the picture to
distrust. Fractions only behave if the parts are equal. Cut the roti carelessly and "three
pieces out of four" is not three quarters of anything.

The bigger break is the one the mistake above turned on. This section tells you how to handle a
fraction once you have one. It cannot tell you what the whole was. That never comes from the
arithmetic. It comes from the document, the table heading or the person who handed you the
number. If none of them says, you do not have a fraction yet.

One more. Nine fourteenths of a meal standard is a clean, exact fraction. Both numbers came from
a schedule of an Act, and are exact by law. Do the same division on two measured
quantities and the neat answer is an illusion. That is the next section.

**Must know points for you.**

- A fraction on its own is not a finding. Nine fourteenths is only a fact once you can say what the fourteen was. Two fractions cut from different wholes can be compared arithmetically and the comparison means nothing, which is why the error survives review.

- To simplify a fraction, divide the top and the bottom by the same number. To compare or add two, put them over the same denominator first. Change one number on its own and you have changed the fraction.

- Indian schemes set different standards for different groups: lower primary and upper primary, children and pregnant women, and more. So "a fraction of the standard" is never enough. Name which standard, and name the group it was written for.

- Asked in public what fraction of a child's need a meal covers, do not answer with the fraction you happen to have. Say what your fraction is a fraction of, and say plainly if the document in front of you does not carry a daily requirement.

- In a committee room, a fraction with its denominator left out does work that an argument could not do on its own. Ask for the denominator every time, of your own numbers first. It is a question nobody can take offence at.

- This gets you the value of a fraction and no further. It cannot tell you whether the two things you are comparing belong in the same sentence. Nor can it tell you whether the numbers underneath were measured well.
