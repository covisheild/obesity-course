## E1 · Atoms, molecules and bonds

### Exercise answers

**Ex 1.** Breaking a bond takes energy in. Making a bond gives energy out. The rule has three steps:
1. Add up the energy needed to break every bond in the starting materials.
2. Add up the energy given out by making every bond in the products.
3. Take the second total from the first.

A negative answer means more came out than went in, so heat is given out (exothermic). A positive answer means heat is taken in (endothermic).

**Ex 2.** The brochure says a strong bond "locks in" more energy. That is wrong: "strong" only means the bond takes more energy to break. Any energy you get depends on the difference between the bonds broken and the bonds made. On that point the brochure has it backwards, because strong bonds in the starting material make that difference smaller. What I would ask for:
- What is broken in the body, and what is made?
- What are the bond energies on both sides?
- Is the drink changed at all, or does it pass through unchanged? If it passes through unchanged, it supplies nothing (must-know, line 134).

**Ex 3.** The energy is not inside the food. It sits in the arrangement of the atoms, meaning which atom is bonded to which. It only shows up when the arrangement changes: break the bonds, make new ones, subtract. For a substance you have not met, ask what is broken and what is made. A figure that gives only one side is half a sum.

I could not show the trainee how to count the bonds for a new substance. See gap 6.

### What it taught me that changes what I do, say, accept or refuse
- I refuse "strong bond = more energy stored".
- I refuse any claim that a food's energy can be released without changing its molecules.
- Asked "what does it release?", I ask what is broken and what is made.
- I will not read a bond energy as saying whether a reaction happens, or how fast it goes.
- A substance that goes in and comes out unchanged supplied nothing.

## E2 · Chemical energy, combustion, and the bomb calorimeter

### Exercise answers

**Ex 1.** There are two steps.
1. **Calibrate.** Burn a substance whose energy is known and measure the temperature rise. Energy equivalent = known energy ÷ rise, in joules per degree. This needs a sample of known energy, and it is repeated before each set of runs.
2. **Measure.** Burn the unknown sample. Energy released = rise × energy equivalent. This needs the rise and the same calorimeter, calibrated that day.

The cascade: gross (ingested) energy, then metabolizable energy, then net metabolizable energy. The losses I can name between gross and metabolizable energy are:
- unabsorbed food in the faeces;
- gas from fermentation in the large intestine;
- urine, mostly the waste left over from protein;
- "a small amount … lost from the body surface". This one is named only in the Definition and never explained.

I cannot name the losses between metabolizable and net metabolizable energy. The text never gives them (gap 11).

## E3 · Conservation of energy, and a system with a boundary

## E5 · The cell, membranes, proteins and receptors

**Ex 1.**

## E6 · Metabolism as chemistry happening in a body

### Exercise answers

**Ex 1.**
- **Rice:** the starch is cut in the gut into single sugars, glucose among them (chewing is never described). Glucose crosses the gut wall into the blood, and the blood goes to the liver. - **Oil:** triglyceride is broken into pieces (fatty acids and glycerol), which cross the gut wall and are rebuilt into triglyceride on the far side. From there I had to stitch lines 55–57 together: it is stored in adipose tissue until needed, or pulled apart and burned like any other fuel. The oil walk-through at line 89 stops at the rebuilding.

**True of both:** each is cut small before it can cross, and ends up either used (ATP plus heat) or stored. **Different:** glucose crosses as it is and has a near store (glycogen). Fat is taken apart, crosses, is rebuilt, and has only the far store.

## E8 · Organs and systems that matter for metabolism

### Exercise answers

**Ex 1.** The six organs and tissues:
- **Gut:** cuts food small and lets it through the wall.
- **Liver:** first stop for everything water-soluble. Stores glucose as glycogen and releases it under glucagon, makes new glucose from amino acids, and makes bile.
- **Pancreas:** sends enzyme juice to the gut, and puts insulin (from beta cells) and glucagon (from alpha cells) into the blood.
- **Adipose tissue:** stores triglyceride and makes its own hormones.
- **Muscle:** takes up glucose under insulin and stores glycogen.
- **Brain:** uses only glucose, and takes it without insulin.

A hormone is a chemical made in a gland and put into the blood. It goes everywhere and acts only where a cell carries its receptor.

**Ex 2.** Taking the leaflet sentence by sentence:
- "Simply storage bags" is wrong: adipose tissue makes hormones, so it is an organ.
- "Once they are full, the body has nowhere…": **cannot be judged from the text.** Nothing says whether fat cells have a capacity or can multiply.
- "Stop filling them": filling and emptying are both the mechanism working, and the text gives no sizes.

In its place: "Fat tissue stores energy and also sends out hormones; storing is its job, not a failure."

**Ex 3.** Taught: insulin is released when blood sugar is high, and acts mainly on muscle and fat, which bring glucose carriers to their surface. Glucagon is released when blood sugar is low, and acts on the liver: glycogen back to glucose, and new glucose from amino acids. The brain, red blood cells, the kidneys and the gut lining take glucose without insulin. Always name the tissue.

**I could not state the liver's relation to insulin consistently** (gap 9).

### What it taught me that changes what I do, say, accept or refuse
- I refuse "insulin lets sugar into cells" unless it names the tissue.
- I ask which cells carry the receptor, not where a hormone went.
- I treat a store filling and a store emptying as both normal.
- I refuse "fat is just a storage bag".
- I keep the pancreas's two jobs apart.
- I refuse any quantitative argument built from this section.
