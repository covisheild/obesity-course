# C1 · Variables and algebra: what a letter stands for

**Definition.** A variable is a letter written in place of a number. It stands either for a number that is
not yet known, or for a number that may take any of several values.

Two things written side by side are multiplied. So 2x means 2 times x, and xy means x times
y. A letter multiplied by itself is written as a power: x^2 means x times x.

An expression is a combination of numbers, letters and operations that stands for a number.
An equation is a statement that two expressions stand for the same number.

Brackets group a part of an expression so that it is treated as one quantity.

Substituting a number for a letter replaces that letter, everywhere it appears, with the
number. The expression then becomes arithmetic.

Every rule of arithmetic holds for a letter, because the letter stands for a number. The
order of an addition or a multiplication does not matter, and like terms may be collected.

**In plain terms.** A letter in a sum is a number wearing a name.

You use a letter for one of two reasons. Either you do not know the number yet, and you want to
write about it anyway. Or the number changes from one case to the next, and you want one line
that covers all of them.

Here is the first thing to get right, because it catches almost everybody.

```working
    2x
```

That is not a two sitting beside an x. It is 2 times x. In algebra, writing two things side by
side means multiply them. So 2x is double whatever x turns out to be.

The same holds for two letters. xy means x times y.

A letter multiplied by itself is written with a small raised number, which is the power notation
you met earlier.

```working
    x^2
```

That is x times x.

Now the part that makes everything after this possible. A letter obeys every rule a number
obeys. It has to, because it is a number. You just do not know which one.

So you add letters the way you add numbers.

```working
    x plus x = 2x
```

And you multiply them the way you multiply numbers.

```working
    3 times 2x = 6x
```

Brackets do one job. They say treat what is inside as one quantity.

```working
    2(x + 5)
```

That is 2 times the whole of x + 5. It is not 2 times x with a 5 added on afterwards.

When you do find out what the number is, you put it in. Putting a number in place of a letter is
called substituting.

Say x turns out to be 7.

```working
    2x
    2 times 7 = 14
```

Fourteen. The letter went away and arithmetic was left.

One more pair of words. An expression is a piece of algebra that stands for a number, like
2x + 5. An equation is a statement that two expressions are the same number, like 2x + 5 = 19.
The equals sign is a claim, not a button.

**Illustration.** An Indian food label gets its energy figure by arithmetic, not by burning the food. The Food
Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020 fix the numbers to multiply
by. Four kilocalories for every gram of carbohydrate. Four for every gram of protein. Nine
for every gram of fat.

Take a packet whose panel says this for every 100 grams. Carbohydrate 60 grams, protein 12
grams, fat 20 grams. Those three gram figures are made up for this problem. The three
factors are not.

Here is the working that comes back first. It is wrong.

```working
    60 plus 12 plus 20 = 92
    92 times 4 = 368
```

368 kilocalories. The arithmetic is fine. What is wrong is using one factor for all three
quantities. Fat does not carry four.

Write the rule with letters instead. Let c stand for the number of grams of carbohydrate.
Let p stand for the number of grams of protein. Let f stand for the number of grams of fat.
And let E stand for the energy in kilocalories.

```working
    E = 4c + 4p + 9f
```

Say it out loud before you use it. Four times the carbohydrate grams, plus four times the
protein grams, plus nine times the fat grams.

Read 9f as nine times f. It is not a nine with an f stuck on the end of it. And the f is not
the symbol for gram. It is standing in for a number of grams, which is a different job.

Now substitute. Put 60 where c is, 12 where p is, and 20 where f is.

```working
    4 times 60 = 240
    4 times 12 = 48
    9 times 20 = 180
    240 plus 48 plus 180 = 468
```

468 kilocalories.

Now watch the letters do the thing letters are for. A second packet comes along with 70 grams
of carbohydrate, 5 grams of protein and 15 grams of fat. These are made up too.

```working
    4 times 70 = 280
    4 times 5 = 20
    9 times 15 = 135
    280 plus 20 plus 135 = 435
```

435 kilocalories.

You did not write a new rule for the second packet. You wrote the same rule and put different
numbers into it. That is what varies in a variable.

**Where this picture breaks.** The letters stand for numbers of grams. They do not stand for grams.

The rule gives the figure a label must declare. It says nothing about what the food does in a
person.

And the three factors here cover carbohydrate, protein and fat only. The same clause sets
separate factors for polyols, erythritol, alcohol, organic acid and dietary fibre. A food
holding any of those needs its own term in the line, with its own number in front.

**Must know points for you.**

- Two things written side by side are multiplied. So 2x is two times x. Reading it as a two next to an x is the error that makes every later line unreadable. Say the multiplication out loud the first few times.
- A letter in a formula is a number, not a unit. In a line that ends 9f, the f is a count of grams and not the symbol for gram. Whoever copies that line reading f as a unit will drop the multiplication.
- Substitute, and the algebra turns into arithmetic you can check. When a formula lands in front of you, put in numbers whose answer you already know and see whether it gives that answer back.
- One line with letters in it covers every case at once. So you check a whole column of a spreadsheet by checking the rule once and then testing two rows, rather than by reading the column.
- A letter standing for a number says nothing about which numbers are allowed. The algebra will hand you a negative mass or a fat content larger than the packet without complaining. Read every answer back as a quantity before you use it.
- Name every letter in words when you put a formula on a board, in the same breath as you write it. A trainee who has to guess what p stands for has stopped following you.

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A first-year resident reads 2x as a two and an x written together. They have ten minutes and

Teach them why the two symbols side by side mean a multiplication, so that they can read the
next formula they meet.


**2.** Write each of these as a line of algebra. Use n for the number.

- five times the number
- the number plus seven

**3.** Work each of these out for a equal to 5 and b equal to 2.

**5.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a rule says E = 4c + 4p + 9f
    a panel gives c as 55, p as 9 and f as 18
    substitute the three numbers into the rule
    4c becomes 455
    4p becomes 49
    9f becomes 918
    455 plus 49 plus 918 = 1,422
    so the energy is 1,422 kilocalories
```
