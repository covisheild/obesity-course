# E1 · Atoms, molecules and bonds — enough to see where energy is stored

**Definition.** An atom contains a very small nucleus composed of positively charged protons and uncharged
neutrons, surrounded by a much larger volume of space containing negatively charged
electrons. The electrons occupy almost all of an atom's volume. The diameter of an atom is on
the order of 10^-10 metres, and the diameter of the nucleus is roughly 10^-15 metres.

A chemical bond is an attraction between atoms or ions that holds them together in a molecule
or a crystal. Although many elements consist of discrete, individual atoms, some exist as
molecules made up of two or more atoms of the element chemically bonded together. Compounds
are formed when two or more elements chemically combine, resulting in the formation of bonds.
A molecular formula uses chemical symbols to indicate the types of atoms in a molecule, with
subscripts to show the number of atoms of each type.

Two kinds of bond are enough for this course. Ionic bonding results from the electrostatic
attraction of oppositely charged ions, which are typically produced by the transfer of
electrons between metallic and nonmetallic atoms. A covalent bond results from the mutual
attraction of two atoms for a shared pair of electrons.

Energy is the capacity to supply heat or do work. Potential energy is the energy an object has
because of its relative position, composition or condition. Kinetic energy is the energy an
object possesses because of its motion. The energy held in a food is potential energy of
composition. It is held in which atoms are bonded to which, and not in the atoms themselves.

A bond's strength describes how strongly each atom is joined to another atom, and therefore
how much energy is required to break the bond between the two atoms. Separating any pair of
bonded atoms requires energy. The stronger a bond, the greater the energy required to break
it. Forming a bond releases energy.

A reaction therefore has two energy events, opposite in direction. Breaking every bond in the
starting materials takes energy in. Forming every bond in the products gives energy out. The
net energy change of the reaction is approximately the difference between those two totals.

Where the bonds in the products are stronger than the bonds in the starting materials, the
reaction releases heat and is called exothermic. Where the bonds in the products are weaker,
the reaction absorbs heat and is called endothermic.

**In plain terms.** Everything you eat is made of atoms. So is everything else.

An atom has a tiny heavy centre, called the nucleus, and a cloud of much lighter particles
around it, called electrons. The centre holds two kinds of particle: protons, which carry a
positive charge, and neutrons, which carry none. The electrons carry a negative charge.

Look at the two sizes and you will see what an atom mostly is.

```working
    10^-10 divided by 10^-15 = 10^5
```

The whole atom is about a hundred thousand times wider than its centre. The electrons take up
almost all of that room. An atom is mostly space.

Atoms stick to each other. The sticking has a name: a bond. A bond is an attraction between two
atoms that holds them together.

A group of atoms held together by bonds is a molecule. Sugar is a molecule. Fat is a molecule.
Water is a molecule.

There are two ways atoms stick, and you need to be able to tell them apart later.

In an ionic bond, one atom hands an electron over to the other. One is left positive, the other
negative, and opposite charges pull.

In a covalent bond, the two atoms share a pair of electrons instead of handing them over.
Covalent bonds are the ones this course will keep meeting.

Now the part this section exists for.

Energy is the capacity to supply heat or do work. It comes in two kinds. One kind is energy a
thing has because it is moving. The other kind is energy a thing has because of where it is,
what it is made of, or what state it is in. That second kind is the one you want.

Here is the sentence to carry out of this section. **The energy in a food is held in the
arrangement of its atoms.** It is in which atom is bonded to which, and not in the atoms
themselves.

You can see why from one fact about bonds.

Pulling a bond apart costs energy. Every time. Making a bond gives energy back.

Most people have that the wrong way round, so say it out loud. Breaking costs. Making pays.

A bond that is hard to pull apart is called a strong bond. Strong means it takes a lot of energy
to break, and it means nothing else.

Now put the two halves together, because a reaction does both at once.

When a food burns, old bonds break and new bonds form. Breaking the old ones takes energy in.
Making the new ones gives energy out. The reaction hands you the difference between the two.

If the new bonds are stronger than the old ones, more comes out than went in. That is heat you
can measure, and it is what anyone means by the energy in a food.

If the new bonds are weaker, the sum runs the other way and the reaction takes heat in instead.

There are two words for the two cases, and you will meet them in print. A reaction that gives
heat out is exothermic. A reaction that takes heat in is endothermic.

So the energy in a food is not a stuff sitting in it, waiting to be poured out. It is a
difference between two sums. It shows up only when the arrangement changes.

Now go and find the energy, because the bond is where it is.

Open section 7.5, Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds, at
https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-5-strengths-of-ionic-and-covalent-bonds.
Search inside it for the word Separating.

> Separating any pair of bonded atoms requires energy.

Read that twice. Pulling a bond apart costs you energy. It does not pay you.

Now ask the opposite question, and go and get its answer off the same page. If breaking costs,
does making pay? Search for the word released.

> ...the energy released when all bonds are formed in the products.

There they are. Two energies, opposite in direction, inside one reaction. Energy in to break.
Energy out to form. Whether the reaction gives you anything depends on which of the two is
bigger.

So do that sum, on the book's own case, on paper.

Hydrogen gas is molecules of two hydrogen atoms, joined by one bond each. Chlorine gas is
molecules of two chlorine atoms, joined by one bond each. Put them together and they make
hydrogen chloride, in which each hydrogen atom is joined to one chlorine atom.

Count the bonds with a pencil. Take one hydrogen molecule and one chlorine molecule. Two bonds
break: the hydrogen-to-hydrogen bond, and the chlorine-to-chlorine bond. Two bonds are then
made, each one hydrogen to chlorine.

The page gives you all three numbers, in kilojoules for a fixed standard amount of each.

```table
bond                    energy in kilojoules, per fixed standard amount
hydrogen to hydrogen    436
chlorine to chlorine    243
hydrogen to chlorine    432
```

Read what those figures are quoted for before you use them. The fixed amount is the same one
in all three, which is what lets you add and subtract them freely. None of them is the energy
of a single pair of atoms.

Energy in, to break the two bonds.

```working
    436 plus 243 = 679
```

Energy out, from making the two new bonds.

```working
    432 times 2 = 864
```

Now the difference. In, minus out.

```working
    679 minus 864 = -185
```

The book's own line does the same sum and prints the same figure.

Read the sign before you read the digits. The answer is negative, which means more came out
than went in. One hundred and eighty-five kilojoules left the reaction as heat.

Now turn that one sum into the thing you came for.

A food is molecules. Burning it breaks the bonds inside those molecules, and makes new bonds
with oxygen from the air. The new bonds are stronger than the old ones. So the sum runs the
way it just ran for hydrogen and chlorine, and the difference comes out as heat.

That difference is what anybody means when they say a food contains energy.

Which tells you what the phrase does not mean. There is no energy sitting in the food the way
tea sits in a cup. Nothing can be poured out of it.

What there is, is an arrangement of atoms. There is a second arrangement the same atoms can be
put into. And there is a difference between the two sums. Change the arrangement and you
collect the difference. Leave it alone and there is nothing there to collect.

Here is what you can do with this from today. When somebody tells you that a substance
releases energy, ask two questions. What gets broken, and what gets made. If the answer names
only one of the two, you have been handed half a sum.

**Where this picture breaks.** The three bond energies are quoted for a fixed counted amount of each substance, not for one
pair of atoms. So 185 kilojoules is not what one molecule gives up. No figure here is the
energy of a single bond you could point at.

The sum is approximate and the book says so. It gives the difference as approximately the net
change, not as the net change. Two bonds with the same name in two different molecules are not
quite the same bond, and the tables carry averages.

Hydrogen and chlorine is not a food. A food molecule carries dozens of bonds rather than one,
and the same two-sided sum then runs over all of them at once. Nothing about the shape of the
sum changes. The counting gets much longer.

The sum gives you the size of an energy change and tells you nothing about whether the change
happens, or how fast. A mixture can sit on a shelf for years, unchanged, with a large release
waiting inside it. Something has to start it.

And it tells you nothing about where the energy goes after it leaves. Heat, light, movement:
the sum is silent on all three, and so is this section.

**Must know points for you.**

- Energy is not a substance sitting inside a food. It is held in the arrangement of the atoms, in which atom is bonded to which, and it appears only when that arrangement changes. So refuse any claim that something can release a food's energy without changing what its molecules are.
- A strong bond is one that takes a lot of energy to break, and that is all the word means. It does not mean the bond is holding more energy for you. Energy comes out when strong bonds are made, so a molecule whose bonds are already strong has less to give, not more.
- Every reaction has two energies, opposite in direction: energy in to break bonds, energy out to make them. When someone gives you a figure for what a reaction releases, ask what was broken and what was made. A figure that names only one side of that is half a sum.
- Bond energies give you the size of an energy change and nothing else. They cannot tell you whether the reaction happens, or how fast, or what starts it. So a large calculated release is never on its own evidence that anything will occur in a body, a bottle or a pan.
- Teach the two-sided sum before you teach a single step of metabolism. Break, then make, then subtract. A trainee holding that shape can follow any pathway the first time they see it, instead of memorising which arrows are labelled energy.
- When a product is sold as giving you energy, the question is what it changes into, and where the products of that change go. A substance that goes into a person and comes out unchanged has supplied nothing, whatever the bonds inside it are worth.
