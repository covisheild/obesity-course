# A2 · Fractions

**Definition.** A fraction is a division that has not yet been carried out. The number below the line, the
denominator, states how many equal parts the whole was cut into. The number above the line, the
numerator, states how many of those parts are being counted.

Multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same amount, or dividing both by the same
amount, leaves the value of the fraction unchanged.

**In plain terms.** A fraction is a sum you have not done yet.

Cut a roti into fourteen equal pieces and take nine of them. Now cut the same roti into seventy
pieces and take forty-five. You are holding exactly the same amount of roti. Both numbers in the
second fraction are five times the numbers in the first, so the two fractions are one number
written twice.

Multiply the top and the bottom by the same thing, or divide both by the same thing, and the
value stays put. Change one of them on its own and you have a different number.

To add two fractions, first make the bottoms match.

Take two fifths and one third. Fifths and thirds are different sizes of piece, so there is
nothing yet to add. Find a size that both can be cut into. Fifteen works, because five goes into
fifteen and so does three.

Now rewrite each one at that size. Two fifths: multiply top and bottom by three, giving six
fifteenths. One third: multiply top and bottom by five, giving five fifteenths. Neither number
changed. You cut the same amount into smaller pieces.

Six fifteenths and five fifteenths are the same size of piece, so count them. Eleven fifteenths.

Add the tops and the bottoms instead and you get three eighths. That is less than the two fifths
you started with, and adding something to a number cannot make it smaller. That is your check.

A fraction is always a fraction of something. So before you compare two fractions, or add them,
check that they were cut from the same whole. If they were not, the arithmetic still works and
the answer still means nothing.

**Illustration.** Open the National Food Security Act, 2013, which you can open at
<https://indiacode.gov.in/handle/123456789/496105>. Search inside it for the words
NUTRITIONAL STANDARDS. It is the second schedule, and it sets what a meal under these
schemes has to deliver.

The standard for a lower primary hot cooked meal is 450 calories and 12 grams of protein. The
standard for an upper primary one is 700 calories and 20 grams. The Act sets what a meal has to
deliver. It does not say what any child receives.

Calories count food energy and grams count weight. A number with its label stripped off is not a
number any more.

What fraction of the upper primary energy standard is the lower primary one?

Write it as a fraction, energy over energy, and work it down.

```working
    450 over 700
    = 45 over 70        (both ended in a zero, so divide both by ten)
    = 9 over 14         (both are in the five times table, so divide both by five)
```

Somebody hears that and says: a lower primary child gets nine fourteenths of what a child needs.

They have swapped the whole. Nine fourteenths is the lower primary standard as a fraction of the
upper primary standard. Neither is what a child needs in a day, and those two rows of the Act
say nothing whatever about a day's requirement. The fraction is right. The sentence built on it
is not.

Do the protein column yourself now, the same way. Twelve over twenty. Divide both by four and
you have three fifths.

Nine fourteenths is a little under two thirds. Three fifths is a little over half.

**Where this picture breaks.** Fractions only behave if the parts are equal.

This section tells you how to handle a fraction once you have one. It cannot tell you what the
whole was. It comes from the document, the table heading or the person who handed you the
number. If none of them says, you do not have a fraction yet.

**Must know points for you.**

- A fraction on its own is not a finding. Nine fourteenths is only a fact once you can say what the fourteen was.
- To simplify a fraction, divide the top and the bottom by the same number. To compare or add two, put them over the same denominator first.
- Name which standard, and name the group it was written for.
- Asked in public what fraction of a child's need a meal covers, do not answer with the fraction you happen to have.
- Ask for the denominator every time, of your own numbers first.
