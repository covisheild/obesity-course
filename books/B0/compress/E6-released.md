# E6 · Metabolism as chemistry happening in a body

**Definition.** Metabolism is the sum of all of the chemical reactions that are involved in catabolism and anabolism. Catabolic reactions break down large organic molecules into smaller ones,
releasing the energy contained in their chemical bonds. Anabolic reactions use the energy produced by catabolic reactions to build larger molecules from smaller ones. Both sets
of reactions run continuously in the same body.

A living cell cannot store significant amounts of free energy. Part of the energy released is transferred to adenosine triphosphate, written ATP, and the remainder is given off
as heat, which tissues and body fluids absorb.

ATP carries energy between the reactions that release it and the reactions that consume it. ATP is therefore a carrier and not a store.

Food molecules are absorbed only after being reduced in size. All carbohydrates are absorbed in the form of monosaccharides. Triglycerides are broken into free fatty acids,
which cross the intestinal membrane and are recombined into triglyceride molecules once across.

Not everything eaten is absorbed. The human body produces no enzyme that can break down most fibrous polysaccharides, such as cellulose.

Glucose is the body's most readily available source of energy. Excess glucose is either stored as an energy reserve in the liver and skeletal muscles as the complex polymer
glycogen, or converted into fat, that is triglyceride, in adipose cells.

A triglyceride is the molecular form that fat takes both in food and in the body. When a person eats more glucose or carbohydrate than the body needs, that excess is turned into
fat. Triglycerides are stored in adipose tissue until they are needed.

Where catabolic reactions release more energy than anabolic reactions use, the body stores the excess energy by building fat molecules for long-term storage. Where catabolic
reactions release less energy than anabolic reactions use, the body uses stored energy to compensate. Nothing in this account fixes the size of either flow, the rate at which
any of it happens, or the fraction of energy that survives any step.

**In plain terms.** Metabolism is a word people use as though it were a thing you could have more or less of. It is
not a thing. It is a name for all the chemical reactions going on in a body at once, added
together.

Some reactions take a big molecule and break it into smaller ones. Reactions that go this way are
called catabolism.

Other reactions take small molecules and join them into bigger ones. Building costs energy.
Reactions that go this way are called anabolism.

A cell cannot hold a useful amount of loose energy. So the energy goes into a small molecule
first, and you have already met that molecule: adenosine triphosphate, written ATP.

Think of ATP as a coin. Breaking food earns coins. Building things spends coins.

Here is what the coin is, physically. ATP has three phosphate groups on it, sitting in a row.
Knock the last one off and energy comes out, and that loose phosphate goes and sticks onto
whatever the cell wanted to set going. What is left is called ADP, which is the same molecule one
phosphate short.

Put a phosphate back on and you have ATP again. Paying for that is what the energy from food is
for.

Not all the energy in a food molecule ends up in coins. Some of it comes off as heat, and the
tissue and fluid around it soak that heat up.

ATP is not where a body keeps its energy. It is what a body moves energy with. Anyone who tells
you the body stores its energy as ATP has mistaken the coin for the bank.

Food has to get in. In, through the wall of the gut and into your blood.

So the gut takes the food apart, cutting big molecules down into pieces small enough to pass
through the lining. That passing through has a name, and the name is absorption.

What you eat and what you absorb are two different amounts.

Some of what is on your plate never crosses. Your body makes no tool for breaking down most plant
fibre, so fibre goes in at one end and out at the other.

A figure on a packet is a figure about the food. It is not a figure about you.

Break a starch — the stuff that rice, wheat and potato are mostly made of — and you get single
sugar molecules. The commonest of them, and the fuel a body reaches for first, is glucose.

Glucose crosses the gut wall into the blood, and that blood runs first to the liver. A cell that
takes glucose in breaks it down and makes coins with the energy.

The near store is glycogen. Glycogen is a great many glucose molecules strung together, kept in
the liver and in the muscles.

The far store is fat, and the fat in food and the fat in a body are the same kind of molecule. It
has a name: triglyceride.

Triglyceride gets into a body two ways. You eat it. Or the body builds it, out of sugar there is
more of than there is a use for right now.

The store is a tissue, and its name is adipose tissue. It is made of cells whose job is to hold
triglyceride, and they hold it until it is needed.

A triglyceride is pulled apart into its two kinds of piece, fatty acids and glycerol. The pieces
are then broken down like any other fuel.

So read the two stores as what they are. One is near and small. One is far and large. Filling
them and emptying them is the system working. That is what a store is for.

Catabolism lets energy go. Anabolism uses it. ATP carries it from one to the other.

And when catabolism lets go of more than anabolism is using, the extra is built into fat and put
away. When it lets go of less, the body draws on what was put away before.

All of it is a direction. None of it is a size. You do not know how much, or how fast, or how
much of it survives each step, and nothing in this section will tell you.

Take a plate you have actually eaten from. Rice, a bowl of dal, a spoonful of oil in the tadka,
and a raw onion sliced on the side. Follow each of the four, using only what this section has
given you.

Rice is mostly starch, which is a long chain of sugar molecules. The gut cuts the chain into
single sugars, and glucose is the one to watch. It crosses the gut wall into the blood. The
blood carries it to the liver.

What the body has no immediate use for does not stay in the blood. Some goes into the near
store, as glycogen in the liver and the muscles. Some goes into the far store, turned into
triglyceride in adipose tissue.

Now the dal. Dal is largely protein, and protein is a chain too, made of amino acids. The gut
cuts it into single amino acids and those cross the wall. On the far side they go one of two
ways: into new proteins, or broken down further to make coins like anything else.

Now the oil. Oil is triglyceride, and a whole triglyceride molecule will not cross the gut wall.
So it is broken into its pieces first. The pieces cross. On the far side they are put back
together into triglyceride again. That triglyceride goes into the body, to be used or stored.

A lot of a raw onion is fibre. Your body makes no enzyme that can break most plant fibre down,
so none of it is cut small enough to cross. It travels the length of the gut and leaves. While
it is inside it is doing something useful: it helps move everything else along. But it never
became part of you, and it handed you no energy.

So look at what happens if you price that plate out of a food table. The table gives a figure
for onion. Add it in, and you have counted energy that never crossed. Your total is a number
about the plate. It is not a number about the person.

When somebody hands you a figure for what a person ate, ask three questions in this order. Where
was it measured — at the mouth, or anywhere else? Was it measured at all, or looked up in a
table? And what happened to it between the plate and the blood?

**Where this picture breaks.** A cell does not keep a purse. It makes coins and spends them almost at once.

Near store and far store describe the size of the two and nothing else. This section gives no
rule about which one is drawn on first, or how fast either fills or empties.

**Must know points for you.**

- What a person eats and what a person takes up are two different amounts. Some of what is eaten never crosses the gut wall at all, because the body makes no tool for most plant fibre. So a figure for intake is a figure about food.
- Adipose tissue holds triglyceride until it is needed. So describe fat storage as a store doing its work.
- ATP is what a body moves energy with. It is not where a body keeps it. The stores are glycogen and triglyceride. Correct a trainee who says energy is stored as ATP, because that error spoils every later sentence they write about stores.
- Somebody will hand you a figure for how many kilocalories make a kilogram of body weight. Book 0 does not give you one. Ask what the change is made of before you accept any sum built on it.
- Catabolism and anabolism are directions, not departments. Both run in the same cells at the same time, and a person is never in one or the other. The answer is always both.
- Metabolism is the name for every chemical reaction in a body added together. So a drink that boosts your metabolism has named nothing at all. Ask which reaction, in which direction, in which tissue, and how the change was measured.
- Direction is the whole of what you have here, and size is no part of it. Spare energy goes to glycogen and to fat. Refuse any sum that needs one of those numbers and does not say where it was measured.
