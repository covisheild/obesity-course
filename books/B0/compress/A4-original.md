### A4 · Percentages, and the percentage of a percentage

*B0-R0-C04 · derivable*

**Definition.** A percentage is a fraction whose denominator is one hundred. Per cent means per hundred, so 24
per cent is 24/100, which is 0.24.

A percentage change is the change divided by the value it started from, multiplied by one
hundred. The starting value is therefore part of the claim, and the same absolute change gives
a different percentage depending on which end it is measured from.

A percentage point is the arithmetic difference between two percentages. A per cent change is
that difference divided by the first percentage. A rise from 2 per cent to 3 per cent is a rise
of one percentage point and a rise of 50 per cent, and both statements describe the same event.

A percentage of a percentage is the product of the two fractions: 40 per cent of 30 per cent is
0.40 times 0.30, which is 0.12, or 12 per cent of the original whole.

**In plain terms.** Per cent means per hundred. So a percentage is a fraction forced to sit over a denominator of one
hundred, whatever it was over before. Here the denominator — the number a percentage is a
percentage of — is always a hundred. Twenty-four per cent is twenty-four out of a hundred, which
is 0.24.

That is the easy half, and almost nobody gets it wrong. The hard half is what the hundred was.

A percentage change is worked out against the number you started from. Take that number, find the
change, divide the change by the starting number, and multiply by a hundred. Because the starting
number sits underneath, going up and coming back down are not the same percentage.

Go up from 450 to 700 and you have risen by about 56 per cent. Come back from 700 to 450 and you
have fallen by about 36 per cent. Same two numbers. Same step. Two different percentages, and
neither is wrong.

Then there are two things called per cent that are not the same thing at all.

If something goes from 2 per cent to 3 per cent, it has gone up by one percentage point. It has
also gone up by 50 per cent, because one is half of two. Both sentences are true. One of them
sounds like almost nothing and the other sounds like a crisis, and whoever is speaking gets to
pick.

Last, a percentage of a percentage. Forty per cent of the thirty per cent who do something is not
forty per cent and not thirty. Multiply the two: 0.40 times 0.30 is 0.12. Twelve people in every
hundred.

**Illustration.** Go back to the two rows you read in the National Food Security Act, 2013. A lower primary meal
is set at 450 calories. An upper primary meal is set at 700.

Somebody wants a sentence for a note. How much more is the upper primary standard?

There are two true answers and they sound completely different. Work out both.

The plain difference first.

```
    700 minus 450 = 250 calories more
```

Now as a percentage. The rule is: the change, divided by what you started from, times a hundred.
You are starting at 450 and going up.

```
    250 divided by 450 = 0.5555...
    0.5555... times 100 = 55.6 per cent      (rounded to one place, as the last section had it)
```

So the upper primary meal is 250 calories more, and it is about 56 per cent more. Both are
right.

Now turn it round, because this is where people slip. Start at 700 and come down to 450.

```
    250 divided by 700 = 0.3571...
    0.3571... times 100 = 35.7 per cent
```

The lower primary meal is about 36 per cent less. Not 56. The step is the same 250 calories. The
number sitting underneath changed, so the percentage changed with it.

That is the whole of it: a percentage change is a fraction, and the thing you started from is
the denominator. Ask which end it was measured from, every single time.

Now the failure this section exists for. Here is a sentence of the kind that reaches a front
page.

> The share of children affected rose by fifty per cent in a decade.

Suppose the real figures behind it are two in a hundred rising to three in a hundred. Write both
ways of describing that.

```
    3 minus 2 = 1 percentage point
    1 divided by 2 = 0.5, so 50 per cent
```

Both are true of the same change. One extra child in every hundred. The writer picked the fifty.

Say the two apart out loud and you will not confuse them again. Percentage points subtract.
Per cent divides.

And notice what you still cannot say. Going from two in a hundred to three in a hundred might be
one extra child in a village. It might be a hundred thousand across the country. The percentages
hide which. Ask for the count as well as the percentage, always.

One last move, because it comes up constantly. Suppose 30 per cent of schoolchildren in a
district eat a packaged snack on a school day. Of those who do, suppose 40 per cent buy it
within a hundred metres of the school gate. What share of all schoolchildren is that? Multiply
the fractions.

```
    0.40 times 0.30 = 0.12, so 12 per cent of all schoolchildren
```

Those two percentages are made up, to show the move. The move is not. A percentage of a
percentage is smaller than either. Reporting the 40 on its own is how a modest number gets
printed as a big one.

**Where this picture breaks.** Percentages hide the size of the thing they are a percentage of, and they hide it by design.
That is what makes them useful for comparing and dangerous for deciding. Fifty per cent of six
people and fifty per cent of six lakh people are the same percentage and not the same problem.

A second break. Percentages of a whole only add up to a hundred when the categories do not
overlap and nothing is left out. Once somebody can be counted in two of them, the column adds
past a hundred and the arithmetic stops meaning anything.

A third, and it is the one that catches careful people. A percentage change and a percentage
point look alike on the page and are different quantities. When a source says "per cent" you
often cannot tell which it meant, and you cannot repair it by reading harder. Go back to the two
original numbers, or say in your own writing that you could not.

**Must know points for you.**

- A percentage point and a per cent are different quantities. Two per cent rising to three is one percentage point and fifty per cent, and both sentences describe one extra person in every hundred. Whichever is quoted at you, ask for the other.

- For any percentage in front of you, ask two questions before you use it: per cent of what, and how many people is that. A percentage with no count beside it cannot be acted on, however precise it looks.

- A rise and the fall that undoes it are different percentages, because the starting value sits underneath. Going up 56 per cent and coming back down 36 per cent land in the same place. Say which end you measured from whenever you quote a change.

- A percentage of a percentage multiplies, so it is always smaller than either. Quoting the inner percentage on its own is the commonest way a modest finding gets printed as a large one. It is done more often by accident than on purpose.

- In a committee a relative change will be offered when it is the larger number and an absolute one when it is the smaller. Bring both to the meeting yourself. Arriving with only the flattering one is how a good case gets dismantled in front of you.

- When a journalist gives you a percentage on the phone, do not react to it. Ask what it is a percentage of and what the two underlying counts were. If the answer does not come, say on the record that the figure cannot be interpreted without them.

- This section gets you the arithmetic of a percentage. It says nothing about whether the change is real, whether it could be chance, or whether it was caused by anything in particular. Those are later and they are harder.
