# E4 · Heat and temperature; how heat moves

**Definition.** Temperature is a measure of how hot or cold something is. Heat is the spontaneous transfer of
energy due to a temperature difference. The two are different quantities: heat is a form of
energy and temperature is not, and they carry different units. Heat is measured in joules, the
SI unit of energy. Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or in kelvin.

Heat is energy in transit. A body does not contain heat; it contains internal energy, and its
internal energy is higher when its temperature is higher. When two bodies at different
temperatures are brought into contact, energy is transferred from the hotter to the colder one
until they reach the same temperature, at which point the transfer ceases. That state is
thermal equilibrium.

The heat capacity of a body of matter is the quantity of heat it absorbs or releases when its
temperature changes by one degree Celsius, which is the same interval as one kelvin. Its unit
is joules per degree Celsius. Heat capacity depends on both what the body is made of and how
much of it there is, so it belongs to a particular object rather than to a material. The
energy equivalent of a bomb calorimeter is that calorimeter's heat capacity.

The specific heat of a substance is the heat required to change the temperature of one kilogram
of it by one degree Celsius. Its unit is joules per kilogram per degree Celsius. Specific heat
depends only on the substance and its phase, not on how much is present.

The quantitative relationship between heat transfer and temperature change contains three
factors, and is written as follows. The letter Q stands for the quantity of heat transferred.
The letter m stands for the mass of the substance. The letter c stands for its specific heat.
The Greek capital letter delta, written Δ, stands for a change in whatever follows it, so the
pair ΔT stands for the change in temperature and is read "delta T".

```working
    Q = m times c times ΔT
```

The change in temperature is the final temperature minus the initial temperature. When a
substance loses thermal energy its final temperature is lower than its initial temperature, so
ΔT is negative and Q is negative, and the negative sign means energy left rather than entered.

Values of specific heat must be looked up in tables, because there is no simple way to
calculate them, and they vary somewhat with temperature. At 15.0 °C the specific heat of water
is 4186 joules per kilogram per degree Celsius, that of glass is 840 and that of iron and steel
is 452. Water has one of the largest specific heats of any material.

Every process involving heat transfer takes place by only three methods. Conduction is heat
transfer through stationary matter by physical contact. Convection is heat transfer by the
macroscopic movement of a fluid. Heat transfer by radiation occurs when microwaves, infrared
radiation, visible light, or another form of electromagnetic radiation is emitted or absorbed.

**In plain terms.** Two sections back, in the section on the calorimeter, you watched a thermometer go up and called
the rise a measurement of energy. That works inside one instrument, and it is about to stop
working. Here is why.

Start with two words that everyday speech treats as one.

**Temperature** is how hot a thing is. A thermometer reads it. It is a level, not an amount. It does not care how much of the thing there is. A spoonful of boiling
water and a bucket of boiling water are at the same temperature.

**Heat** is energy moving, because one thing is hotter than another. It is an amount, and it is
measured in joules, like every other energy in this book.

So the bucket and the spoonful are at the same temperature and hold very different amounts. That
one sentence is the section.

Now the rule about which way it moves, and it has no exceptions. Energy goes from the hotter
thing to the colder thing, and it keeps going until the two are at the same temperature. Then it
stops. Nothing has to push it and nothing can make it go the other way on its own.

Next, how much. Three things decide how far a temperature moves when energy goes in.

How much stuff there is. What the stuff is. And how big a temperature change you are after.

Multiply the three together and you have the amount of energy. Say it in words first.

The energy equals the mass, times a number belonging to the material, times the change in
temperature.

Now in letters, one at a time. The letter Q stands for the quantity of heat. The letter m stands
for the mass. The letter c stands for the number belonging to the material. The Greek capital
letter delta, written Δ, is how mathematics writes "the change in". So the pair ΔT stands for the
change in temperature, and it is read "delta T".

```working
    Q = m times c times ΔT
```

That number belonging to the material has a name: the **specific heat**. It is the joules you
need for one kilogram of the stuff to go up by one degree. Every material has its own, you look
it up in a table, and nobody can work one out from first principles.

Here is the number to remember, and it is why this section exists.

```table
material         joules for one kilogram, one degree
water            4186
glass            840
iron and steel   452
```

Water needs more than nine times as much energy as steel for the same rise. So pour the same
energy into a kilogram of each and the steel gets more than nine times hotter. **A bigger
temperature rise does not mean more heat went in.** It means the two things were different
things.

One more word, because the section on the calorimeter used it without naming it. **Heat
capacity** is the same idea for one particular object rather than for a material. It is the
joules that object needs to go up by one degree. A calorimeter has one, and you have already used
it. The energy equivalent from that section is the calorimeter's heat capacity. It is also why a
calorimeter's degrees can be trusted: inside one instrument the object never changes, so the
number never changes.

Now the sign, and you have already met negative numbers in the section on counting.

The change in temperature is the temperature at the end minus the temperature at the start. If
the thing cooled, that subtraction gives a negative number, and so does Q. The minus sign is not
an error. It means the energy went out rather than in.

Last, how heat actually gets from one place to another. There are exactly three ways, and that is
not a list somebody stopped writing. It is all of them.

**Conduction** is touching. Energy moves through matter that is staying put, by contact. A steel
tumbler of hot chai and your fingers.

**Convection** is carrying. A fluid — a liquid or a gas — moves, and the energy goes with it. Hot
air rising off a tawa. Blood carrying warmth from the middle of you out to your skin.

**Radiation** is shining. Energy crosses as light, or as the invisible light below red that
bodies give off, and it needs nothing in between. The warmth on your face from an open fire
across the room.

Why you want those three names. Anything that keeps something warm or cool acts on one of them
directly. So when somebody tells you their product keeps heat in, ask two questions. Which of
the three does it act on? And what happens to the other two?

**Illustration.** Start with the belief the section on the calorimeter may have left you with, because it is
wrong and it is reasonable.

In the calorimeter, a bigger temperature rise meant more energy. You measured the rise, you
multiplied by the instrument's energy equivalent, and you got joules. Do that a few times and a sentence forms by itself. **A bigger temperature rise means more heat.**

Go and break it, with two things you have in the kitchen.

Take a steel tawa that weighs one kilogram. Take one kilogram of water in a pan. Put them on two
identical burners and give each exactly the same amount of energy.

Use 4,186 joules, because it makes the first sum come out at one. Look up what each material
needs, from Table 14.1 of OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 14.2, at
https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity.

```table
| material | joules per kilogram per degree Celsius |
| Water (15.0 °C) | 4186 |
| Glass | 840 |
| Iron, steel | 452 |
```

Now the water. Multiply the mass by the specific heat to get the joules it needs for one degree.

```working
    1 times 4186 = 4186
```

Four thousand one hundred and eighty-six joules for one degree. You are giving it exactly that,
so the water goes up by one degree Celsius.

```working
    4186 divided by 4186 = 1
```

Now the steel, the same way.

```working
    1 times 452 = 452
```

Four hundred and fifty-two joules for one degree. So 4,186 joules will take the tawa a long way
up. Bracket it before you reach for the calculator, because the bracket is the part worth
seeing.

Nine degrees would use this much.

```working
    452 times 9 = 4068
```

Four thousand and sixty-eight, and you gave it 4,186. So it goes past nine degrees. Now try ten.

```working
    452 times 10 = 4520
```

Four thousand five hundred and twenty, which is more than you gave it. So the rise lands between
nine and ten degrees. A calculator puts it at 9.26 degrees Celsius.

Stop and read the pair. Same energy, both times. One kilogram, both times. One thermometer reads
a rise of one degree and the other reads a rise of more than nine. **The temperature rise did
not measure the heat.** It measured the heat divided by what the object needed per degree, and
the two objects needed very different amounts.

Now run it the other way, which is the direction most real questions arrive in. How much energy
to raise each by exactly one degree?

```working
    1 times 4186 times 1 = 4186
```

```working
    1 times 452 times 1 = 452
```

Four thousand one hundred and eighty-six joules against four hundred and fifty-two. The book
puts the same comparison in words.

> We see from this table that the specific heat of water is five times that of glass and ten
> times that of iron

Ten times is the round version, and you have just seen where the rounding is. Ten times 452 is
4,520, and the table gives water as 4,186.

```working
    4520 minus 4186 = 334
```

So "ten times" overshoots by 334 joules per kilogram per degree, which is about eight per cent
of water's figure. The true ratio is a little over nine. The sentence and the table are not in
conflict; the sentence rounded, and it says so by writing "ten times" instead of giving a
number.

Now go back and ask what was true about the calorimeter, because something was.

Inside one calorimeter the object never changes. It is the same steel, the same water, the same
thermometer, every run. So the joules it needs per degree never change either, and that is the
energy equivalent you were handed. It has a proper name now. It is the calorimeter's **heat
capacity**, in joules per degree Celsius, and it belongs to that instrument rather than to the
food in it.

Which tells you exactly how far the calorimeter sentence travels. Within one instrument, twice
the rise is twice the energy. Across two instruments it is worth nothing, because the two heat
capacities differ and nobody has said by how much.

Last for this half, the sign, on a cup you can watch.

Two hundred grams of chai are poured at 95 degrees Celsius and left. An hour later they are at
30. The change in temperature is the end minus the start.

```working
    30 minus 95 = -65
```

Minus 65 degrees, and the minus is doing work. Now the energy, taking 0.2 kilograms of a liquid
that is mostly water.

```working
    0.2 times 4186 = 837.2
    837.2 times -65 = -54418
```

Minus 54,418 joules. The minus sign says it left. Fifty-four thousand joules went out of that
cup into the room, and the only thing that showed on any instrument was a thermometer falling by
65 degrees. Negative numbers come from the section on counting. Nothing new is being asked of
you here except to keep the sign and read it.

You now know which way heat goes and how much. Neither tells you how it gets there, and the
route is the only part of this you can usually change.

Open OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 14.4, Heat Transfer Methods, at
https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-4-heat-transfer-methods. Read the
sentence just before the numbered list.

> Yet every process involving heat transfer takes place by only three methods:

Only three. That is a strong claim and the book makes it flatly. Here they are, in its words,
and then in your kitchen.

> 1. Conduction is heat transfer through stationary matter by physical contact.

Pour chai into a steel tumbler and pick it up. Your fingers know within a second. The steel
stayed where it was and the energy came through it into your hand. Now pour the same chai into a
clay kulhar and pick that up. You can hold it. Nothing about the chai changed. The matter
between the chai and your fingers did.

> 2. Convection is the heat transfer by the macroscopic movement of a fluid.

Hold your palm a foot above a hot tawa, not touching it. The air over the tawa is being heated,
it rises, and it brings the energy up to your hand. The air is the fluid and the moving is the
point. Your own body does this to itself, and the book says so in its section on convection. When you
overheat, the vessels in your skin widen. More blood flows to the skin, and it carries warmth
from the middle of you to the outside.

> 3. Heat transfer by radiation occurs when microwaves, infrared radiation, visible light, or
> another form of electromagnetic radiation is emitted or absorbed.

Stand three feet from an open tandoor and face it. Your face warms, and the air in between did
not have to carry anything. That is the third route, and it needs nothing in between at all. The
book gives the warming of the Earth by the Sun as the obvious case and adds a less obvious one:
thermal radiation from the human body. You are doing it now.

Now the part that makes the three names useful rather than decorative. Read what the same
section says about controlling them.

> We can control rates of heat transfer by choosing materials (such as thick wool clothing for
> the winter), controlling air movement (such as the use of weather stripping around doors), or
> by choice of color (such as a white roof to reflect summer sunlight).

Three controls, and each of them bites on a route you can name. Choosing the material acts on
how easily heat gets through it. Thick wool works by holding still air in its fibres, which
slows conduction and convection at once. Stopping the air moving acts on convection. Choosing
the colour acts on radiation.

So here is what to do with your hands the next time somebody claims a thing keeps heat in or
keeps heat out. Take a sheet of paper and write the three routes down the left side. Then put
the claim against each one in turn and ask what it does about that route.

Try it on a white roof, which is the book's own example. Colour acts on radiation, so the roof
absorbs less of the sunlight falling on it. That is the only route the colour touches. The
other two are downstream of it. A cooler slab conducts less heat down into the ceiling, and a
cooler ceiling warms the air under it less. So naming the route tells you where the control
bites, not which routes end up different. Whether the white roof is enough depends on how much
of the heat was arriving as sunlight on the roof in the first place.

That last sentence is the whole method. Naming the route turns a slogan into a question with an
answer, and the question is always the same one: how much was coming that way?

**Where this picture breaks.** The two one-kilogram masses and the 4,186 joules are chosen to make the first sum come out at
one. The three specific heats are real and come from Table 14.1.

The heat equation holds only while the substance stays the same substance in the same phase.
Boil the water and it stops. The energy then goes into turning liquid into steam, and the
temperature sits still while that happens. A formula with the change in temperature in it then
returns zero for an energy that is not zero. The same is true of ice melting. Any calculation
that crosses a boiling point or a melting point has left this section behind.

Specific heat is not truly a constant. The book says it varies with temperature, and Table 14.1
labels its water row 15.0 °C, not "water" on its own. Over the ranges used here the variation
is small enough to ignore. Over a wide range it is not.

The two books in this course do not give the same figure for water, and it is worth knowing
which one you are holding. College Physics 2e Table 14.1 gives 4186 joules per kilogram per
degree Celsius. Chemistry 2e section 5.1 gives 4.184 joules per gram per degree Celsius, which
is 4184 joules per kilogram per degree. The gap is under one part in two thousand, and neither
book explains the other's figure. Use one book's figure all the way through a calculation, and
say which one you used.

A person is not one substance and has no single specific heat you can look up. A body is water,
fat, muscle and bone at once. Chemical reactions are running inside it the whole time. So both
conditions above are broken at once. Nothing here gives you a figure for heating or cooling a
person, and none of these numbers should be carried across to one.

All three routes run at once in every real situation. The tumbler conducts into your fingers
while the chai's surface radiates and the air above it convects. Naming the route a control
acts on does not settle what the other two then do. Sometimes they follow, the way a cooler
roof slab conducts less. Sometimes they do not, and the control is undone by the two it left
alone.

This section gives no rates. It says by which routes heat moves and never how fast. So it cannot settle whether any of these
controls is large enough to matter in a particular house, pan or person. Deciding that needs measurement, and measurement is not
here.

The clay kulhar and the steel tumbler differ in more than one way at once. The clay conducts
less well, and the wall is thicker. The comparison shows you that the matter in between
decides the answer. It does not isolate which property of that matter did it.

Sweating is not one of the three, and the book does not treat it as one. Section 14.6 mentions the skin being cooled by sweating in the same breath as blood flow. This
course has not given you the physics of a liquid turning into a vapour. Any claim that turns on evaporation is outside what you are holding. Say so, rather than reach for
the nearest of the three names.

"Only three methods" is a claim about routes across a boundary. It is not a promise that every
warming or cooling you meet is explained by naming one. A person getting warmer may be doing it
by running a chemical reaction inside their own boundary. That is not a transfer across the
boundary at all.

**Must know points for you.**

- A bigger temperature rise does not mean more heat went in. Anyone who has used a calorimeter will believe it does. Give a kilogram of steel and a kilogram of water the same energy. The steel rises more than nine times as far. So a thermometer reading is not an energy. It becomes one only when you multiply it by a figure that belongs to the object being heated.
- Heat is energy on the way, not a thing an object holds, so refuse the sentence "this object contains so much heat". What it holds is internal energy. Heat is the name of a transfer. Keep the two apart and nobody can treat a temperature as a store of energy to be passed on.
- When anyone turns a temperature reading into an energy, ask two things. What heat capacity did they use? Where did it come from? Inside one machine the heat capacity is fixed, so the reading can be trusted. Across two machines a degree is not a degree. Rises set side by side with no heat capacity named are not energies set side by side.
- Water needs more than nine times the energy that iron or steel needs, for the same rise in the same mass. The figures are 4186 joules per kilogram per degree Celsius at 15.0 °C, against 452. Carry that ratio and you can check any claim about how fast a wet thing heats or cools.
- The heat equation holds only while the substance stays the same substance in the same phase. It breaks at every boiling point, every melting point and every chemical reaction. A body has all three running at once. That is why nothing here gives you a figure for heating or cooling a person. When a calculation reaches one of those points, stop rather than substitute.
- A temperature is not an amount of energy, so never read a patient's temperature as a statement about energy gained or lost. One is in degrees and the other in joules. Nothing converts between them without a figure for what that body needs per degree, and nobody has one.
- Heat moves by conduction, convection and radiation, and by nothing else, so make every claim about keeping warm or cool name its route. Once the route is named, the question becomes how much was arriving that way, and that can be answered. While it is unnamed, the claim commits to nothing.
- Teach the two-objects demonstration before the formula. Let a trainee watch a kilogram of steel and a kilogram of water take the same energy to different temperatures. After that they will not read a rise as an energy again. Not once. One who has only learnt the formula will do it the first time they are tired.
