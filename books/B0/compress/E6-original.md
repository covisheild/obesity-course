# E6 · Metabolism as chemistry happening in a body

**Definition.** Metabolism is the sum of all of the chemical reactions that are involved in catabolism and
anabolism. Catabolic reactions break down large organic molecules into smaller ones, releasing
the energy contained in their chemical bonds. Anabolic reactions use the energy produced by
catabolic reactions to build larger molecules from smaller ones. Both sets of reactions run
continuously in the same body.

The energy released by a catabolic reaction is not delivered directly to the work that consumes
it. A living cell cannot store significant amounts of free energy. Part of the energy released
is transferred to adenosine triphosphate, written ATP, and the remainder is given off as heat,
which tissues and body fluids absorb. The amount of energy released by breaking a molecule is
less than the total amount contained in it.

ATP carries energy between the reactions that release it and the reactions that consume it.
Removing its terminal phosphate group releases energy, leaving adenosine diphosphate (ADP), and the released phosphate group activates the molecule it binds. ADP is continuously
regenerated into ATP by the reattachment of a third phosphate group. In nearly every living
thing on Earth, the energy for that reattachment comes from the metabolism of glucose, fructose
or galactose. ATP is therefore a carrier and not a store.

Food molecules are absorbed only after being reduced in size. Large food molecules must be
broken down into subunits small enough to be absorbed by the lining of the alimentary canal.
All carbohydrates are absorbed in the form of monosaccharides. Triglycerides are broken into
free fatty acids, which cross the intestinal membrane and are recombined into triglyceride
molecules once across. Proteins are broken down into individual amino acids, which are used as
building blocks of new proteins or broken down further for the production of ATP.

Not everything eaten is absorbed. The human body produces no enzyme that can break down most
fibrous polysaccharides, such as cellulose. Indigestible polysaccharides provide no nutritional
value, and indigestible fibres are eliminated in the faeces, having served instead to help
propel food through the alimentary canal. What passes from the small intestine into the large
intestine is essentially indigestible food residue, some water and bacteria.

Glucose is the body's most readily available source of energy. After digestive processes break
polysaccharides down into monosaccharides, including glucose, the monosaccharides are
transported across the wall of the small intestine and into the circulatory system, which
transports them to the liver. In the liver they are either passed on through the circulatory
system or stored. Excess glucose is either stored as an energy reserve in the liver and
skeletal muscles as the complex polymer glycogen, or converted into fat, that is triglyceride,
in adipose cells.

A triglyceride is the molecular form that fat takes both in food and in the body.
Triglycerides within the body are ingested as food, or synthesised by adipose cells or liver
cells from carbohydrate precursors. When a person eats more glucose or carbohydrate than the
body needs, that excess is turned into fat. To obtain energy from fat, a triglyceride must
first be broken down by hydrolysis into its two principal components, fatty acids and glycerol.
Triglycerides are stored in adipose tissue until they are needed.

The direction of the whole system follows from the two sets of reactions. Where catabolic
reactions release more energy than anabolic reactions use, the body stores the excess energy by
building fat molecules for long-term storage. Where catabolic reactions release less energy
than anabolic reactions use, the body uses stored energy to compensate. Nothing in this account
fixes the size of either flow, the rate at which any of it happens, or the fraction of energy
that survives any step.

**In plain terms.** The section on the cell gave you a cell and the small parts inside it. This one is about what
goes on inside that cell all day, and about what the body does with what you eat.

Metabolism is a word people use as though it were a thing you could have more or less of. It is
not a thing. It is a name for all the chemical reactions going on in a body at once, added
together.

Those reactions go in two directions, and each direction has a name.

Some reactions take a big molecule and break it into smaller ones. Breaking it lets go of the
energy that was held in its bonds, which is the energy the section on atoms showed you. Reactions
that go this way are called catabolism.

Other reactions take small molecules and join them into bigger ones. Building costs energy.
Reactions that go this way are called anabolism.

Both are happening in you right now, in the same cells, at the same time. Catabolism is not
something that happens when you are hungry and anabolism when you are full. They run together,
and the only question is which of them is running further ahead.

Now the part in the middle, and most people have never been told it.

The energy let go by breaking a molecule does not travel straight to the job that needs it. A
cell cannot hold a useful amount of loose energy. So the energy goes into a small molecule
first, and you have already met that molecule: adenosine triphosphate, written ATP.

Think of ATP as a coin. Breaking food earns coins. Building things spends coins. Nothing in a
cell trades directly in food, and everything trades in coins.

Here is what the coin is, physically. ATP has three phosphate groups on it, sitting in a row.
Knock the last one off and energy comes out, and that loose phosphate goes and sticks onto
whatever the cell wanted to set going. What is left is called ADP, which is the same molecule one
phosphate short.

Put a phosphate back on and you have ATP again. Paying for that is what the energy from food is
for. So the cell runs a loop all day: food broken, coins made, coins spent, work done.

Two things follow from the loop, and the second is the one people get wrong.

First. Not all the energy in a food molecule ends up in coins. Some of it comes off as heat, and
the tissue and fluid around it soak that heat up. The heat is not a mistake. It is part of what
keeps you at the temperature you are.

Second. ATP is not where a body keeps its energy. It is what a body moves energy with. A cell
holds very little of it and spends what it holds almost at once. Anyone who tells you the body
stores its energy as ATP has mistaken the coin for the bank.

So where is the bank? There are two, and they are most of the rest of this section. But one thing
has to happen before either of them can be filled.

Food has to get in. Not into your mouth. In, through the wall of the gut and into your blood.

A piece of chapati is far too big to go through that wall, and so is every molecule in it. So the
gut takes the food apart, cutting big molecules down into pieces small enough to pass through the
lining. That passing through has a name, and the name is absorption.

Now the sentence this section exists to put in your hands. **What you eat and what you absorb are
two different amounts.**

Some of what is on your plate never crosses. Your body makes no tool for breaking down most plant
fibre, so fibre goes in at one end and out at the other. It is not doing nothing while it is
inside. It is helping push everything else along. What reaches the end of the small intestine is
mostly the residue that nothing could take up, some water and bacteria.

Hold on to that. A figure on a packet is a figure about the food. It is not a figure about you.
Nobody measured what crossed your gut wall, and that is not what the packet says.

Now the two banks. Start with sugar.

Break a starch — the stuff that rice, wheat and potato are mostly made of — and you get single
sugar molecules. The commonest of them, and the fuel a body reaches for first, is glucose.

Glucose crosses the gut wall into the blood, and that blood runs first to the liver. From there
it goes round the rest of the body. A cell that takes glucose in breaks it down and makes coins
with the energy. That is what glucose is mainly for.

What about glucose that nobody needs this minute? It does not sit in the blood waiting. It gets
put away, in one of two places.

The near store is glycogen. Glycogen is a great many glucose molecules strung together, kept in
the liver and in the muscles. It is quick to build and quick to take apart again.

The far store is fat, and the fat in food and the fat in a body are the same kind of molecule. It
has a name: triglyceride.

Triglyceride gets into a body two ways. You eat it. Or the body builds it, out of sugar there is
more of than there is a use for right now. Eat more carbohydrate than the body has work for and
some of it is turned into fat. That is the second store filling.

The store is a tissue, and its name is adipose tissue. It is made of cells whose job is to hold
triglyceride, and they hold it until it is needed.

Getting energy back out is the same move in reverse. A triglyceride is pulled apart into its two
kinds of piece, fatty acids and glycerol. The pieces are then broken down like any other fuel.

So read the two stores as what they are. One is near and small. One is far and large. Filling
them and emptying them is the system working. That is what a store is for.

Here is the whole section in three lines.

Catabolism lets energy go. Anabolism uses it. ATP carries it from one to the other.

And when catabolism lets go of more than anabolism is using, the extra is built into fat and put
away. When it lets go of less, the body draws on what was put away before.

One last thing, and it is a warning about everything you have just read. All of it is a
direction. None of it is a size. You now know that spare energy goes to glycogen and to fat. You
do not know how much, or how fast, or how much of it survives each step, and nothing in this
section will tell you. Those are measurements. This is only the shape they hang on.

**Illustration.** Start with a question you would actually ask, and watch the answer fail to arrive.

You have just been told that breaking food lets energy go. Some of what is let go becomes ATP
and some of it leaves as heat. The obvious next question is how much. So go and get the
figure.

Open OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, section 24.1, *Overview of Metabolic Reactions*, at
https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/24-1-overview-of-metabolic-reactions.
Open your browser's find box. Search the page for the word **percent**.

A sentence comes up straight away. It gives you a figure for how much of the released energy
goes into ATP, and another for how much leaves as heat. It is exactly what you went looking
for.

Now do the second half, which is the half almost nobody does. Find out where those two numbers
came from.

Look at the end of the sentence. There is no reference number. Look at the paragraph around it.
No study is named. Look at the foot of the page. There is no reference list.

Read the whole section. The figures are stated flat, in running prose. The page states them in
the same voice it uses to say that catabolism releases energy.

So there are two claims on that page, and they are not the same kind of claim.

One is that breaking food lets energy go and that some of it leaves as heat. That is settled,
and a textbook is the right place to get it.

The other is a measured split between two fractions. Somebody measured that, somewhere, in some
people, with some instrument, at some time. The page does not say who, or how, or when, or in
whom. Nothing on it lets you check it and nothing on it lets you disagree with it.

**A number is a different kind of claim from a mechanism, and it needs a different kind of
source.** This course does not take that figure, and neither should you.

Do the same search on one more page, so that it stops looking like a single unlucky sentence.

Section 24.7 of the same book, *Nutrition and Diet*, will hand you a figure for how many
calories make a pound of body weight. It is the most repeated number in this whole subject. It
is stated the same way, with no study behind it.

And look at what the page does with it next. It multiplies the figure out to a pound every
eighteen days and twenty pounds a year. That second step is the one this course refuses
outright. It would still refuse it if a study were produced tomorrow. A body is not a bank
account that compounds.

The section on conservation of energy and the boundary has already told you why Book 0 will not
give you a figure of that kind. What a kilogram of change in a body is made of decides the
answer. Nobody quoting the figure has told you what it is made of.

Now the clean case, and you can do this one on paper.

Take a plate you have actually eaten from. Rice, a bowl of dal, a spoonful of oil in the tadka,
and a raw onion sliced on the side. Follow each of the four, using only what this section has
given you.

Start with the rice. Rice is mostly starch, which is a long chain of sugar molecules. The gut
cuts the chain into single sugars, and glucose is the one to watch. It crosses the gut wall into
the blood. The blood carries it to the liver. From there it reaches cells all over the body, and
a cell that takes it in breaks it down and makes coins.

What the body has no immediate use for does not stay in the blood. Some goes into the near
store, as glycogen in the liver and the muscles. Some goes into the far store, turned into
triglyceride in adipose tissue.

Now the dal. Dal is largely protein, and protein is a chain too, made of amino acids. The gut
cuts it into single amino acids and those cross the wall. On the far side they go one of two
ways: into new proteins, or broken down further to make coins like anything else.

Now the oil. Oil is triglyceride, and a whole triglyceride molecule will not cross the gut wall.
So it is broken into its pieces first. The pieces cross. On the far side they are put back
together into triglyceride again. That triglyceride goes into the body, to be used or stored.

Now the onion, and this is the one the whole illustration is for.

A lot of a raw onion is fibre. Your body makes no enzyme that can break most plant fibre down,
so none of it is cut small enough to cross. It travels the length of the gut and leaves. While
it is inside it is doing something useful: it helps move everything else along. But it never
became part of you, and it handed you no energy.

Now count. Four things went on that plate. Three of them handed energy across the gut wall and
one of them did not.

So look at what happens if you price that plate out of a food table. The table gives a figure
for onion. Add it in, and you have counted energy that never crossed. Your total is a number
about the plate. It is not a number about the person.

That gap between what is eaten and what is absorbed is why the section on measuring food energy
spent so long on it. It is the single most useful thing here.

Here is what to do with it, starting today. When somebody hands you a figure for what a person
ate, ask three questions in this order. Where was it measured — at the mouth, or anywhere else?
Was it measured at all, or looked up in a table? And what happened to it between the plate and
the blood?

The third question is the one that is never answered. It is also the one that decides whether
the number means what the sentence around it says.

**Where this picture breaks.** The coin is a picture of what ATP does and not of how much of it there is. A cell does not keep
a purse. It makes coins and spends them almost at once. This section gives you no number of
coins, no rate of spending and no size for any of it.

The plate is a made-up plate, chosen because it is an ordinary one. Only the behaviour of the
four foods comes from the book. Nothing here tells you how much energy any of them carries. The
section on measuring food energy holds the only real food-energy figure Book 0 gives you.

Near store and far store describe the size of the two and nothing else. This section gives no
rule about which one is drawn on first, or how fast either fills or empties.

Catabolism and anabolism are described here for a whole body. Nothing in this section tells you
which tissue is doing which at any given moment, and both are going on everywhere.

The onion is an example of fibre, not a claim about onions. How much of any particular food is
fibre is a measured quantity, and this section does not carry one.

The dead end at section 24.1 was found on 23 September 2026 in the edition of the book at that
address. A later edition may add a citation to the sentence. The habit — find the number, then
go and find where it came from — survives the edition. The particular result may not.

Everything in this section is a direction and none of it is a size. It carries no rate, no
efficiency, no fraction and no amount. Any question whose answer is a number is outside it.

**Must know points for you.**

- What a person eats and what a person takes up are two different amounts. Only the first one ever gets written down. Some of what is eaten never crosses the gut wall at all, because the body makes no tool for most plant fibre. So a figure for intake is a figure about food. Say that out loud before anyone does sums on it as though it were a figure about a body.
- Adipose tissue holds triglyceride until it is needed. That is a job, in the same way that making bile is the liver's job, and the textbook states it in those words. So describe fat storage as a store doing its work. The other way of saying it is no more accurate, and it costs the person in front of you something.
- ATP is what a body moves energy with. It is not where a body keeps it. A cell cannot hold much loose energy, and it holds very little ATP at any moment. The stores are glycogen and triglyceride. Correct a trainee who says energy is stored as ATP, because that error spoils every later sentence they write about stores.
- Somebody will hand you a figure for how many kilocalories make a kilogram of body weight. Book 0 does not give you one. The answer depends on what the kilogram of change is made of, and nobody quoting the figure has told you that. Ask what the change is made of before you accept any sum built on it.
- Catabolism and anabolism are directions, not departments. Both run in the same cells at the same time, and a person is never in one or the other. When somebody says a body is in a building state or a breaking state, ask what is being built and what is being broken. The answer is always both.
- Metabolism is the name for every chemical reaction in a body added together. So a drink that boosts your metabolism has named nothing at all. Ask which reaction, in which direction, in which tissue, and how the change was measured. Saying that to a journalist is more use than arguing about evidence, because there is no claim for the evidence to be about.
- Direction is the whole of what you have here, and size is no part of it. Spare energy goes to glycogen and to fat. How much, how fast, and how much of it survives each step are not in this section at all. Refuse any sum that needs one of those numbers and does not say where it was measured.
