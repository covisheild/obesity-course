# F4 · Argument: premise, inference, conclusion

**Definition.** An argument is a set of statements offered as reasons, called premises, together with a further
statement they are meant to establish, called the conclusion. The move from the first to the
second is the inference.

An argument can fail in exactly two independent ways. A premise can be false. Or the inference
can fail to carry, even when every premise is true.

Separating those two failures is the whole skill. True premises plus a broken inference
establish nothing. A sound inference with one false premise can be repaired.

**In plain terms.** Someone hands you a claim and asks you to accept it. Along with the claim, they give you reasons
— these are the premises. The claim itself is the conclusion. The step from premises to
conclusion is the inference, the move meant to carry you from one to the other.

An argument can fail in two separate ways, and you test each on its own. A premise can turn out
false. Or the inference can fail to carry, even when every premise is true. Mixing the two tests
wastes time. People argue for years over whether a premise is true, when the real fault sits in
the inference and not in the premise at all.

Ask the two questions in order. First: if every premise were true, would the conclusion follow?
Then, only then: are the premises actually true? Keeping the two apart is most of the skill. A
true premise paired with a broken inference proves nothing. A sound inference with one false
premise can often be mended, once you see the fault plainly.

**Illustration.** Here is a claim you will meet often once you work in obesity policy. Take it apart, on paper, in
three lines.

- *Premise one:* obesity happens when a person takes in more energy than they use.
- *Premise two:* how much a person eats is something they choose.
- *Conclusion:* obesity is a matter of personal choice.

Test the inference first. Grant both premises. Say them aloud as though you believed every word.
Now ask: does the conclusion follow?

It does not. Premise two says intake is chosen. The conclusion needs something far stronger —
that intake is *freely* chosen. It would have to be untouched by price, by what sits on the
shelf nearby, by advertising, by sleep, by the biology that drives appetite.

Somewhere between the premise and the conclusion, "is a choice" has quietly turned into "is only
a choice". That swap is the fault. It sits in the inference, and it stands whether or not the
premises themselves are true.

Now test the premises, one at a time. Premise one holds up as plain accounting — energy in
against energy out. Premise two is partly true and badly incomplete; it leaves out everything
that shapes the choice before the person ever makes it.

Notice what you just did. You reached "this argument does not work" without settling either
premise. Run the inference test first, always. It is quicker, and it keeps you out of a long
fight over facts that were never going to decide the question anyway.

**Where this picture breaks.** This three-line shape fits an argument laid out in the open, premise by premise. Most arguments
you meet in a hostile room are not laid out at all.

The premise doing the real work is usually the one left unspoken, left out because saying it
aloud would expose it. Nobody in the claim above says out loud that eating sits untouched by
price, or by what is on the shelf nearby. That premise stays hidden, and the argument leans on
it anyway.

So when you cannot find the fault in an argument, the likely reason is simple. You have not yet
written down the premise the speaker left out. Look there first.

**Must know points for you.**

- True premises do not make a conclusion true. Whether the premises are true and whether the inference carries are separate questions, and you need both to hold before the argument does any work.
- Test the two failure modes in order: ask first whether the conclusion follows, then ask whether the premises are true.
- The shape of an argument does not change from one country to the next. What changes is which premise gets left unspoken. In Indian food policy debate, the commonest unspoken premise is that people already know what is in their food.
- Grant every premise aloud, then show the conclusion still does not follow. That move costs you nothing. It cannot be answered with more data, and it closes the argument instead of extending it.
- Watch yourself when you are handed an argument you already agree with. Check its inference as hard as you would if you disagreed, because left alone you will find that you do not.
- This gives you the shape of an argument and the point where it breaks. It does not tell you whether a premise is true — that needs evidence — or whether a conclusion matters, which needs judgement.

**Exercise 1** (critique). Take this claim: "Countries that eat more packaged food have more obesity, so packaged food
