# B4 · Energy units: joule, kilojoule, calorie, kilocalorie

**Definition.** The joule is the SI unit of energy. Within the SI the joule stands in a fixed relation to the
base units, written J = kg m^2 s^-2, where J is the joule, kg the kilogram, m the metre and s
the second.

A kilojoule is one thousand joules. The prefix kilo means one thousand.

The calorie is not an SI unit. Its size in joules is fixed by agreement, not by measurement,
and two agreements are in use at once. The thermochemical calorie is 4.184 joules exactly. The
International Table calorie, settled at the Fifth International Conference on the Properties
of Steam in July 1956, is 4.1868 joules.

A kilocalorie is one thousand calories. It is the calorie used to express the energy content
of foods, and in food writing the prefix kilo is usually dropped.

In India, where a pre-packaged food has to carry nutritional information, the energy is
declared in kilocalories. Regulation 5(3)(b) of the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and
Display) Regulations, 2020 requires the label to carry the energy value in kcal. That figure
is given per 100 g, or per 100 ml, or per single consumption pack. The same clause separately
requires, per serve, the percentage contribution to the Recommended Dietary Allowance, worked
out on a basis of 2000 kcal of energy a day. Those regulations require no kilojoule figure
anywhere.

The duty is not universal. Regulation 5(3)(c) exempts thirteen classes of food from mandatory
nutritional labelling altogether, among them unprocessed single-ingredient products, drinking
water, herbs and spices, salt and salt substitutes, table-top sweeteners, coffee, tea and
herbal infusions, and vinegars. Regulation 8(1) exempts any package whose surface area is not
more than 100 square centimetres. A packet of salt, or a sachet smaller than 100 square
centimetres, carries no energy declaration at all.

The amounts the panel carries are listed in regulation 5(3)(b)(ii): protein, carbohydrate
with total sugars and added sugars, the fats with cholesterol, and sodium. Some of those
entries are conditional, the clause's provisos saying when they are required. Dietary fibre,
polyols and organic acid are not on the list at all, although all three carry an energy
factor under 5(3)(e)(i).

Regulation 5(3)(e)(i) of the same regulations fixes the factors by which the declared energy
is calculated rather than measured. They are: carbohydrate 4 kcal/g, polyols other than
erythritol 2 kcal/g, erythritol 0 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, fat 9 kcal/g, alcohol 7 kcal/g,
organic acid 3 kcal/g and dietary fibre 2 kcal/g.


**In plain terms.** Energy is measured, so energy has a unit. The SI unit is the joule.

One joule is a small amount. A school meal runs to more than a million of them, which is why you
almost never see a food figure written in joules. A thousand joules is one kilojoule. Kilo means
a thousand, here and everywhere else.

Now the calorie, and this is where the care is needed. The calorie is not part of the SI. Its
size was decided by committees, and two of them decided differently.

One committee fixed the thermochemical calorie at 4.184 joules. Another fixed the International
Table calorie at 4.1868 joules. Both numbers are exact, because both are decisions. Nobody went
to a laboratory and got 4.184. That is the whole difference between this section and the
arithmetic sections before it.

A kilocalorie is a thousand calories. It is the unit used for the energy in food.

And here is the habit that saves you. In food work people write calories when they mean
kilocalories. The American guide to the SI says so in as many words: the prefix kilo is usually
left off. So when a packet, a paper or a scheme document says calories, read kilocalories. Then
check the size of the number to be sure.


**Illustration.** Start with the mistake, because it is the one waiting for you.

Open the National Food Security Act, 2013, and go to Schedule II. It sets nutritional
standards for meals. Find the row for lower primary classes. The hot cooked meal is set at 450.

Take that 450 as calories and turn it into joules.

```working
    450 times 4.184 = 1,882.8
```

A meal of 1,882.8 joules. That is under two kilojoules, which is nothing at all. Something has
gone wrong, and it is not the arithmetic.

Go back to the table and read the head of the column instead of the number under it. It says
Calories, and then, in brackets, Kcal. The Act named its unit and you read past it.

So the meal is 450 kilocalories. Do it again properly, one line at a time.

```working
    450 kilocalories, first into calories
    450 times 1,000 = 4,50,000
    now into joules
    4,50,000 times 4.184 = 18,82,800
    and into kilojoules
    18,82,800 divided by 1,000 = 1,882.8
```

Look at the first answer and the last. The digits are the same and the unit is not. Kilocalories
to kilojoules uses the same 4.184 as calories to joules, because both sides of the sum were
multiplied by a thousand.

```working
    450 times 4.184 = 1,882.8
```

Now the second thing, which nobody tells you and which you need the day you compare two papers.
Take the upper primary figure from the same table, 700 kilocalories, and convert it with each of
the two calories in turn.

```working
    700 times 4.184 = 2,928.8
    700 times 4.1868 = 2,930.76
```

Two answers, from one figure, both correct.

```working
    2,930.76 minus 2,928.8 = 1.96
```

Just under two kilojoules apart. The gap is in the fourth digit. The Act gave you two digits, so
both answers round to 2,900 kilojoules and the choice made no difference here.

It makes a difference the moment you are checking somebody else's conversion digit for digit. If
their kilojoule figure and yours part company in the fourth digit, neither of you has slipped.
You used different calories.


**Where this picture breaks.** The two conversion factors are exact and they stay exact. They are definitions, so no experiment
can revise them and only a standards body can replace them.

What a conversion cannot do is improve the number you started with. It renames an amount. Every
error in the figure you were given is still there afterwards, carried across unchanged.

And the size check in this example works because a meal is a familiar quantity. It tells you
nothing where you have no feel for the size, which is most of a laboratory report.


**Must know points for you.**

- Nobody measured the calorie. Committees fixed it, and both of their values are exact by definition. Treat a conversion factor as a decision you can look up and date. It is not a fact about the world that a better experiment will sharpen.
- One thermochemical calorie is 4.184 joules. That is the factor to use unless a paper tells you otherwise, and the same 4.184 turns kilocalories into kilojoules.
- A second calorie is in use. The International Table calorie is 4.1868 joules. Two papers can report the same food in kilojoules and part company in the fourth digit. Check which calorie each of them used before you tell either of them they are wrong.
- When food material says calories, read kilocalories, because the prefix is usually left off in practice. Then check the size. A meal in the hundreds is kilocalories. A meal in the hundreds of joules is a reading error.
- Say the unit out loud with every energy figure you quote, in clinic and in a meeting. A factor of a thousand hides inside a dropped prefix, and the number on its own cannot warn anybody.
- Converting a figure never improves it. The factor is exact, so every error you were handed survives the conversion untouched. A two-digit figure written out to six digits after conversion has gained nothing but the look of precision.
- Schedule II of the National Food Security Act, 2013 heads its energy column Calories, with Kcal in brackets. Quote that unit whenever you quote the figure under it, because the figure alone can be read two ways.
- An Indian food packet that must show nutritional information gives its energy in kilocalories. The rule is regulation 5(3)(b) of the labelling regulations of 2020. It asks for the energy value in kcal, per 100 g or 100 ml or per single consumption pack. Per serve it asks for a different thing, the share of the Recommended Dietary Allowance. It asks for no kilojoule figure at all, so you must be able to convert. A panel is not always due: 5(3)(c) exempts thirteen classes of food, salt and tea among them. Regulation 8(1) exempts a package of 100 cm^2 or less, so salt with no panel is the rule kept.
- The energy printed on an Indian label is worked out, not measured. Regulation 5(3)(e)(i) of the same 2020 rules fixes the factors. Carbohydrate and protein are 4 kcal/g each, fat is 9 kcal/g and alcohol is 7 kcal/g. So take the grams printed on the label, multiply each by its factor, and add them up. On a food of carbohydrate, protein and fat that reproduces the declared figure. On a high-fibre or sugar-free food it will not: the panel need not print the grams of fibre, polyols or organic acid. Find out which kind of food you hold before you call a gap a mistake.
