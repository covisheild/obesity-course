# E5 · The cell, membranes, proteins and receptors

**Definition.** A cell is the smallest unit of a living thing, and cells are the basic building blocks of all
organisms. The unified cell theory holds that one or more cells make up every living thing,
that the cell is the basic unit of life, and that new cells arise from existing cells.

An animal cell, which is one kind of eukaryotic cell, is bounded by a plasma membrane: a
phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins that separates the internal contents of the cell
from its surrounding environment. Within that boundary the cytoplasm holds the organelles. The
nucleus houses the cell's DNA. Ribosomes build proteins. Mitochondria are responsible for
making adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which is the cell's main energy-carrying molecule.

A phospholipid has a head area that is hydrophilic, meaning it mixes with water, and a tail
area that is hydrophobic, meaning it does not. In water these molecules arrange themselves with
their hydrophobic tails facing each other and their hydrophilic heads facing out. The result is
a double layer whose two outer faces are hydrophilic and whose interior is hydrophobic.

The plasma membrane controls the passage of organic molecules, ions, water and oxygen into and
out of the cell. Cells exclude some substances, take in others, and excrete still others, all
in controlled quantities. To move a substance against its concentration gradient the cell must
use energy, and specific carrier proteins or pumps carry out that movement.

Proteins are amino acid polymers arranged in a linear sequence. A protein's shape is critical
to its function, and many different types of chemical bond maintain that shape. Changes in
temperature, acidity or exposure to chemicals may change the shape permanently, which loses the
function. Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts in biochemical reactions. Each enzyme is
specific for the substrate on which it acts, and every enzyme increases the rate of its
reaction.

A ligand is a molecule that binds another specific molecule, in some cases delivering a signal
in the process. Receptors are protein molecules in the target cell or on its surface that bind
ligand, and a specific ligand typically has a specific receptor that binds only that ligand.
Internal receptors sit in the cytoplasm and respond to hydrophobic ligand molecules that are
able to travel across the plasma membrane. Cell-surface receptors span the plasma membrane and
perform signal transduction, by which a signal outside the cell is converted into a signal
inside it. Binding changes the shape of the receptor, and the changed shape is what does the
work.

**In plain terms.** Everything alive is made of cells. A cell is the smallest unit of a living thing. You are made of
them, and so is the wheat that went into your chapati.

A cell is a bag of watery jelly with a skin round it. The skin has a name: the plasma membrane.
It is the most important thing in this section, because it is what decides what gets in and what
stays out.

Floating in the jelly are small parts, each doing one job. Three of them matter later in this
book.

The nucleus is the compartment that holds the cell's instructions, which are written in its DNA.
The section on genes is where that gets taken up.

The ribosomes are the machines that build proteins. You will see in a moment why that matters.

The mitochondria make a small molecule called adenosine triphosphate, written ATP, which the cell
uses to carry energy from one reaction to another. The next section is about what it does.

Now the membrane, and take this slowly, because the whole of the drug half of this course rests
on it.

The membrane is built out of one kind of molecule, and that molecule has two ends that behave
differently. One end mixes with water. The other end will not mix with water and mixes with
grease instead.

Put a lot of them in water and they sort themselves out. The water-hating ends turn inwards and
huddle together, away from the water. The water-loving ends turn outwards, where the water is.
What you get is a sheet two molecules thick. That sheet is called a bilayer, which is just a word
for two layers back to back.

So a membrane is wet on both faces and greasy in the middle.

That single fact does most of the work. A molecule that dissolves in grease can slip through the
greasy middle on its own. A molecule that dissolves in water cannot, and has to be carried
across. The carrying is done by proteins that sit in the membrane and take things through. And
when the cell has to push something the way it would not go by itself, it has to spend energy to
do it.

Now proteins, because almost everything in this section turns out to be one.

A protein is a chain. The links of the chain are called amino acids, and there is a small fixed
set of kinds. The chain folds up into a shape, and the shape is decided by the order of the
links.

Here is the sentence to keep. **The shape is what the protein does.** Not the chain, the shape.
Change the shape and you change what it does, and a shape that is pulled apart far enough stops
working altogether. Heat does that. So does acid. That is why an egg white turns solid in a hot
pan and never turns back.

One kind of protein has a job worth naming on its own. An enzyme is a protein that makes one
particular reaction go faster. It works because of its shape: the thing it acts on fits into it.
Every enzyme speeds its reaction up. That is as far as this book goes with enzymes, and it is
enough.

Now the last idea, and it is the reason this section exists.

Some proteins are shaped so that one particular molecule fits into them. The molecule that fits
is called the ligand. The protein it fits into is called the receptor.

When the ligand sits in the receptor, the receptor changes shape. The new shape does something
the old shape could not do. That is the whole event, and it is what "acts on a cell" means every
time you meet the phrase.

There are two places to put a receptor, and you can now work out why there are two.

A water-hating ligand crosses the membrane on its own, so its receptor can sit inside the cell.
A water-loving ligand cannot cross, so its receptor sits in the membrane itself, sticking out on
both sides. The ligand lands on the outside end and never enters. The inside end changes shape
and carries the message in.

That is the floor. Every drug in the later books, and every signal the body makes for itself,
works by fitting into something and changing its shape. A reader who cannot picture that cannot
follow any of it, and a reader who can has most of what those books assume.

**Illustration.** Start where the check failed, because the failure is where the idea actually is.

Nearly everyone who has met the membrane once remembers the phrase selectively permeable. Go
and find it.

Open OpenStax Biology 2e, section 5.1, Components and Structure, at
https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/5-1-components-and-structure. Open your browser's
find box. Type the words selectively permeable and press enter.

Nothing. The section that describes the membrane does not use the phrase anywhere.

Now search the same page for the word exclude. One sentence comes up.

> Cells exclude some substances, take in others, and excrete still others, all in controlled
> quantities.

That is the behaviour. It says the membrane picks. It does not say how the membrane picks, and
the phrase you went looking for would not have told you either. A label is not a mechanism, and
a phrase you remember from a lecture is not a citation.

So build the mechanism yourself, and start with your hands.

Take a glass of water. Pour in a spoonful of cooking oil. Stir it hard, then stop and watch.
The oil comes back together and sits apart from the water. You knew that before you opened any
book.

Now read the phospholipid paragraph on the page you already have open, with that glass in front
of you.

> ...phospholipids arrange themselves with their hydrophobic tails facing each other and their
> hydrophilic heads facing out.

Hydrophilic means water-loving, and hydrophobic means water-hating. Each of these molecules has
one of each end. So they do exactly what the oil did in your glass, with one difference. They
cannot get fully clear of the water, because one end of each molecule likes it.

Draw the result on paper. Two rows of molecules. Tails pointing at each other in the middle,
heads facing out on both sides. Two wet faces with a greasy layer between them.

That picture makes a prediction, and you can go and test it. If the middle is greasy, a
water-hating molecule should be able to walk straight through, and a water-loving one should
not.

Leave this chapter. Open section 9.1, Signaling Molecules and Cellular Receptors, at
https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-1-signaling-molecules-and-cellular-receptors.
Search inside it for the word hydrophobic.

> ...respond to hydrophobic ligand molecules that are able to travel across the plasma
> membrane.

Read the end of that again. Water-hating molecules travel across the membrane. Your prediction
holds, and the sentence that confirms it sits four chapters away from the one describing the
membrane. Nobody put the two next to each other. You did.

Now the part the rest of this course stands on.

A signal arriving at a cell is a molecule. The word for it is the ligand, which means the thing
that binds. The protein it binds to is the receptor.

And there are two places to put a receptor, for exactly the reason you have just worked out.

Take the water-hating case first. The ligand crosses the membrane by itself, so its receptor
waits inside the cell, in the jelly.

Now the water-loving case. That ligand cannot cross. So its receptor is built through the wall
instead.

> This type of receptor spans the plasma membrane and performs signal transduction, through
> which an extracellular signal is converted into an intracellular signal.

Strip that down. The receptor goes right through the membrane. The ligand lands on the outside
end and stays outside. The receptor changes shape. The inside end, which is now a different
shape, starts something happening in the cell.

So the message got in and the messenger never did.

Here is what you can do with this, starting today. Take any claim that a substance acts on the
body and ask three questions in order. What is the molecule? What does it bind? What changes
when it binds?

The third question is the one that does the work. A claim that cannot answer it has described
something that was present, not something that acted.

**Where this picture breaks.** The glass of oil and water gets you the greasy middle and nothing else. A membrane is not a
puddle. It is two ordered rows of molecules, and it is fluid, so the molecules in it slide
about.

Water-hating in, water-loving out is a rule with real exceptions, and the book says so itself.
The membrane controls the passage of water too, and water is the most water-loving thing there
is. Take the rule as telling you what usually crosses unaided, not as a law about every
molecule.

The key-in-a-lock picture for a ligand and a receptor breaks in two places. A lock does not
change shape when the key turns, and a receptor does, and that change is the entire point. And
one key can open more than one kind of lock here: the same signal can reach several kinds of
cell.

Binding and acting are not the same event. The book says a ligand binds another molecule, in
some cases delivering a signal. In some cases. Something can sit in a receptor and do nothing,
and something can sit in it and stop the usual ligand getting in.

This whole section is about what can happen and never about how much. It carries no size, no
speed, no dose and no strength of binding. Any question that has a number in its answer is
outside it.

And the reading method used here has a limit worth naming. Finding a sentence in one chapter
that fits a picture you built in another is a good check, and it is not proof. It tells you the
book is consistent with your picture. It does not tell you the picture is right.

**Must know points for you.**

- A substance being present in a tissue is not the same as it acting there. It acts where something binds it. So ask which cells carry the receptor before you accept any claim about where a medicine works. The answer is often a much smaller set of cells than the claim implies.
- Ask three questions of every mechanism claim, in this order. What is the molecule, what does it bind, and what changes when it binds. A sentence that cannot answer the third has told you something was present and nothing about it acting.
- Binding and acting are two claims, not one. A molecule can sit in a receptor and do nothing, or sit in it and keep the usual ligand out. So "it binds the receptor" never settles what a substance does. Say that out loud when a paper or a sales sheet runs the two together.
- A protein's shape is what it does, so anything that changes the shape changes the job. Heat, acid and some chemicals do exactly that, and far enough is permanent. So before you accept that a protein can be given by mouth, ask what the stomach does to it. Unfolding proteins with acid is part of how the stomach digests food.
- Refuse any claim that a mechanism settles whether an effect is big enough to matter. A mechanism tells you what can happen and never how much. This section carries no size, no speed and no dose. A mechanism that is real and a mechanism that changes an outcome are different findings, and only measurement tells them apart.
- When a product claims to work at the cellular level, ask what it binds and what changes when it does. The phrase names no molecule and no receptor, so it commits to nothing. Saying that plainly to a journalist is more useful than arguing about the evidence behind it.
- Teach the two receptor places before you teach a single drug. A water-hating signal crosses the membrane and meets its receptor inside; everything else is handled by a receptor built through the wall. A trainee holding that can place a new drug the first time they meet it, instead of memorising it.
- An enzyme makes one reaction go faster, and its shape is the reason. Somebody will tell you that a food or a supplement speeds up a process in the body. Ask which enzyme, and ask how the rate was measured. Without those two answers the claim has no content you can check.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). Without looking back, write down what happens from the moment a signal molecule reaches a cell
to the moment something changes inside it. Do it twice: once for a water-hating signal and once
for a water-loving one.

Then say, in one sentence, why the two go different ways.

**Exercise 2** (critique). A company sheet for a herbal capsule says this.

> The active molecule has been shown to bind the same receptor as the prescription medicine. It
> therefore has the same action, without the side effects.

Say what is wrong, and say what you would ask for. The capsule and the sheet are made up for
this exercise.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A first-year resident has ten minutes and a whiteboard. They can recite that insulin acts on
cells, and they cannot say what "acts on" means.

Teach them the membrane and the receptor so that they leave able to place a drug they have
never met.
