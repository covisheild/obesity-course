# C2 · Rearranging an equation

**Definition.** An equation is a statement that the expression on the left of the equals sign and the
expression on the right stand for the same number. It is not an instruction to work the left
side out.

If two expressions stand for the same number, then applying the same operation to both of
them gives two expressions that still stand for the same number. Four operations therefore
leave the solutions of an equation unchanged.

- adding the same quantity to both sides
- subtracting the same quantity from both sides
- multiplying both sides by the same quantity that is not zero
- dividing both sides by the same quantity that is not zero

To solve an equation for a letter is to reach an equation with that letter alone on one side,
and no occurrence of it on the other.

An operation applied to a side that is a sum applies to the whole of that sum, not to one
term of it.

Dividing both sides by an expression that may be zero can destroy a solution, so the case
where that expression is zero is treated on its own.

**In plain terms.** This section stands on the one before it, where a letter became a number you do not know yet.
Now you go and find out which number it is.

Start with what the equals sign is saying. It says the thing on its left and the thing on its
right are the same number. It is not telling you to work the left side out and write the answer
on the right.

Picture a pair of scales with a pan on each side, sitting level. Take three kilograms off one
pan and the scales tip. Take three off both and they stay level.

That is the whole rule.

> Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.

Now use it. Suppose you are told this.

```working
    x + 7 = 12
```

You want x on its own. The 7 is in the way. It got there by being added, so take 7 off both
sides.

```working
    x + 7 minus 7 = 12 minus 7
    x = 5
```

Check it by putting the 5 back where x was.

```working
    5 plus 7 = 12
```

True, so the 5 is right. That check costs you one line and catches almost everything.

Multiplying works the same way. Suppose you are told this.

```working
    3x = 21
```

The 3 got there by multiplying, so divide both sides by 3.

```working
    3x divided by 3 = 21 divided by 3
    x = 7
```

Two things to hold on to from here on.

The first is order. When more than one thing is in the way, undo them in the reverse of the
order they were done. Take this line.

```working
    2x + 3 = 11
```

The x was multiplied by 2 first and had 3 added second. So undo the adding first and the
multiplying second.

```working
    2x + 3 minus 3 = 11 minus 3
    2x = 8
    2x divided by 2 = 8 divided by 2
    x = 4
```

Check.

```working
    2 times 4 = 8
    8 plus 3 = 11
```

The second is that a side which is a sum has to be treated whole. If you divide a side by 9,
you divide every term on it by 9, not the first one you come to. Brackets are how you say that
on paper.

There is one more use for the rule, and it is the one you will use most. Sometimes nothing is
unknown. A formula ties several quantities together, and you want a different one of them on
its own. Getting one letter on its own like that is also called making it the subject.

Take the rule that ties a distance, a speed and a time. The distance is the speed times the
time.

```working
    d = s t
```

To get t by itself, divide both sides by s.

```working
    d divided by s = s t divided by s
    d divided by s = t
```

The time is the distance divided by the speed. You did not measure anything new. You moved the
same statement into the shape that answers your question.

One warning goes with that. Never divide both sides by a letter unless you know it is not zero,
and the same goes for multiplying by one. Dividing by zero is not a move, and a letter can hide
one.

**Illustration.** You met the rule an Indian food label uses for its energy figure in the section before this
one. Four kilocalories for every gram of carbohydrate, four for every gram of protein, nine
for every gram of fat.

```working
    E = 4c + 4p + 9f
```

A packet reaches you with its fat line torn off. The panel still shows the energy as 468
kilocalories in every 100 grams, the carbohydrate as 60 grams and the protein as 12 grams.
Those three figures are made up for this problem; the factors are not. Somebody wants the
fat.

Here is the working that comes back first. It is wrong.

```working
    E = 4c + 4p + 9f
    divide both sides by 9
    f = E divided by 9, minus 4c, minus 4p
    468 divided by 9 = 52
    52 minus 240 minus 48 = -236
    so the fat is -236 grams
```

Minus two hundred and thirty-six grams of fat. No packet holds that, and no packet holds a
negative anything.

Read the third line again. The division by 9 was applied to one term on the right and to
nothing else. The right side is a sum of three terms, and dividing that side means dividing
all of it. Divide only the 9f and you have changed what the right side says. The two sides
are then no longer equal, and the statement has stopped being true.

Do it in an order that keeps the sum whole. Get the 9f on its own first, by taking the other
two terms off both sides.

```working
    E minus 4c minus 4p = 9f
```

Now the side carrying f is a single term. Dividing by 9 is safe, because there is nothing
else on that side for the 9 to miss.

```working
    the whole of E minus 4c minus 4p, divided by 9 = f
```

Put the numbers in, one line at a time.

```working
    4 times 60 = 240
    4 times 12 = 48
    240 plus 48 = 288
    468 minus 288 = 180
    180 divided by 9 = 20
```

Twenty grams of fat.

Now check it, the way you check every rearrangement. Put the 20 back into the rule you
started from and see whether the energy comes out as the panel says.

```working
    4 times 60 = 240
    4 times 12 = 48
    9 times 20 = 180
    240 plus 48 plus 180 = 468
```

468 kilocalories, which is the figure on the panel. The rearrangement held.

Notice what the check would have done to the wrong answer. Put -236 in for f and the energy
comes out nowhere near 468. One line would have caught it before anybody said it out loud.

**Where this picture breaks.** Rearranging moves a quantity from one side to the other. It does not improve it.

The 20 grams you recover is only as good as the 468 and the 60 and the 12 you put in. If the
panel rounded them, your answer carries that rounding, and it carries it through a division
that can make it bigger.

The working here also assumes the packet holds nothing but carbohydrate, protein and fat
that carries energy. A food with dietary fibre, polyols or alcohol in it has more terms in
the line. Each of those terms carries its own factor. Leave them out and the fat you
recover is wrong by whatever they contribute.

**Must know points for you.**

- An equals sign states that two things are the same number. It is not an instruction to compute the left side, which is what it means on a calculator and nowhere else. Read it as a claim and the rest of this section follows; read it as a button and none of it does.
- Do the same thing to both sides, and write both sides of that step on the same line. A step that shows only what happened to one side is the step where the error hides.
- A side that is a sum is divided or multiplied as a whole. Dividing one term of a sum and leaving the rest is the commonest way a rearrangement breaks. The answer it gives is usually wildly wrong rather than slightly wrong.
- Check every rearrangement by putting your answer back into the equation you started from. One line, every time, before the number leaves your desk.
- Any one quantity in a formula can be recovered from the others. So a register with a missing column is not always a register with missing information. Say that in a meeting, instead of asking for the fieldwork to be done again.
- Never divide both sides by a letter unless you know that letter is not zero. Divide a formula through by a quantity that turns out to be zero and the answer has nothing behind it. Nothing on the page shows that it happened.
- A recovered number carries the rounding of everything you put in, and a division can make that rounding larger. Say which figures you started from whenever you quote a quantity you worked backwards to.
- Teach the order before the moves. Undo what was done last, first. A trainee who learns rearranging as tricks for moving symbols across the sign has learned something fragile. It fails the moment the equation has brackets in it.

**10.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

