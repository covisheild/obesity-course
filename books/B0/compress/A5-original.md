### A5 · Ratios, rates and proportions

*B0-R0-C05 · derivable*

**Definition.** A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division, and is written a to b. It has no
unit, because the units cancel.

A proportion is a part divided by the whole it came from. It runs from zero to one, and a
percentage is a proportion multiplied by one hundred.

A rate is a count divided by the quantity of something else it was counted over: time, or
people, or both at once. Its denominator is part of the number and not a note attached to it, so
a rate quoted without saying per what, and over what period, is not a quantity.

Two rates may be compared only when their denominators are the same. Five kilograms per person
per month and thirty-five kilograms per household per month cannot be set against each other
until the size of a household is supplied.

**In plain terms.** Three words get used as though they meant the same thing. They do not, and the differences are
where the mistakes live.

A ratio sets two quantities of the same kind side by side. Twelve grams against twenty grams is a
ratio of twelve to twenty, or three to five. It has no unit, because the grams cancel out.

A proportion is a part out of the whole it came from. If three of every five children in a room
had the meal, the proportion is three fifths, or 0.6. Multiply by a hundred and you have a
percentage. A proportion can never be more than one.

A rate is a count divided by the thing it was counted over. Usually that is time, or people, or
both. Five kilograms a month is a rate. Fifteen deaths per thousand people per year is a rate.

Now the point of the whole section. The bottom of a rate is not a footnote. It is part of the
number. Change it and you have a different quantity, even when the digits look the same.

Five kilograms per person per month and thirty-five kilograms per household per month look like a
small number and a big one. They are not comparable at all until somebody tells you how many
people are in a household. Say the denominator out loud every time: per what, and over how long.
Do that and this whole class of error disappears.

**Illustration.** Go back to section 3(1) of the National Food Security Act, 2013, which you opened two sections
ago. It gives a person in a priority household five kilograms of foodgrains per person per
month. This time read the sentence after it.

A proviso just below sets a different figure for households covered by the Antyodaya Anna
Yojana. It is thirty-five kilograms of foodgrains per household per month. That figure applies to the extent the Central Government specifies for each State
under that scheme.

Here is the sentence somebody writes.

> Antyodaya households get seven times as much as priority households.

Look at where that seven came from. Thirty-five divided by five. The arithmetic is perfect and
the claim is meaningless, because the five is per person and the thirty-five is per household.
Two different denominators went into one division.

Make them the same and the picture changes. Take a household of six people.

```
    5 kilograms per person per month, times 6 people
    = 30 kilograms per household per month
```

Now both numbers are per household per month, so they can be set beside each other.

```
    35 against 30
    35 minus 30 = 5 kilograms more
    5 divided by 30 = 0.1666..., so about 17 per cent more
```

Seventeen per cent more, not seven times as much.

Now change the household and watch the answer move.

```
    household of 7:   5 times 7 = 35, so the two are identical
    household of 3:   5 times 3 = 15, so 35 is more than twice as much
    household of 10:  5 times 10 = 50, so 35 is less
```

The comparison does not have one answer. It depends entirely on the denominator that the
original sentence dropped. That is not a quibble about wording. It is the difference between
the Antyodaya entitlement being generous and it being worse than the ordinary one.

One more thing to take from this, because it comes up in every table you will read. Rates for
uncommon events are written per thousand or per hundred thousand people rather than per person.
The only reason is to avoid a string of zeros after the point. Fifteen per thousand and 0.015
per person are the same rate. So when two tables disagree, check the denominators before you
check anything else. That is where they usually differ, and it is the cheapest thing to check.

**Where this picture breaks.** Making the denominators match is arithmetic. Deciding which denominator is the right one is not,
and this section cannot help you with it.

A per-household entitlement and a per-person one are aimed at different things. One assumes a
household shares what it gets. The other does not. Convert between them with an average
household size and you get a number you can compare. You have also assumed that every household
is average. Real ones are not, and the households a food entitlement matters most to are usually
the furthest from it.

So the conversion above is a tool for catching a false comparison. It is not a way of deciding
who is better off. If somebody asks which scheme is more generous, do not pick one. Name the
household size at which they are equal — seven, here. Then ask how many households are above it
and how many below.

And a rate per thousand people says nothing about which thousand. One figure for a state can
hold districts that differ several times over. The rate will not show you that. It was never
built to.

**Must know points for you.**

- Two rates with different denominators cannot be compared, and dividing one by the other still produces a number. That is what makes this error survive: nothing fails, and the answer looks like an answer.

- Say the denominator out loud before you use any rate: per what, and over how long. If either half is missing you do not have a quantity yet. Go and get it rather than carrying on.

- Indian entitlements and scheme figures move between per person, per household and per beneficiary without warning. The words sit in a different part of the sentence each time. Read the whole provision, including the proviso, before you take a number out of it.

- When two schemes are stated on different denominators, find the point at which they are equal and quote that. "They match at a household of seven" is a sentence nobody can argue with, and it moves the discussion to how many households are larger.

- Converting a per-household figure to a per-person one using an average household size assumes every household is average. The households a food entitlement matters most to are usually the furthest from it, so say you made that assumption.

- Asked to say which of two programmes delivers more, do not answer with the larger number. Ask what each is divided by. Getting this wrong in front of a journalist produces a headline that is out by a factor you will then spend a week correcting.

- Rates per thousand or per hundred thousand exist only to keep the zeros off the page. They are not more precise than a rate per person and they are not a different measurement. Convert freely, and check the denominator first whenever two tables disagree.
