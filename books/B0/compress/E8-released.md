# E8 · Organs and systems that matter for metabolism

**Definition.** Absorbed material leaves the gut by two different routes. Water-soluble nutrients enter the
capillary blood in the villi and travel to the liver via the hepatic portal vein. Lipid-soluble
nutrients diffuse through the plasma membrane, are packaged inside the cell, and enter the
lacteals of the villi. Lymphatic vessels then carry them to the general circulation.

The liver is the largest gland in the body. Besides its role in digestion it has many roles in
metabolism and regulation. Under the hepatic portal circulation, all blood from the alimentary
canal passes through the liver. Drugs and toxins are absorbed along with the nutrients. After
processing what the blood brings, the liver releases the nutrients other cells need back into
the blood. It stores excess glucose as glycogen and converts glycogen back into glucose.

The liver's digestive role is to produce bile and export it to the duodenum. The gallbladder
stores, concentrates and releases that bile.

The pancreas has two separate functions. As an exocrine gland it produces pancreatic juice,
containing digestive enzymes and bicarbonate ions, and delivers it to the duodenum through a
duct. As an endocrine gland it secretes hormones directly into the surrounding fluid. Its
pancreatic islets, formerly known as the islets of Langerhans, secrete glucagon, insulin,
somatostatin and pancreatic polypeptide. The alpha cell produces glucagon and the beta cell
produces insulin. Receptors located in the pancreas sense blood glucose levels, and the
pancreatic cells secrete glucagon or insulin accordingly.

An endocrine organ secretes a chemical, the hormone, into the extracellular fluid. The
interstitial fluid and the blood vessels then transport hormones throughout the body. A hormone
binds to receptors on target cells, inducing a characteristic response. Adipose tissue is among
the tissues known to produce hormones.

Elevated blood glucose stimulates the release of insulin. The primary function of insulin is to
facilitate the uptake of glucose into body cells. Insulin also stimulates the liver to convert
excess glucose into glycogen for storage, and promotes triglyceride and protein synthesis.
Skeletal muscle cells and adipose cells are the primary targets of insulin.

Low blood glucose stimulates the release of glucagon. Glucagon stimulates the liver to convert
its stores of glycogen back into glucose, which is released into the circulation. It stimulates
the liver to take up amino acids from the blood and convert them into glucose. It also
stimulates lipolysis, the breakdown of stored triglycerides into free fatty acids and glycerol.

Nothing stated here fixes how much of anything any of these organs handles.

**In plain terms.** The section on the cell and the section on metabolism gave you a cell, and then the chemistry
that goes on inside it. This one is about which parts of a body do which part of that work.

Six of them matter here. The gut, the liver, the pancreas, fat tissue, muscle and the brain.

The gut is a tube.

Until a molecule passes through the wall of the tube and into your blood, your body can do
nothing with it.

So the gut has one job. Cut food down until the pieces are small enough to go through the wall,
and then let them through. It is covered in tiny fingers, and the pieces cross into the blood
vessels inside those fingers.

What dissolves in water and what dissolves in fat leave the gut by two different roads.

Whatever dissolves in water goes into the blood there, and that blood runs straight to the liver.
Whatever dissolves in fat takes a different road and joins the blood further along. So fat
reaches the rest of the body before the liver has looked at it, and sugar does not.

Now the liver.

Every drop of blood leaving the gut goes through the liver before it goes anywhere else. All of
it.

It sits in the doorway.

It is also the near store. Sugar that nobody needs this minute is packed away there as glycogen,
which you met in the section on metabolism.

And it makes bile, which it sends down into the gut.

Next to the liver is a small bag called the gallbladder. It holds bile, concentrates it and lets
it out.

Now the pancreas, and it does two completely separate jobs. Keep them apart in your head, or
every sentence you ever read about the pancreas will confuse you.

The pancreas makes a juice full of enzymes and sends it down a tube into the gut, where the juice
helps take food apart.

Scattered through the pancreas are small islands of another kind of cell. They put chemicals
straight into the blood, and two of those chemicals run the rest of this section.

A chemical made in one part of the body that affects other parts some distance away is called a
hormone.

A hormone starts in a gland, which is an organ whose job is to make it. The gland lets it out
into the fluid around its own cells. That fluid drains into the blood, and the blood goes
everywhere.

A hormone does not travel to a destination. It goes everywhere, past everything.

It washes past every cell in the body. It does something only where there is a receptor shaped to
hold it.

Two things follow, and both matter.

First, if you want to know where a hormone acts, never ask where it went. Ask which cells carry
the receptor.

Second, the same hormone can do different things in different places. What happens next depends
on the cell it landed in, not on the hormone. So "this hormone does that" is an unfinished
sentence until it names a tissue.

Eat, and sugar crosses the gut wall into the blood.

The islands notice. When blood sugar is high, one kind of cell in them, called a beta cell,
releases insulin.

Insulin's main job is to move glucose out of the blood and into cells. It lands on a receptor.
The cell responds by bringing glucose-carrying proteins to its own surface, where they can take
glucose in.

Muscle cells and fat cells are the ones insulin acts on most.

Blood sugar falls.

The same islands notice that too. A different cell in them, called an alpha cell, releases a
different hormone. Its name is glucagon.

Glucagon does the opposite job in the same places. It tells the liver to turn its glycogen back
into glucose and push that glucose out into the blood. It tells the liver to build new glucose
out of amino acids.

Sugar up, insulin, stores fill. Sugar down, glucagon, stores empty.

Neither half is the good half. A store that only ever filled would not be a store.

Glucose is the fuel every cell prefers. But some organs can use nothing else, and the brain is
the one that matters. So blood sugar must not be allowed to fall too far. That is what glucagon
is there for.

The brain does not need insulin to take glucose in. Neither do red blood cells, the liver, the
kidneys or the lining of the gut. They carry no insulin receptors at all, and they take glucose
straight out of the blood.

Muscle and fat wait for insulin. The brain does not, and it takes what it needs first.

Fat tissue has a proper name: adipose tissue. It makes hormones of its own and puts them into the
blood. That makes it an organ of the same kind as the pancreas.

Muscle is the other tissue insulin acts on most. It is a store too, and it holds glycogen, as the
liver does.

All of it is what each part does. None of it is how much.

**Illustration.** The rule is this. Insulin is what lets sugar into cells.

The brain runs on sugar. So the brain should be the first thing to fail, and it should fail for
want of a way in.

The brain is on it. So is the liver.

The rule is right about some cells and silent about others, and nobody told you which were

Muscle and fat wait for insulin. The brain does not wait at all.

Open section 24.2, Carbohydrate Metabolism, at
https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/24-2-carbohydrate-metabolism, and

> Certain key organs, including the brain, can use only glucose as an energy source; therefore,
> it is essential that the body maintain a minimum blood glucose concentration.

The brain can burn nothing but glucose. It takes glucose out of the blood whether or not insulin
is there. So nothing protects the brain when blood sugar runs low except putting more sugar into
the blood.

And that is why there are two hormones and not one. One to put sugar away when there is plenty.
One to get it back out when there is not.

Here are the two, side by side.

```table
hormone   released when blood sugar is  made by      what it does to the stores
insulin   high                          beta cells   fills them
glucagon  low                           alpha cells  empties them
```

Take a person who eats a meal at one o'clock and does not eat again until eight. Follow the
seven hours.

One o'clock. Sugar goes into the blood, and the blood carrying it runs first to the liver.

Blood sugar rises. Beta cells release insulin, and the insulin goes everywhere in the blood.

Muscle cells and fat cells bring glucose carriers to their surface and take sugar in. The liver
packs spare sugar away as glycogen.

Four o'clock. The brain has not stopped, and neither has anything else, so blood sugar starts to
fall.

Alpha cells release glucagon.

The liver turns its glycogen back into glucose and pushes it out into the blood. It also starts
building new glucose out of amino acids.

Eight o'clock. Blood sugar is still in a workable range and the brain has never gone short.

The store filling and the store emptying are both the mechanism working. Neither one is a
failure, and neither one is a success.

Everything absorbed from the gut in water goes through the liver before the rest of the body
sees it. All of it. So whenever somebody tells you that a substance taken by mouth does what
that substance does when it is injected, you have one question ready.

What did the liver do to it first?

The answer is often that nobody checked.

**Where this picture breaks.** Six organs is a working list and not the whole body.

The table gives no amount, no speed and no threshold at which anything happens.

Insulin and glucagon are not the only hormones the islands make, and they are not the only
hormones acting on these stores.

The brain not needing insulin does not mean the brain is untouched by insulin. What the source
establishes is narrower: it takes glucose without it.

The two roads out of the gut describe a route and nothing else.

The seven hours are a shape, not a measurement. The order of events is what the sources carry.

Any question whose answer is a number is outside this section.

What changes when part of the loop stops working is a later subject, and nothing here settles
it.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Insulin is what lets sugar into cells" is wrong for several of the cells that matter most. The brain, the liver, the kidneys, red blood cells and the lining of the gut carry no insulin receptors and take glucose without it. So make any claim about insulin name its tissue.
- A hormone does not travel to a target. It goes everywhere in the blood and acts where a receptor holds it. Ask which cells carry the receptor, and what those cells do when it binds.
- So "this hormone causes that" is unfinished until it names a tissue. Ask for the tissue before you accept the claim.
- Everything taken up from the gut in water passes through the liver before the rest of the body sees it. All of it, every time.
- A store filling and a store emptying are both the mechanism working. Insulin fills, glucagon empties, and both run in the same person every day. So when weight comes back after it fell, say what the section has given you. A store fills under one signal and empties under another, and that loop runs the same way in every person. This section does not tell you which signals, or by how much. And it never licenses a sentence about the person's character.
- Adipose tissue makes hormones of its own and puts them into the blood. That makes it an organ with a job, not a bag that things end up in.
- The pancreas does two separate jobs and a trainee who merges them misreads every pancreas sentence after that. One job sends a juice full of enzymes down a tube into the gut. The other puts hormones straight into the blood.
- Nothing here carries an organ's share of energy use, a concentration in blood, or a rate. Refuse any argument that uses it that way.
