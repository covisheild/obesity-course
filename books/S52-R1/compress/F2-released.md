# F2 · Reading a figure, and the ways a figure misleads

**Definition.** Reading a figure means settling four things before its shape. What one mark stands for. What
each axis measures, and in what unit. Where each axis starts, and how it is spaced. Where the
numbers came from.

A length carries a value only when it is measured from zero. So the value axis of a bar chart
must start at zero. The value axis is the one a bar's length is read against: the side axis when
bars stand up, the bottom axis when they lie flat.

A figure can mislead while every number on it is correct. A bar chart's value axis may start
above zero. Pictures or areas may stand in for lengths. Bins or time steps may be unequally
spaced. The window of time shown may be chosen to suit a claim. Counts may be shown where rates
are needed. And two vertical axes may be used, so that where the lines cross is set by the two
scales chosen.

**In plain terms.** **What one mark is.** A mark is anything drawn that stands for one number: a bar, a point, a
slice of a pie, a picture. Find what one mark stands for. It may be one group, one district or
one year. This is the question you asked about one row of a table.

**What each axis measures, and in what unit.** Read the label along the bottom and the label up
the side. If an axis is a rate or a percentage, ask what it is out of, as you did for a table's
columns.

**Where each axis starts, and how it is spaced.** Read the lowest number printed on each axis.
Then read along it, and check that equal steps on the page are equal steps in the numbers.

**Where the numbers came from.** Look under the figure for a source. With no source, you have
nothing to check it against.

Only then look at the shape.

Next, see how a mark carries its number. There are three common ways, and each can mislead in
its own way.

By position. A point on a line chart carries its value by where it sits. You read it by looking
across to the numbers on the side.

By length. A bar carries its value by how long it is. Your eye compares bars by comparing their
lengths. So a bar's length has to be its value, and that holds only when the bar starts at zero.

By area. A picture, a bubble or a slice of a pie carries its value by how much of the page it
covers. The eye takes in area more readily than height. A pie chart is a circle standing for a
whole. Each slice's share of the circle is its share of the total, so a slice shows a share, not
an amount.

So a bar chart must start at zero, and a line chart need not. The section on graphs told you to
redraw an alarming line from zero before you repeat it. Keep doing that. It is a check on the
impression a slope gives, not a rule of honesty. A line on a printed non-zero axis is honest; a
bar on one is not.

The two rules do not fight. A line
chart asks you to read positions, and a printed axis starting at 400, stated in the caption,
tells you where they are. A
bar chart asks you to compare lengths, and a bar cut off at 400 has the wrong length. A line's
steepness is still somebody's choice, as the section on graphs showed. So read the size of a
change off the numbers, not off the slope.

Double the height and the width of a picture, and it covers four times the page. The number you
multiply every length by is called the scale factor. The area goes up by the scale factor
squared.

Here are six ways a figure misleads while every number on it is right.

**A bar chart whose side axis does not start at zero.** Read the lowest number on the axis. If
it is not zero, subtract it from each value to see how long each bar was drawn.

**Pictures in place of bars.** Compare the numbers, never the pictures. A picture scaled both
ways shows the scale factor squared where the numbers show only the scale factor. Two pie
charts drawn the same size for two years hide any change in the total.

**Unequal spacing.** A bar can stand for a range of values grouped together, called a bin. Read
the range printed under each bar, and check that each covers the same span of values. A label
such as "over 80" does not say where its range ends, so ask.

A bar covering a wider span catches
more values. It may be drawn wider, or drawn the same width and taller. Either way it cannot be
compared with the others as it stands. To compare it fairly, divide its count by how many of the
ordinary spans it covers. That gives the count for each ordinary span, on average.

A time axis can also change its step part way across, say from years to months. Read the date
under each point and work out the gap to the next.

**A chosen window.** A chart over time starts and ends where somebody chose. That stretch is
its window. A short window can show a fall inside a long rise. Ask for the whole series, and ask
why the chart starts where it does.

**Counts where rates are needed.** A district with more people has more of almost everything.
Divide each count by the population before you rank, as in the section on rates.

**Two side axes on one figure.** One line is read against the left axis and one against the
right. Where the two lines cross depends on how the two axes were chosen. Read each line
against its own axis, and never read the crossing as the moment the two were equal.

**Illustration.** Schedule II of the National Food Security Act, 2013 sets the energy that each meal or ration under the Act is
to give.

```table
the group  the meal  kilocalories
children, 6 months to 3 years  take home ration  500
children, 3 to 6 years  morning snack and hot cooked meal  500
children, 6 months to 6 years, who are malnourished  take home ration  800
lower primary classes  hot cooked meal  450
upper primary classes  hot cooked meal  700
pregnant women and lactating mothers  take home ration  600
```

Picture these six as a bar chart on a slide. The side axis starts at 400 kilocalories.

Each bar is drawn up from 400, so its length is its value minus 400.

```working
    450 minus 400 = 50
    800 minus 400 = 400
```

```working
    400 divided by 50 = 8
```

The long bar is drawn eight times as long as the short one.

```working
    800 divided by 450 = 1.7777...
```

About 1.78, which is under twice. Every number on the slide is correct, including the 400 at the bottom.

Redraw the chart with the side axis starting at zero. Each bar is now as long as its value.
Or keep the axis from 400 and draw each value as a dot with no bar under it. A dot carries its
value by position.

That is why the zero rule belongs to bars.

Take the hot cooked meals: 450 kilocalories for lower primary classes and 700 for upper primary. An
infographic — a chart decorated with pictures — draws each meal as a plate. The bigger plate is made as tall
as its value requires.

```working
    700 divided by 450 = 1.5555...
```

The bigger plate is 1.556 times as tall. A plate stretched upward alone would look wrong, so it is widened by
the same amount.

Whatever its shape, a plate made 1.556 times as tall and 1.556 times as wide covers 1.556 times
1.556 as much of the page.

```working
    1.556 times 1.556 = 2.42
```

The bigger plate covers 2.42 times the page, for 1.556 times the energy.

Before the shape, ask yourself how each mark carries its number. A point carries it by position, so read the scale. A
bar carries it by length, so find the zero. A picture carries it by area, so compare the numbers and ignore
the pictures.

**Where this picture breaks.** The eight times belongs to an axis starting at 400. Start at 440 and the same two bars come
apart far further.

```working
    800 minus 440 = 360
    450 minus 440 = 10
    360 divided by 10 = 36
```

The closer the start sits to the shortest bar, the larger the distortion.

Stretch a picture upward only, and its area grows in step with its value. It will look
stretched, which is its own warning.

**Figure.** The six energy values of Schedule II drawn twice. In the upper panel every bar starts at zero, so each length is its value and the 800 bar is under twice the 450 bar. In the lower panel the side axis starts at 400, so the same 800 bar is drawn eight times as long as the 450 bar. Every printed number is correct in both panels.

*What the figure shows:* Two bar charts, one above the other, each with six bars for 500, 500, 800, 450, 700 and 600 kilocalories. In the upper chart the side axis runs from 0 to 900 and the bars differ modestly. In the lower chart the side axis runs from 400 to 900; the 450 bar is a short stub and the 800 bar towers over it.

**Figure.** Two meal energies, 450 and 700 kilocalories, drawn as bars and as pictures. The larger picture is 1.556 times as tall as the smaller and, to keep its shape, 1.556 times as wide, so it covers 2.42 times the page for 1.556 times the energy.

*What the figure shows:* Left panel: two bars from zero, 450 and 700 kilocalories tall, the taller about one and a half times the shorter. Right panel: two plate pictures whose heights match the same two values; the larger plate is also wider and covers well over twice the area of the smaller.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Every chart must start at zero" is the error. A bar chart must, because a bar's length is its value. A line chart need not, because a point carries its value by position, read off the printed scale.
- Settle four things before you look at the shape. What is one mark? What does each axis measure, and in what unit? Where does each axis start, and how is it spaced? Where did the numbers come from?
- A picture enlarged by some factor in height and width covers that factor squared. When you are shown an infographic, compare the numbers and never the pictures.
- When a bar chart in a meeting starts above zero, ask for it redrawn from zero before anybody discusses the size of a gap.
- Ask for each count divided by its population before any district is named as the worst.
- On a figure with two side axes, where the lines cross is set by the two scales somebody chose.
- Teach a trainee to ask one question of every mark: is its number carried by position, by length or by area?
- These checks catch a figure that draws true numbers unfairly. They cannot catch a wrong number, and they cannot tell you whether a real gap matters.

**1.** A bar chart has a side axis that starts at 60. One bar stands for 80 and another for 100.

Work out how long each bar is drawn, in the units of the axis. Then work out how many times as
long the second bar is drawn as the first, and how many times as large its value is.

**2.** A picture is enlarged so that its height and its width are both multiplied by the same number.

**7.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a picture is enlarged 2 times in height and 2 times in width
    its area grows 2 plus 2 = 4 times
    a second picture is enlarged 3 times in height and 3 times in width
    its area grows 3 plus 3 = 6 times
    so the second picture covers 6 times the area of the original
```
