# S37-R1-C10 · How the state moves a price: support price, subsidy, import duty

**Definition.** A price wedge is a gap that a public decision opens between two prices of the same food.
Three wedges are computed here, each by one rule.

The margin of a minimum support price (MSP) over cost is the MSP minus the cost, divided by
the cost, times 100. The cost is the all-India weighted average cost of production for that
crop, as the Union government's release states it. An MSP set at 1.5 times cost carries a
margin of 50 per cent over cost.

The subsidy on grain issued for the public distribution system is the economic cost minus the
central issue price. The economic cost is what the grain costs the government to buy, store
and move. The central issue price is the price at which the Union government issues the grain
to the States. The share of the economic cost that is subsidy is the subsidy divided by the
economic cost, times 100. Where the issue price is zero, the subsidy equals the economic cost.

The landed price of an import under a duty charged as a percentage of its value is the price
before duty times (1 plus the duty rate as a decimal).

**In plain terms.** The government changes food prices in three ways that you can work out with a calculator.

First, it sets a minimum support price (MSP) for a crop. That is the price at which its agencies
stand ready to buy. The Union government says each MSP is at least one and a half times the cost
of growing the crop. That extra half is the margin. It is a percentage of the cost, not of the
price, and that choice of base changes the number a great deal.

Second, it sells grain to ration-card holders for less than the grain cost it. The gap between
the two is the subsidy. The full cost of buying, storing and moving the grain is called the
economic cost. Since 1 January 2023 the grain under the National Food Security Act (NFSA) has
been free, so the subsidy is now the whole economic cost.

Third, it charges a duty on imports, as a percentage of their value. The price at the port after
duty is called the landed price. It is the price before duty, made bigger by the duty's share.

Each rule is one line of arithmetic. The hard part is the base. Always ask: a percentage of what?

**Must know points for you.**

- "MSP gives a 50 per cent margin" is true only over cost. The same gap is a third of the price. When anyone quotes an MSP margin at you, ask for the base, and quote the base yourself.
- The economic cost of rice for 2025-26 is Rs 4173.34 a quintal, the Food Ministry's budget estimate in its December 2025 bulletin. That is about Rs 41.73 a kg. With the issue price at zero since 1 January 2023, all of it is subsidy.
- With free grain, a higher MSP does not change what the ration-card holder pays. It raises the economic cost, and so the subsidy. Say this when someone argues that MSP makes ration grain dearer.
- A duty cut from 10 to 5 per cent is a fall of 5 percentage points in the duty. It is not a 50 per cent fall in anything a shopper pays. Work out the landed price before and after, and quote the change in that.
- Government releases on edible oil use two terms, "basic customs duty" and "effective customs duty", and they are not the same charge. Before you compute a landed price from a duty figure, find which one it is, and say it.
- A paddy MSP and a rice economic cost are prices of different goods. Paddy is milled before it becomes rice. Never subtract one from the other, however neat the answer looks.
- These rules stop at the edge of the government's books: the border, the procurement centre, the ration shop. They cannot tell you the price in an ordinary shop. The margins along the chain, from the section on the price build-up, sit between the wedge and the plate. For that you need a measured retail price.
- An MSP margin is over an all-India average cost. It says nothing about one farmer's margin, or about a farmer who never sold to a procurement agency. Do not carry it to a district or a household without saying so.
