# S37-R1-C07 · What Indians eat and spend on food, measured

**Definition.** India's Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES), run by the Ministry of Statistics
and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), records what a sample of households spent on goods and
services over set reference periods. Its headline figure is the monthly per capita
consumption expenditure (MPCE): a household's spending in a month, divided by the number of
people in it. The share of a food group in MPCE is the group's MPCE divided by total MPCE,
times 100.

In 2022-23, food took 46.38 per cent of MPCE in rural India and 39.17 per cent in urban
India. In 2011-12 the figures were 52.90 and 42.62. Cereals and cereal substitutes took 10.75
per cent of rural MPCE in 2011-12 and 4.91 per cent in 2022-23. The group "beverages,
refreshments and processed food" rose from 7.90 to 9.62 per cent rural and from 8.98 to 10.64
per cent urban over the same years. That group includes purchased cooked meals.

In 2023-24, food took about 47 per cent of rural MPCE and about 40 per cent of urban MPCE.
Beverages, refreshments and processed food was the largest food group: 9.84 per cent rural
and 11.09 per cent urban. Cereals and cereal substitutes took about 4.99 per cent rural.

These are shares of rupees spent, at the prices of each survey year, as the respondents
reported them. The headline shares leave out the value of food received free through
welfare programmes. From 2022-23 the survey records that food separately and reports a
second set of estimates that includes it.

The National Sample Survey's Report 594 converts the food each household recorded as
consumed into energy with a conversion table. It gives an all-India average of 2,233
kilocalories a person a day in rural areas in 2011-12 and 2,212 in 2023-24. For urban areas
the figures are 2,206 and 2,240. The report calls the change from 2009-10 to 2023-24 a slight
increase.

**In plain terms.** Two government surveys tell you most of what is known about Indian food at the scale of the
country. Neither weighs anybody's plate.

The first asks households what they spent. Divide what went on one kind of food by what went
on everything, and you have that food's share of the budget. In rural India, cereals took
about a tenth of the budget in 2011-12. By 2022-23 they took about a twentieth. The group
that holds drinks, snacks, packaged food and meals bought ready to eat went the other way. It
is now the biggest food group in both villages and towns.

The second survey takes the food each household said it used. It turns those amounts into
kilocalories with a table of how much energy each food holds. Book 1 used its figures for the
size of a day's intake. Across the same years its average hardly moved: about 2,200
kilocalories a person a day.

Put the two together and you see the thing this section is about. The budget changed shape a
great deal. The energy total barely changed. A share of spending is a share of rupees. It is
not kilograms and it is not kilocalories. Before you say what Indians eat, say which of these
numbers you are reading.

Rung 2 reads these changes against the nutrition transition. That is Popkin and colleagues'
name for a shift in the world's diets towards processed food, edible oils and sweet drinks.
This section gives you the Indian numbers that argument has to stand on.

**Figure.** Share of rural MPCE, per cent, from the HCES. Cereals and cereal substitutes fell from 10.75 in 2011-12 to 4.91 in 2022-23 and 4.99 in 2023-24. Beverages, refreshments and processed food rose from 7.90 to 9.62 and 9.84. Shares of rupees, not amounts eaten.

*What the figure shows:* Grouped bars for three surveys. Cereals: 10.75, 4.91, 4.99 per cent. Beverages, refreshments and processed food: 7.90, 9.62, 9.84 per cent. The two groups swap places after 2011-12.

**Must know points for you.**

- A falling share of spending is not a falling amount eaten. Cereals' share of rural MPCE halved between 2011-12 and 2022-23 while the rupees spent on them rose. Estimated energy per person barely moved. Before you repeat any "Indians now eat less of" claim, ask whether it came from rupees, kilograms or kilocalories.
- Say points or per cent, every time. Cereals fell 5.84 percentage points in the rural budget, which is a fall of about 54 per cent of the old share. A reader who hears one and thinks of the other is wrong by a factor of about nine.
- Beverages, refreshments and processed food is the largest food group in Indian household spending. In 2023-24 it took 9.84 per cent of rural MPCE and 11.09 per cent of urban MPCE. It includes purchased cooked meals. Do not quote it as spending on packaged snacks alone.
- The headline shares leave out the value of grain received free through welfare programmes. A household living on free PDS rice shows less cereal spending than a household buying the same rice. When you read cereal spending among poorer households, find the survey's second set of estimates, the one that imputes free items.
- Both surveys count what a household bought or used, not what each person ate. They cannot tell you who in the house ate the food, how it was cooked, or how much was thrown away. When a question is about one person's diet, such as a child's or a woman's, do not answer it from these figures. Ask for a survey that recorded what individuals ate.
- Before you set two rounds side by side, check that they measured the same way. The surveys before 2009-10 used a different reference period for some foods. Report 594 used a conversion table from a committee set up in 2025. Ask which table each calorie figure came from.
- When someone cites the nutrition transition for India, ask for the Indian numbers. The worldwide argument is rung 2's. The evidence that India is on that path is spending and intake data like these, and it has to be read with every limit above.

**Exercise 3** (critique). A draft report says this.

> Food's share of rural spending fell from 52.90 per cent in 2011-12 to 46.38 per cent in
> 2022-23. This shows that rural food security has worsened.

Find the flaw.

**1.** A household spends 450 in a month, and 54 of it goes on one item. What share of the month's
spending is that item, in per cent?

**2.** A share was 12 per cent and is now 9 per cent. Give the change in percentage points, and the
change as a per cent of the old share.

**3.** A total is 2,000 and one item's share of it is 7.5 per cent. How much is the item?

**4.** In the HCES 2022-23 factsheet, rural households spent Rs 136 a person a month on edible oil,
out of an MPCE of Rs 3,773. Work out edible oil's share of MPCE. The factsheet prints 3.59
per cent. Say why your answer may differ in the second decimal place.

**5.** Take urban India in 2022-23. The factsheet gives Rs 687 a person a month on beverages,
refreshments and processed food. It gives Rs 235 on cereals and cereal substitutes. MPCE was
Rs 6,459. Work out each group's share of MPCE. Then say how many times the first is the second.

**6.** Food's share of rural MPCE was 52.90 per cent in 2011-12 and 46.38 per cent in 2022-23 (HCES
2022-23 factsheet, Statement 3). Give the change in percentage points and as a per cent of the
2011-12 share.

**7.** Report 594 of the National Sample Survey gives urban per capita energy intake. It was 2,206
kilocalories a day in 2011-12 and 2,240 in 2023-24. Give the change in kilocalories a day and
as a per cent of the 2011-12 figure, with the units kept on each.

**8.** In rural India in 2011-12, MPCE was Rs 1,430 and cereals and cereal substitutes took 10.75
per cent of it. In 2022-23 the factsheet gives Rs 185 on the same group. Work out the 2011-12
rupees, then the change in rupees as a per cent of 2011-12. Then say what this does not tell
you about cereal eaten.

**9.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
cereals' share of rural MPCE: 10.75 per cent in 2011-12, 4.91 per cent in 2022-23
10.75 minus 4.91 = 5.84
so cereals' share fell by 5.84 per cent
```

