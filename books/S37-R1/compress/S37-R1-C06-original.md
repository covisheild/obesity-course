# S37-R1-C06 · Where a country's food comes from: production, imports, stocks

**Definition.** Take one food and one stated period. A country's supply of that food for use within the
country is its production, plus its imports, minus its exports, minus the rise in its
stocks over the period (or plus the fall). The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) uses this form, which
it calls supply for domestic utilization, in its food balance sheets. It is a stock-and-flow
identity: grain that went into store in the period was produced or imported but was not
available for use.

India's official series of per capita net availability of foodgrains, published in the
Economic Survey's Statistical Appendix Table 1.19, applies the same identity with two
further subtractions. Net availability is gross production, less seed, feed and wastage,
less exports, plus imports, less the change in stocks. Net availability divided by the
population gives per capita net availability in kilograms a year. Divided again by 365 it
gives grams a day. The table states that these figures are not strictly representative of
actual consumption.

The FAO's import dependency ratio (IDR) is imports divided by production plus imports
minus exports, times 100. Its self-sufficiency ratio (SSR) is production divided by the
same total, times 100. The two add to 100 only when exports are zero. A net imports figure,
imports minus exports, is enough for the SSR and not enough for the IDR.

**In plain terms.** Where does the food a country eats in a year come from? Three places. What it grew, what it
bought from abroad, and what it had put away in store.

So start with what was grown. Add what came in from abroad. Take off what went out. Then
look at the stores. If the stores grew over the year, some of the harvest went into them and
was not eaten, so take that off. If the stores shrank, the grain that came out was available,
so add it on.

That total, shared among everyone in the country, gives kilograms a person a year. Divide by
365 and you have grams a person a day. India publishes exactly this for grains and pulses.

Two percentages tell you how much a country leans on the rest of the world. Import
dependence is the imported part of what a country kept for itself. Self-sufficiency is the
home-grown part of the same total. If the country exports nothing, the two add to 100. If it
exports, they do not.

None of this says what anyone ate. It says what was there to be eaten.

**Must know points for you.**

- Production is not supply. What a country had to use in a year is production plus imports, minus exports, minus whatever went into store. Dividing the harvest by the population gave 51 grams of pulses a person a day for 2022-23, against the official 47.1. Write the identity out before you argue about any one term in it.
- A rise in stocks is subtracted and a fall is added. The FAO's handbook says so in as many words, and India's table follows it. Getting the sign wrong moves the answer by twice the stock change.
- India's official per capita net availability of foodgrains for 2022-23 is 568.8 grams a person a day (Economic Survey 2025-26, Table 1.19). It is what was there to be eaten, not what was eaten. The table itself says it is not strictly representative of consumption.
- India imported 56.25 per cent of its edible oil supply in 2023-24, by the government's own figures: 15.66 million tonnes imported against 12.18 produced. For cereals it was a net exporter. When someone says India is self-sufficient in food, ask which food.
- Import dependence and self-sufficiency are not two halves of 100 unless the country exports nothing. The FAO's handbook warns that one cannot be taken as the complement of the other. Work each out from its own formula.
- Run the identity on every row of a supply table before you use the row. Table 1.19's rice row for 2022-23 misses its own total by 4,567 thousand tonnes, about 9 grams a person a day in the foodgrain headline. If a row does not close, say so and do not build a trend on it.
- Availability stops at the household door. The FAO's waste element excludes waste in the household. India's table does not say that its wastage includes waste at home, and it says its figures are not strictly representative of consumption. Do not use availability as intake, and do not use a national average for a state or a family.
- Import figures and import duties change on different dates. The edible oil releases of 2024 and 2025 give duty rates that no longer apply. On 24 September 2026 the basic customs duty on crude soybean and palm oil was cut to 5 per cent. Put the date beside every trade figure you quote.
