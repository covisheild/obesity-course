# B5 · Concentration units: mg/dL, mmol/L, and converting between them

**Definition.** A concentration is an amount of something held in a volume of something else.

A mass concentration is a mass in a volume. Milligrams per decilitre, written mg/dL, is a
mass concentration.

An amount-of-substance concentration is an amount of substance in a volume. Millimoles per
litre, written mmol/L, is an amount-of-substance concentration. A
millimole is one thousandth of a mole.

The molar mass of a substance is the mass of one mole of it. Molar mass is a
property of the substance and differs from one substance to another.

Converting a mass concentration into an amount-of-substance concentration therefore
requires the identity of the substance.

A molar mass is measured and computed, not decided, so it can be revised.

**In plain terms.** Milligrams per decilitre, written mg/dL, is a weight of something in a volume of blood. What mg/dL counts is weight.

Millimoles per litre, written mmol/L, counts something else. A mole is a fixed count of
things, molecules here. What mmol/L counts is how many of those things there are.

Those are two different kinds of quantity. One says how heavy. The other says how many.

To get from how heavy to how many, you have to
know how heavy one mole is. That weight is called the molar mass, and it is written in grams
per mole, g/mol.

The molar mass belongs to the substance. Glucose has one. Cholesterol has a
different one. So there is no single conversion factor between mg/dL and mmol/L, and there
never can be.

So the job has four steps.

1. Find out what the substance is. Read the row label, not the number.
2. Get its molar mass in grams per mole. Look it up. Do not guess it.
3. Multiply the mg/dL figure by ten, because a litre holds ten decilitres. That gives
   milligrams per litre.
4. Divide by the molar mass. That gives millimoles per litre.

To go the other way, run the same steps backwards. Multiply by the molar mass, then divide by
ten.

A conversion renames an
amount. It does not make the amount better known.

**Illustration.** Start with the sum you cannot do, because that is the one this section is really about.

Do the glucose one first.

The record is compound 5793. The page gives
the molecular formula as C6H12O6 and the molecular weight as 180.16 g/mol.

Take a glucose value of 100 mg/dL and work it a line at a time.

```working
    100 mg in every decilitre
    a litre holds ten decilitres
    100 times 10 = 1,000
    so 1,000 mg in every litre
    one mole weighs 180.16 grams, so one millimole weighs 180.16 milligrams
    1,000 divided by 180.16 = 5.5506
    so 5.5506 mmol in every litre
```

Write 5.55
mmol/L. The calculator's 5.550621670 is a claim about the laboratory's work, not about
yours.

Now the cholesterol, and watch it stop.

Go back to PubChem and search for cholesterol. The record is compound 5997. The page gives
the molecular formula as C27H46O.

And you still cannot do the sum.

You cannot turn a mass into an amount of substance without the molar mass of the
substance in front of you. Glucose's 180.16 is glucose's, and it says nothing about
cholesterol.

You know the method. You know the substance. None of that is enough. The one number the method needs is a measured property of
cholesterol, and nothing you know produces it.

Do not reuse glucose's divisor because both numbers came
off the same report.

Write down that the conversion is pending. Name what you need,
which is cholesterol's molar mass in grams per mole. Go and read it off PubChem compound
5997. Looking it up is not a detour around the method. It is part of the method.

**Where this picture breaks.** The 180.16 g/mol belongs to D-glucose and to nothing else. The working carries over to another substance only once that substance's
own molar mass is in your hand.

And the conversion renames an amount. It cannot improve it.

**Must know points for you.**

- There is no single number that turns mg/dL into mmol/L. The factor belongs to the substance, not to the pair of units. Dividing by 18 is a glucose move and it is wrong on everything else.
- One mole of D-glucose weighs 180.16 grams, as PubChem's record for compound 5793 gives it. That one figure moves a glucose value between mg/dL and mmol/L in either direction.
- Convert in four steps and say them out loud. Name the substance. Get its molar mass in grams per mole. Multiply the mg/dL figure by ten, because a litre holds ten decilitres. Divide by the molar mass, and the answer is in mmol/L.
- Two laboratory numbers in the same unit are not two numbers about the same thing. Read the row label before you convert, and carry the substance with every converted value you hand on.
- This conversion needs one substance with one molar mass. A figure that is a mixture reported as a single total has no molar mass, so it cannot be put into mmol/L at all.
- A conversion never adds a digit. A value the laboratory gave you as 100 mg/dL comes out as 5.55 mmol/L, not as 5.550621670 mmol/L.
- When you do not hold the molar mass, say so and stop. Quoting a converted value you cannot source costs you everything you say after it.

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A journalist has three minutes. They have two papers open. One reports a blood sugar figure

**9.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    total cholesterol is 200 mg/dL
    to turn a mg/dL figure into mmol/L you divide by 18
    200 divided by 18 = 11.1
    so the cholesterol is 11.1 mmol/L
```

**10.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working

```
