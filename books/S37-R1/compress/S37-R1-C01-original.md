# S37-R1-C01 · What a food system is

**Definition.** The High Level Panel of Experts on Food Security and Nutrition (HLPE) of the Committee on World
Food Security adopted a definition of a food system in 2014. Its 2017 report Nutrition and food
systems uses it again. A food system "gathers all the elements (environment, people, inputs,
processes, infrastructures, institutions, etc.) and activities that relate to the production,
processing, distribution, preparation and consumption of food, and the output of these
activities, including socio-economic and environmental outcomes".

Inside the system, HLPE names the food supply chain: the activities and actors that take food
from production to consumption and to the disposal of its waste. It lists the chain's steps
as production; storage and distribution; processing and packaging; and retail and markets.
The choices of households and individuals about what food to acquire, store, prepare and eat
it treats as consumer behaviour, another part of the same system.

This book reads the system as eight stages: production, storage, processing, distribution,
retail, preparation, consumption and waste. The list is this book's reading of HLPE's
sentences, not a list HLPE prints. Each stage is an activity carried out by actors. Each takes
inputs and gives outputs, and the outputs of one stage are inputs to another.

Which stages, places and flows count as inside is fixed by a boundary, chosen by whoever is
doing the accounting, as for any system. HLPE itself says food systems interact with other
systems, such as health, energy and transport.

**In plain terms.** A food system is everything that happens to food, and everyone who does it, from the field to
the bin.

Start with the plate in front of you and walk backwards. Someone cooked it. Before that, someone
bought the food from a shop or a cart. Before that, it was moved by truck. Perhaps it was stored
in a warehouse, or milled and packed in a factory. Before all of that, a farmer grew it. And
after the meal, what is left over goes somewhere too.

This book gives those steps eight names: production, storage, processing, distribution, retail,
preparation, consumption and waste. Each step is done by somebody, such as a farmer, a trader,
a mill owner, a shopkeeper or the person who cooks. Call each of them an actor — anyone whose
decision moves the food on. Each step takes things in, such as seed, fuel or grain. Each sends
things out, such as grain, flour or a cooked meal.

"Food system" is not everyday English. It is a phrase an expert panel defined. That panel is the
HLPE, which reports to an international committee on food security, and it adopted its
definition in 2014. The eight stages are this book's way of reading that definition.

As with any system (Book 0, E3), you choose where to draw the line round it. A family's kitchen, a district and the whole of India are all food systems.
Each one answers a different question.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Food system" is often heard as "farming". It is not. Farming is one stage of eight. By HLPE's definition the system also holds storing, processing, moving, selling, cooking, eating and throwing away, and the people and institutions at each. When someone says a problem "comes from the food system", ask which stage they mean.
- Do not use "supply chain" and "food system" as if they were one thing. HLPE's supply chain steps stop at retail and markets. Cooking and eating sit in the food system as consumer behaviour. A claim about the supply chain says nothing about the kitchen.
- For any food, write the eight stages down the page. At each, name the actor, what goes in and what comes out. A stage you cannot fill is a question to check, not a blank to skip. Teach a trainee to do this before they argue about any single stage.
- Before you accept a figure about "the food system", ask what line was drawn round it. A kitchen, a state or the country? Household waste in or out? Food bought from abroad in or out? Two honest figures can differ only because their boundaries differ.
- "Food system" is a definition an expert panel adopted in 2014, not a fact of nature. HLPE says its own picture of the system was adapted from earlier ones. Quote it as HLPE's definition, with the year, and expect other bodies to draw the system differently.
- The stage list tells you where things happen and who does them. It does not tell you how much food passes each stage, how much is lost there, or which actor has the most power. It weighs nothing. For any of those you need a number from a source, stage by stage.

**Exercise 1** (interpretation). Take a packet of milk that you or your family buy. Write the eight stages for it. For each
stage, name one actor, one thing that goes in and one thing that comes out. Then mark each
line "checked" or "guessed".

**Exercise 2** (critique). A speaker at a meeting says: "Obesity is a food system problem, so the answer is to help
farmers grow healthier food." What has the speaker done with the phrase "food system"?
