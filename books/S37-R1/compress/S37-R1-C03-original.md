# S37-R1-C03 · A packaged food is many chains meeting

**Definition.** A packaged food made from several ingredients is where several supply chains meet. Each main
ingredient, such as a cereal flour, a sugar, an edible oil, salt or milk solids, arrives
through its own chain from its own farm. The processor adds processing, packaging, a brand
and distribution.

The Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020, made by the Food
Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), govern what the label of a pre-packaged food
must declare. Regulation 5(2) requires a list of ingredients on every package except
single-ingredient foods. Regulation 5(2)(b) requires the ingredients to be "listed in
descending order of their composition by weight or volume, as the case may be at the time of
its manufacture". The list therefore ranks the ingredients. It states an ingredient's
percentage only where regulation 5(2)(g) requires one, for example where the label emphasises
that ingredient in words or pictures.

Some ingredients may be declared under a class title. Sucrose may be declared as "Sugar", and
milk products as "Milk solids", with the source optional. An edible vegetable oil must be
declared by the name of the specific oil. A compound ingredient that makes up less than 5 per
cent of the food need not have its own ingredients declared, apart from food additives that
serve a technological function.

The label must name the brand owner, who may be the manufacturer or only the marketer
(regulation 5(6)). If the manufacturer is someone else, only the manufacturer's licence
number must appear (regulation 5(7)(b)). A country of origin is required for food imported
into India (regulation 5(12)). The ingredients clause does not ask where an ingredient was
grown, who supplied it or what it cost.

These are the regulations as consolidated by FSSAI in Version-VIII of its compendium, dated
9 September 2025.

**In plain terms.** The last section followed one fresh food down one chain. A packaged food has many chains
behind it. Turn over a packet of biscuits. The list of ingredients on the back is a map of farms.

Wheat flour came from wheat fields. Sugar came from a sugar crop and a sugar mill. The oil came
from an oil crop, perhaps grown in another country. Milk solids came from dairy animals. Each
of these travelled its own chain of farmer, trader, mill and truck before it reached the
factory. The factory mixed them, baked them, packed them and put a brand name on the front.
Then the packets travelled another chain to your shop.

The list is not written as the company likes. A regulation says how it must be written. The
ingredients go in order of weight when the food was made, heaviest first. So the first
ingredient weighs more than any other single one. The list does not usually say how much of
each there is.

The label also leaves a great deal out. It names the company that owns the brand, which may
not be the one that made the food. It does not say which farm, which state or which country
the wheat or the oil came from, or what anything cost. To find those, you need other sources.

**Must know points for you.**

- "The first ingredient is most of the product" is wrong. The list ranks ingredients by weight or volume at manufacture. The first is only heavier than each other single ingredient. A percentage appears only where the rules require one, as when the label emphasises that ingredient.
- Add up an ingredient that appears under more than one name before you say where it ranks. Sugar and invert sugar syrup are listed as two lines, each in its own place. Together they may outrank the first ingredient. When you read a label with a patient, say that the list cannot settle it.
- The address on a packet is the brand owner's, who may only market it. If the maker is someone else, the label shows the maker's licence number, not its name. Never tell anyone that a packet's address is where the food was made, still less where it was grown.
- A compound ingredient under 5 per cent of the food need not list its own ingredients, apart from additives. Its chains are out of sight on the label. When you trace a product's chains, mark such an ingredient as a known gap, not as one chain.
- The label asks for a country of origin only on food imported into India. An Indian-made packet need not say where its oil or its wheat was grown. Do not read silence on the label as evidence that an ingredient is Indian.
- For any packaged food, read the first four or five ingredients as a list of farm chains. For each, name the crop, the kind of mill or plant between farm and factory, and what you would still have to find out. This is the first step of describing where a familiar food came from.
- The list is one pack's ranking at manufacture, under one set of rules. It cannot tell you who supplied an ingredient, what it cost, or who kept how much of the price. Those need other sources. Working out margins along a product's chains is left to rung 2.

**Exercise 1** (interpretation). A made-up packet of namkeen lists: "Ingredients: Gram pulse flour (Besan), Edible vegetable
oil (Palmolein), Peanuts, Iodised salt, Spices and condiments." Name the chain behind each
ingredient. Which ingredient hides more than one chain behind a single name, and which rule
lets it?

**Exercise 2** (critique). The front of a made-up packet says "Made with real milk" beside a picture of a glass of milk.
The list shows "Milk solids" as the eighth ingredient, with no percentage. A friend says:
"Eighth means there's hardly any milk in it." What can you say, and what should you look for?
