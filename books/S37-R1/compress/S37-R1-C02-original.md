# S37-R1-C02 · One fresh food from field to plate in India

**Definition.** Fresh produce in India commonly passes through several hands between the farm and the
household. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) working paper on tomato, onion and potato (Working
Paper 08 of 2024) describes the onion route from the Nashik region to Delhi. Farmers mostly
sell in a mandi run by an Agricultural Produce Marketing Committee (APMC), where the price is
set by open auction. A trader buys with the help of commission agents. Mandi fees and
commission charges are paid there.

The trader then deals with traders or wholesalers at a
secondary mandi, such as Azadpur in Delhi. Retailers buy there and sell in neighbourhood
markets. For potato in western Uttar Pradesh, the paper adds cold storage as a stage. It calls
cold storage the most important selling point. At a secondary mandi, "Mandi fees and
commission charges are paid again for the same produce".

Agricultural markets are run by the states, through their APMCs. The National Agriculture
Market (NAM), run online as e-NAM, links physical mandis to an online trading portal; it
describes itself as "not a parallel marketing structure".

Food leaves the chain in two ways that are measured separately. The United Nations Environment
Programme (UNEP) defines food loss as food that exits the supply chain "up to, and excluding,
the retail level". It defines food waste as food and its inedible parts removed at retail,
food service and household level. For India, a study by the consultancy NABCONS for the
Ministry of Food Processing Industries (reference years 2020-22) reports per cent loss after
harvest in farm operations and at market level separately. Tomato is 8.37 and 3.25, wheat 3.61
and 0.56. UNEP estimates India's household food waste at 55 kg per person per year, with
"Medium confidence", from seven city studies.

These are one study's estimates and one agency's modelled figure. A national survey with a
different method could move any of them.

**In plain terms.** The last section named eight stages of a food system. Here is one real food passing through
them. An onion bought in Delhi has usually passed through several pairs of hands.

The farmer grows it and takes it to a mandi — a regulated wholesale market run under state
law. There it is auctioned. A trader buys it, helped by a commission agent — a middleman paid a
percentage for arranging the sale. The trader pays a fee to the mandi and a commission to the
agent. Then the onions travel by truck to a big city mandi, and change hands again. A shopkeeper
or vendor buys them there and sells them in your neighbourhood. Potatoes often sit for months in
a cold store on the way.

Some food never reaches you. Two different words describe what is lost, and the difference
matters. Food loss is what goes before the shop: spoiled in the field, dropped in sorting, rotted
in a store. Food waste is what goes at the shop, in a restaurant or at home, and it counts peels
and bones too.

India has numbers for both, and neither is exact. A government study puts tomato's loss at about
8 per cent on the farm and about 3 per cent at market. A United Nations agency estimates that
each Indian household member throws away about 55 kg of food a year. That figure is built from
just seven city studies, and the agency itself rates it only "medium confidence".

**Must know points for you.**

- Loss and waste are not two words for one thing. In UNEP's definitions, food loss happens up to the shop and food waste at the shop, in food service and at home. They are measured by different agencies with different methods. When someone gives you one number for "food lost and wasted in India", ask which of the two it is.
- Tomato loses 8.37 per cent in farm operations and 3.25 per cent at market level. That is the NABCONS study for 2020-22, as the ministry reported it in 2025. The release gives no total. Quote both figures, with the study and its years, and never add them as if that were the study's own total.
- India's household food waste of 55 kg per person a year is UNEP's "medium confidence" estimate. It is built from seven city studies scaled to the national population. When a journalist quotes it as a national measurement, correct them. Say it is modelled, and that it counts peels and bones.
- Read a table's unit before any number in it. The eggs row of the ministry's loss table sits under "million tonnes" and cannot be tonnes. If a figure is absurd under its column heading, drop it and say why. Do not guess the unit the table left out.
- Much fresh produce in India passes through state-regulated mandis. A trader may pay mandi fee and commission at a primary mandi and again at a city mandi. When you describe a chain, count the hands and the charges at each. They are where the price the farmer got turns into the price you paid.
- To say where a fresh food came from, list the actors in order and the place each hands it on. Mark each one as checked in a source or guessed. A sketch with the guesses marked is honest; a smooth story of farmer, mandi, shop is not.
- A traced chain holds for the crop, route, city and years it was traced. The onion route here is Nashik to Delhi, from field visits in January 2023. Before you apply it to another vegetable, another state or another season, find a source for that one.
