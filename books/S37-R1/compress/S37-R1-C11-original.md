# S37-R1-C11 · The National Food Security Act and the public distribution system

**Definition.** The National Food Security Act, 2013 (Act 20 of 2013) makes a quantity of grain a legal
entitlement. Under section 3(1), every person in a priority household is entitled to five
kilograms of foodgrains per person per month, at the prices in Schedule I. The State
Government supplies it under the Targeted Public Distribution System. Households under the Antyodaya Anna
Yojana are entitled instead to thirty-five kilograms per household per month.

Section 2(5) defines foodgrains as rice, wheat or coarse grains, or any mix of them.

Section 3(2) extends the entitlement to up to seventy-five per cent of the rural population
and up to fifty per cent of the urban population. Under section 9 the Central Government fixes
each State's coverage. Under section 10 the State Government identifies the Antyodaya
households and the priority households within it.

Schedule I set the price at not more than Rs 3 a kg for rice, Rs 2 for wheat and Re 1 for
coarse grains for three years from the Act's commencement. After that the price is whatever the
Central Government fixes, not exceeding the minimum support price.

The Targeted Public Distribution System is the system for distributing essential commodities
to ration-card holders through fair price shops (section 2(23)). A fair price shop is licensed
by an order under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (section 2(4)).

Since 1 January 2023 the grain has been issued free, by decision of the Union government under
the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana. That decision was extended on 29 November 2023 for
five years from 1 January 2024.

**In plain terms.** Since 2013, a set quantity of grain each month has been a legal right for most Indians. The
law is the National Food Security Act (NFSA).

There are two kinds of eligible household. The poorest are Antyodaya households. They get 35 kg
of grain a month for the whole household. The rest are priority households. They get 5 kg a
month for each person in the household. The grain is rice, wheat or coarse grains such as
millets, and nothing else.

The Union government decides how many people each State may cover. Up to three rural people in
four, and up to one urban person in two. The State then decides which households those are, and
gives them ration cards.

The grain comes from a fair price shop, a licensed ration shop. The Union government buys,
stores and moves the grain to its depots. The State takes it from there to the shop.

The Act set a price of at most Rs 3 a kg for rice. Since January 2023 the grain has been free.
That came from a Union government decision, not from a change in the Act.

**Figure.** Grain a month under section 3(1) of the National Food Security Act, 2013. A priority household gets 5 kg for each person; an Antyodaya household gets 35 kg whatever its size. The two are equal at 7 people, and above that the priority household gets more.

*What the figure shows:* A rising straight line for priority households, from 5 kg at 1 person to 50 kg at 10, and a flat line at 35 kg for Antyodaya households. They cross at 7 people.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Five kilograms a person" is the priority-household rule only. An Antyodaya household gets 35 kg in all, so above seven people it gets less per head. Say which household you mean whenever you quote the entitlement.
- About 81.35 crore people, that is 813.5 million, are to get free grain for five years from 1 January 2024. That was the Cabinet decision of 29 November 2023.
- The quantity is a legal right. The zero price is not. The Act caps the price; the Cabinet made it zero, to the end of 2028. Asked whether free rations are a right, say both halves.
- The Act's grain is cereal only: rice, wheat or coarse grains. Pulses, oil and vegetables are not in section 3 at all. Anything more that a ration shop hands out comes from some other scheme. Find that scheme before you describe it as part of the Act.
- The Food Ministry itself calls the public distribution system "supplemental". It does not aim to meet a household's whole need. Never work out a household's diet from its ration alone.
- Seventy-five per cent rural and fifty per cent urban are national ceilings, "up to". They are not the share covered in any one State. The Centre fixes each State's share under section 9, and the State picks the households. A complaint about who was left out goes to the State.
- If the grain does not come, section 8 entitles the household to a food security allowance from the State. When a patient says the ration shop gave nothing, that is the provision to point to.
