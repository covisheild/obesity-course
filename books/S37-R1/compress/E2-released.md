# E2 · Chemical energy, combustion, and the bomb calorimeter

**Definition.** Combustion is burning: a substance combines vigorously with oxygen.

A bomb calorimeter operates at constant volume and is used for reactions that release large
amounts of energy and produce gases, which is what combustion does. It is a strong steel
container, the bomb, which holds the sample and is itself submerged in water. The bomb is
filled with oxygen at high pressure and a small electrical spark ignites the sample. The
energy produced is taken up by the steel bomb and the water around it, and a thermometer
records the rise.

A substance whose energy is already known is burned in it, and the rise that produces is
measured. The known energy divided by that rise is the calorimeter's energy equivalent, in
joules for each degree. Afterwards any rise measured in that calorimeter, multiplied by that
same energy equivalent, gives the energy the reaction released.

What that measures is the total combustible energy content of the food, also called its
theoretical maximum energy content. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United
Nations calls this the ingested energy, or the gross energy.

Not all of it is available to a person. Incomplete digestion in the small intestine, sometimes
with fermentation of unabsorbed carbohydrate in the colon, loses energy as faecal energy and
as gaseous energy in combustible gases. Some of the energy that is absorbed is then lost as
urinary energy, mainly as nitrogenous waste compounds from incomplete breakdown of protein. A
small amount is lost from the body surface. What remains after those losses is metabolizable
energy.

Below that the Organization's 2003 report carries one further step, net metabolizable energy.
It takes off the energy the body itself spends on digesting the food and on handling what is
absorbed. The report's cascade has no step called digestible energy. The phrase is not in
chapter 3, where the report sets out the cascade, or in chapter 4.

The energy figure on a food label is not a burning measurement. It is derived using the
Atwater system, which uses the average caloric content of protein, carbohydrate and fat. Those
averages are the Atwater general factors. There is one value for each of the three, used
regardless of the food it is found in. They are based on the heats of combustion of protein,
fat and carbohydrate, corrected for losses in digestion, absorption and urinary excretion of
urea.

The three values are 17 kilojoules for each gram of protein, 37 for each gram of fat and 17
for each gram of carbohydrate. In kilocalories for each gram the same three values are 4.0,
9.0 and 4.0.

One thing the general system does not do is take fibre out. Some labelling systems do
discount fibre, as OpenStax Chemistry describes. Atwater's general system does not. The Indian
labelling regulations give dietary fibre its own factor, 2 kilocalories a gram.

To work out the energy content of a food, the quantities of carbohydrate, protein and fat are
each multiplied by the factor for that one, and the products are added.

**In plain terms.** You cannot weigh energy. What you can do is burn the food and watch a thermometer.

The machine for it is called a bomb calorimeter. The name is unhelpful and the thing is
straightforward. It is a thick steel pot, sealed, sitting in a tank of water. You put a weighed
scrap of dried food inside, fill the pot with oxygen, seal it, and set it off with an electric
spark. The food burns completely. The warmth spreads into the steel and the water. A thermometer
in the water reads higher than it did before.

Nothing about a temperature rise tells you how many joules it took. That depends on the machine:
how much steel, how much water, how it is built.

**You burn something whose energy you already know.** You watch how far the thermometer goes up.
Then you divide.

```working
    the energy equivalent = the known energy, divided by the rise it caused
```

Call it the calorimeter's energy equivalent. Doing this is called calibrating the machine, and
it is done again before each set of measurements rather than once when the machine is bought.

After that the work is one multiplication.

```working
    the energy released = the rise, times the energy equivalent
```

Burn a known thing to get the constant, then burn the unknown thing and multiply.

What you have measured is all the energy the food will give up when it is burnt to nothing. The
Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations calls it the gross energy. It is the
most the food could ever supply.

A person is not a furnace.

Some food is not digested and absorbed at all, and leaves in the faeces, carrying its energy
with it. Some unabsorbed carbohydrate is fermented in the large intestine and leaves as gas,
which also carries energy. Some of what is absorbed leaves in the urine, mostly as the waste
left when protein is broken down and not finished off. And a small amount is lost from the body
surface.

What is left after those losses has a name. It is called metabolizable energy, spelt with a z in
the report that names it.

So the cascade is gross energy, then metabolizable energy. Below that the report carries one
more step, called net metabolizable energy. It takes off the energy the body itself spends on
digesting the food and handling it. The report's cascade has no step called digestible energy.
The phrase is not in chapter 3, where the report sets out the cascade, or in chapter 4. You may
hear somebody put one in the middle and say the report has it. If they do, they have not read
that chapter.

The number on a packet was almost certainly never measured by burning anything. It was
calculated.

The fixed figures are called the Atwater general factors.

```table
what it is     kilojoules a gram  kilocalories a gram
protein        17                 4.0
fat            37                 9.0
carbohydrate   17                 4.0
```

Those factors already have the losses taken out of them. They were built from burning
measurements and then corrected for what is lost in digestion, in absorption and in the urine.

So the figure on the packet is an estimate of metabolizable energy for an average, arrived at by
arithmetic on three weights. It is a good estimate and it is not a measurement of that packet.

Burning tells you what is in the food. The factors tell you what an average person would get out
of it. Neither one tells you anything about the person in front of you.

Every figure in this calorimeter example is made up.

The known sample releases 26,400 joules. The thermometer goes up by 2.2 degrees Celsius, which is the ordinary degree marked on a kitchen thermometer.

Divide the energy by the rise.

```working
    26,400 divided by 2.2 = 12,000
```

That is the calorimeter's energy equivalent, and it is the only thing you needed.

A dried pellet of food weighing 1.50 grams goes in, and the thermometer goes up by 1.85 degrees.

```working
    12,000 times 1.85 = 22,200
```

Put it in kilojoules, because nobody quotes food in joules.

```working
    22,200 divided by 1,000 = 22.2
```

Now for each gram, since the pellet weighed 1.50 grams.

```working
    22.2 divided by 1.50 = 14.8
```

Turn it into the unit food is quoted in, using the 4.184 from the section on energy units.

```working
    14.8 divided by 4.184 = 3.5373
```

About 3.5 kilocalories in each gram.

The question nobody asks is what it is a number about.

The report's kilojoule column and its kilocalorie column were each rounded on their own, and they are not exact conversions of one another. A footnote on the same
page gives protein's precise value as 16.7 kilojoules a gram, and that one does convert properly.

```working

```

The 17 is the rounded figure, and rounding it is what moved your answer in the third digit.

You do not have a measurement of any meal. Schedule II says what a meal must meet, not what any meal contained.

You do not have what reaches a child either, even if a meal met the standard exactly.

This book does not carry either number, and you should not supply one from memory.

Every time somebody hands you a food-energy figure, ask three questions in order. Was it burnt or was it calculated. Is it the gross figure or the metabolizable
one. And is it a standard somebody set, or a measurement somebody took.

**Where this picture breaks.** Every figure in the calorimeter example is made up. Only the 4.184, the Atwater factors and the two Schedule II figures come from a source.

The energy equivalent belongs to one calorimeter on one day. Carry it to another machine and every answer it gives is wrong, and wrong
silently, because the arithmetic still works.

The method needs the sample to burn completely. Nothing in the arithmetic will tell you it happened.

The Atwater factors are averages over foods. So the method cannot tell apart two foods with the same grams of protein, fat and carbohydrate.

The two printed columns pair up for protein and for carbohydrate. They do not pair up for fat. The footnote that gave protein's 16.7 gives
fat's precise value as 37.4 kilojoules a gram, and the report prints 9.0 kilocalories beside it. Try the conversion both ways round.

```working
    37.4 divided by 4.184 = 8.9388
    9.0 times 4.184 = 37.656
```

Read each column as its own set of numbers and do not work one out from the other.

The eleven per cent figure is a share of two numbers the Act sets, not a share of anything a child ate.

None of the arithmetic here says anything about a person. It gives you what is in a food and what an average person would get out of it. What
one person absorbs, what they spend, and what any change in their body would be made of are three separate questions. This book answers none
of them.

**Must know points for you.**

- A bomb calorimeter figure is the food's total combustible energy. It is not the energy a person gets. So never quote a burning figure as what somebody received.
- The report's cascade has no digestible-energy step. It runs from gross or ingested energy to metabolizable energy. The losses named are faecal, gaseous, urinary and surface. A third step sits below, net metabolizable energy. So do not object to a three-step reading of this report. Object to the word digestible when it is credited to this cascade. Name the step that was made up.
- A temperature rise is not an energy until the machine that produced it has been calibrated. The calibration is repeated before each set of runs, not done once.
- The Atwater general factors are the numbers behind nearly every food-energy figure you will ever be handed. They are 17 kilojoules, or 4.0 kilocalories, for each gram of protein. For fat they are 37 or 9.0, and for carbohydrate 17 or 4.0. Knowing them means you can recompute any label yourself instead of trusting it.
- Those factors use one value for each of protein, fat and carbohydrate, whatever the food. It cannot show that one food is taken up better than another.
- The energy declared on an Indian packet is worked out from the grams, not measured by burning anything. So two packets showing the same figure have been through the same arithmetic, not the same measurement.
- When a journalist gives you a food-energy figure, the useful sentence is about what it describes. Give them that line before you argue about the number itself.
- Teach the calibration before the formula. A trainee who has seen a known burn turned into a constant will never again treat a thermometer reading as an energy. They will also carry the question to every instrument they meet afterwards: what was this measured against.

**9.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke. The figures in this problem are made up.

```working
    the calorimeter is worth 10,000 joules for each degree
    the thermometer read 21.4 degrees before the sample was lit
    the thermometer read 24.9 degrees after the sample had burned
    10,000 times 24.9 = 249,000
    so the sample released 249,000 joules, which is 249 kilojoules
```

**10.** A packet's nutritional information panel reads: total carbohydrate 30 grams, of which sugars
12 grams; protein 6 grams; total fat 5 grams. The packet is made up for this problem.

Here is a worked answer for the energy in the packet. Find the step that broke.

```working
    carbohydrate: 30 times 4 = 120
    sugars: 12 times 4 = 48
    protein: 6 times 4 = 24
    fat: 5 times 9 = 45
    120 plus 48 plus 24 plus 45 = 237
    so the packet holds 237 kilocalories
```
