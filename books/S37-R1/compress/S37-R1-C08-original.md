# S37-R1-C08 · Built against hunger

**Definition.** India's central food institutions were set up in answer to shortages of grain, and the
Department of Food and Public Distribution's own account says so. A separate Food Department
was established on 1 December 1942, during the Second World War, because India faced acute
food shortage. The Food Corporation of India (FCI) was set up in 1965, under an Act of 1964,
because the country faced a major shortage of foodgrains, especially wheat. The public
distribution system (PDS) was started in the 1960s to manage the scarcity of foodgrain
supplies in the open market and to distribute foodgrains at affordable prices.

The Department states the FCI's job today in quantities of grain. Its Annual Report 2025-26
calls the FCI the main instrument of the Government of India for procurement and distribution
of wheat, rice and coarse grains, and for maintaining the buffer stock of foodgrains.

The measure these institutions are judged by is also a quantity. The Economic Survey
2025-26's table of foodgrain availability counts production, net imports and stocks in
thousand tonnes, and availability per person in grams a day. For 2022-23 it gives net imports
of cereals of minus 30,352 thousand tonnes: India exported more cereal than it imported. It
gives net imports of pulses of 1,733 thousand tonnes.

The nutrition standards that the National Food Security Act, 2013 attaches to its meals are
floors. Schedule II gives calories and protein that the meals are "required to be met". No
instrument quoted here sets an upper limit on energy, sugar, fat or salt.

**In plain terms.** The section on where a country's food comes from counted what is grown, what is bought
abroad and what is held in store. This one asks why the state built what it built.

The government's own history gives a plain answer. Each time, the trigger was too little
grain. A Food Department was set up in 1942, in the war, because of acute food shortage. The
Food Corporation of India came in 1965, because of a major shortage of grain, wheat above all.
The ration shops of the public distribution system started in the 1960s to manage scarcity.

So the tools were built to buy grain, store it, move it and sell it cheaply. Read how the
Department describes the Food Corporation today. It is the main instrument for buying and
distributing wheat, rice and coarse grains, the cereals other than those two. It also holds
the buffer stock. And the score the country keeps is in tonnes and grams of grain. By that score the
system worked: in 2022-23 India sent out more cereal than it brought in.

Nothing in that design asks whether people eat too much of anything. The food law's meal
standards say how many calories a child's meal must reach. None says how many it must not
pass. That is not an oversight in a system built for obesity. It is a system built for a
different problem, and it did what it was built to do.

**Illustration.** Open two instruments and one table, and ask each the same question: what does it count?

First, the Department of Food and Public Distribution's Annual Report 2025-26
(https://dfpd.gov.in/WriteReadData/AnnualRecordUploadDocuments/43bf089b-73cc-4329-aeb6-c883d2f2ea55_Food%20AR%202025-26%20English.pdf).
Search it for "main instrument". Paragraph 3.64 comes up. It calls the Food Corporation the
Government's main instrument "for procurement and distribution of wheat, rice and
coarse-grains". It adds the buffer stock of foodgrains.

Procurement means the state buying grain from farmers. A buffer stock is grain held in
reserve. So the work is buying, moving and storing, and every object of it is a grain. Now
read the whole paragraph again and look for "nutrition". It is not there.

Second, the National Food Security Act, 2013
(https://indiacode.gov.in/handle/123456789/496105). Go to Schedule II, "Nutritional
Standards". Every row gives a number of calories and grams of protein. Read how the Schedule
frames them: standards "required to be met". A standard you must meet is a floor. Look along
each row for a maximum, or for sugar, fat or salt. There is none.

Third, the Economic Survey 2025-26, Statistical Appendix, Table 1.19
(https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/stat/tab1.19.pdf). This is the scoreboard.
Find the 2022-23 rows. Net imports, in thousand tonnes, read like this.

```table
group  net imports 2022-23 (thousand tonnes)
rice  -22347
wheat  -4682
other cereals  -3323
pulses  1733
```

A minus sign here means India sent out more than it brought in. The three cereal rows add up
to the cereals total.

```working
22,347 plus 4,682 plus 3,323 = 30,352
```

So cereals went out by 30,352 thousand tonnes, about 30 million tonnes, more than came in.
Pulses came in, 1,733 thousand tonnes more than went out. The system built to end a grain
shortage now runs a grain surplus. The foods it was not built around are the ones still
bought abroad.

Put the three together. The report counts grain bought and held. The Schedule counts calories reached.
The table counts tonnes available. None of them has a place to record too much.

**Where this picture breaks.** The net import figures hold for the financial year 2022-23 and for all India. They describe
grain available, not grain eaten. The table's own note says per person availability is "not
strictly representative of actual level of consumption". They say nothing about any state or
household, and a single year of trade can swing with one harvest.

**Figure.** Net imports in 2022-23, thousand tonnes (Economic Survey 2025-26, Table 1.19). Rice -22347, wheat -4682 and other cereals -3323 are net exports, together -30352. Pulses, 1733, are still a net import.

*What the figure shows:* Bars drawn from a baseline. The rice, wheat and other cereals bars point down, to -22347, -4682 and -3323 thousand tonnes. The pulses bar points up, to 1733.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Indian food policy ignored nutrition" is the wrong charge. It was built against a grain shortage and judged by tonnes of grain, and by that measure it succeeded. Say that first in a committee. Then argue that the measure has to change, which people who built the system can hear.
- Before you ask an institution to do nutrition work, read what the state says it is for. The Food Corporation of India buys and moves wheat, rice and coarse grains. It also holds the buffer stock. That is how the Department's own report puts it. A plan for it to buy vegetables or oils changes its job. Argue it as a change, not as a small fix.
- In 2022-23 India's net imports of cereals were minus 30,352 thousand tonnes, a net export of about 30 million tonnes (Economic Survey 2025-26, Table 1.19). Pulses were a net import of 1,733 thousand tonnes.
- The nutrition standards in Schedule II of the National Food Security Act are floors, "required to be met". When someone tells you a scheme "meets the nutrition standards", ask what they would say if a meal carried twice the energy. The instrument has no answer to that.
- The PDS was never meant to be a whole diet. The Department calls it "supplemental in nature". Do not judge the ration as if it had to supply everything a family eats. Judge what it adds, and what it makes cheap.
- The departmental histories give the state's own reasons, in its own words. They do not measure how bad any shortage was, or what else drove the choices. For that, go to a historian's account or to the data of the period. Do not quote an annual report as proof of what happened in 1965.
- Grams of foodgrain available a day per person is not grams eaten. The Economic Survey's own note says its per person figure is "not strictly representative of actual level of consumption". Do not set it beside a requirement as if it were intake.
