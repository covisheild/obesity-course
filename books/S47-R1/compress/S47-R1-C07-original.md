# S47-R1-C07 · How a rule or regulation is made, and where the public gets in

**Definition.** A rule or a regulation is made by the body an Act names, under the power that Act gives, and
by the procedure that the same Act sets. Rules and regulations together are called
subordinate legislation. The section that gives the power can carry three conditions. "By
notification": the instrument is made by publishing it in the Gazette. "With the previous
approval of" another authority: a second body must agree first. "After previous
publication": a draft must be published first.

Section 23 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 applies where a Central Act gives a power to
make "rules or bye-laws" subject to previous publication. The authority must publish a draft
for the information of persons likely to be affected. It must publish with the draft a
notice of the date on or after which the draft will be taken into consideration. It, and any
authority whose sanction, approval or concurrence is needed, must consider any objection or
suggestion received from any person before that date. Publication of the final rule in the
Official Gazette is conclusive proof that it was duly made.

Not every power carries the condition. Under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, the
Central Government makes rules by notification (section 91(1)), with no words requiring a
draft. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) makes regulations with the
previous approval of the Central Government and after previous publication (section 92(1)).
A State Government makes rules after previous publication and with the previous approval of
FSSAI (section 94(1)).

Every rule and regulation made under that Act is laid before each House of Parliament, as
soon as may be after it is made, for a total of {{n:laying_days}} days of sitting (section 93). If both
Houses agree to modify or annul it, it then has effect only as modified, or not at all,
without affecting anything already done under it.

The Pre-legislative Consultation Policy of 10 January 2014, a decision of the Committee of
Secretaries, is headed as covering principal and subordinate legislation. It asks that a draft
of legislation or rules placed in public come with a note in simple language. It asks that a
summary of the feedback go on the department's website. It asks that new Acts provide for
previous publication of rules. It is a policy of the Union government, not a statute.

**In plain terms.** Parliament passes an Act. The Act often leaves the detail to someone else. It may be a rule,
made by the Central Government. Or it may be a regulation, made by a body such as the Food
Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI). The section that hands over the power also says how the detail
must be made.

Here is where you get in. Many Acts say a rule or regulation may be made only "after previous
publication". That means a draft comes first, with a date on it. Anyone may send an objection
or a suggestion before that date, and the body must consider it. Then the final text appears
in the Gazette, the government's official newspaper.

After that it goes before Parliament, which can change it or cancel it. But by then it has
already been made.

So the window to be heard runs from the draft to the date in its notice. A comment that
arrives after that date arrives after the decision.

**Figure.** The FSSAI Packaging Regulations, 2018, in days: 14 from the draft's date (19 March 2018) to Gazette copies reaching the public (2 April 2018); 30 for objections; 280 from the draft's date to the final notification of 24 December 2018.

**Must know points for you.**

- "A regulation takes effect only once Parliament approves it" is wrong for the Food Safety and Standards Act. Section 93 lays each rule and regulation before Parliament after it is made. The Houses may then modify or annul it, and anything already done under it stays valid. Do not tell anyone they can wait for Parliament.
- Before you plan a comment, open the section that gives the power. Find "by notification", "with the previous approval of" and "after previous publication". They tell you whether a draft will appear, and which two bodies must hear you.
- Count the objection period from the day the notice names. For the 2018 packaging draft, that was the day Gazette copies reached the public, 2 April 2018, not the draft's date of 19 March. Counting from the wrong day loses you two weeks.
- Two different thirties sit in the same process. The 2018 packaging draft's period for objections was {{n:packaging_draft_objection_days}} days. Section 93 keeps a made regulation before Parliament for {{n:laying_days}} days of sitting. Say which one you mean.
- Section 23 of the General Clauses Act makes the body consider an objection. It does not make the body accept it, or answer it. Once the final rule is in the Official Gazette, that publication is conclusive proof it was duly made. So the time that counts is before the date in the notice.
- The 2014 consultation policy binds officials as a decision of the Committee of Secretaries, not as a law. A department may skip it and record why, and the Law Ministry kept no compliance record (2022). Do not call a missing consultation unlawful on the policy's strength alone; check whether the Act itself asks for previous publication.
- The final notification tells you the steps were taken. It does not tell you what any objection said or whether it changed a word. To find out what changed, set the draft beside the final text, clause by clause.
