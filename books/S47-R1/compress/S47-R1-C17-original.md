# S47-R1-C17 · Informing policy and campaigning for it

**Definition.** The same evidence can be put to two different jobs. Informing policy sets out the options
open to the body that decides, what the evidence says about the effects of each, and how
uncertain that evidence is, without choosing among the options. The OpenStax textbook
*American Government* calls the people who do this policy analysts. Campaigning for policy,
also called advocacy, starts from a position about what ought to be done and argues for one
option; the same textbook calls these people policy advocates.

Both jobs are legitimate. The defect is a document that does one job while presenting itself
as doing the other, most often a campaign presented as a neutral account of the evidence. It
shows in four marks. Evidence is used selectively. Uncertainty is dropped, or doubt is invented
about evidence the writer dislikes. A value judgement is written as if it were a finding. An
interest in the decision, the writer's or the funder's, is not declared.

Oliver and Cairney (2019) put the choice as one between acting as an "honest broker" of the
evidence and acting as an "issue advocate". They report that the boundary between the two is
hard to locate in practice. Cairney and Oliver (2017) use "honest broker" for a researcher who
works with stakeholders to define policy problems, a different sense. This book's rule is to
decide which job a document does before writing it, and to say so in the document.

**In plain terms.** The section before this one showed what evidence can settle in a policy choice and what it
cannot. It can say how big a problem is and what an option does. It cannot choose between
values (`S47-R1-C16`). This section is about what you do with that limit when you write.

You can do two different jobs with the same evidence.

The first job is informing. You set out the options open to the body that decides. For each
option you say what the evidence shows, and how sure anyone can be. You do not pick one. An
American textbook on government calls people who do this policy analysts.

The second job is campaigning, also called advocacy. You start from what you think ought to
happen, and you argue for one option. The same textbook calls these people policy advocates.

Both jobs are honest work. A health department that writes to its State Government asking for a
higher tax is campaigning, openly, and it is entitled to. The trouble starts when a document does
one job and looks like the other. The usual case is a campaign dressed as a neutral summary of
the evidence. Readers who trust the summary are being argued with, and they do not know it.

Here are four marks to look for. Each one is a sign that a document that says it informs is
campaigning.

```table
| Mark | What it looks like |
| 1. Selective evidence | only the studies that support one option are cited, and the reader is not told how they were chosen |
| 2. Uncertainty changed | ranges, model assumptions and limits are dropped; or, the other way, doubt is made up about evidence the writer dislikes |
| 3. A value written as a finding | "the evidence shows we must..." — a judgement about what ought to be done, written as if a study had found it |
| 4. An interest not declared | the writer, the department or the funder stands to gain or lose from the decision, and the document does not say so |
```

Mark 3 is the commonest in health writing. The section before this one showed why it fails. A
premise, from Book 0, is a statement an argument rests on (`B0-R0-C42`). A conclusion about what
ought to be done needs a value premise: a premise that says what matters, or what ought to be done.
No study supplies one.

Mark 2 cuts both ways. A campaign for an option drops the doubt. A campaign against it often
adds doubt that the evidence does not carry. Both are the same fault.

People who write about researchers in policy use two other names. Oliver and Cairney, in a review
of advice to academics, put the choice as being an "honest broker" of the evidence or an "issue
advocate". They also say the line between the two is hard to find in practice. Be careful with
the first name. An earlier paper by the same two authors uses it differently. There, an "honest
broker" is a researcher who works with others to define the problem. When you meet the phrase, check
which sense the writer means.

The rule this book keeps is short. Decide which job a document does before you write its first
line. Then say so, in the document. "This note sets out the options and does not recommend one."
Or: "This letter argues for a higher tax." Either is honest. Leaving the reader to guess is not.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to treat a sentence with a number or a citation in it as informing. A campaign chooses which numbers to show. Ask what was left out before you ask whether what is there is right.
- Decide which job a document does before you write its first line. Then say so in its opening sentence: "This note sets out the options and does not recommend one." Or: "This letter argues for..." A reader can weigh either. A reader left to guess will assume the worse.
- Give a value its own sentence in a note meant to inform. Make that sentence say it is a choice. For example: "Whether the rate should rise is a choice between health, revenue and the cost to poorer buyers. This note does not make it." Then the committee can see where the evidence stops and the judgement starts.
- Refuse "the evidence shows we must" in any document you sign, including a trainee's draft you are correcting. Split it in two: what the evidence shows, in one sentence; what you think should be done, in another, marked as yours.
- Watch for doubt as well as for confidence. A document against an option campaigns by making the evidence look weaker than it is. If the studies agree and the summary says "the evidence is unclear", that is mark 2 run backwards.
- Declare your department's own position and money, even when nobody asks. An undeclared interest, once found, discredits the true sentences in the same note along with the false ones.
- The line between the two jobs is disputed. Some of the advice Oliver and Cairney reviewed says researchers who want influence must engage so far that it blurs. This book's rule does not deny that. It asks you to say which job each document does, so the blur is never hidden.
- The four marks tell you whether a document is honest about its job. They do not tell you whether its evidence is good or whether the option it argues for is right. A document can pass all four and still be wrong. Then read the evidence itself, as the sections on checking a reference and on what evidence can settle taught.

**Exercise 2** (critique). A made-up "evidence summary" reaches the district committee from a drinks makers' association. It
reads, in part:

> The evidence on sugary-drink taxes is mixed and uncertain. Taxes in some countries have not
> reduced obesity. A tax would hurt small shopkeepers and poorer families. The committee should
> therefore wait for better evidence before supporting any change.

Say which job the document does, which marks it carries, and what you would ask its authors for.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). Audience: a journalist, three minutes. They ask: "Your department wrote a note on the sugary-drinks
tax. Are you for it or against it?" Your note was written to inform. Your head of department has
said in public that the rate should rise. Give your answer, with one sentence the journalist can
quote.

**1.** Say whether each sentence informs or campaigns.

a. Option A costs more than option B and reaches fewer people.
b. Option B is the only responsible choice.
c. We recommend option A.
d. The study did not measure what happened after the first year.
e. Nobody who cares about children could oppose option A.

**2.** Each sentence comes from a document that calls itself a neutral summary. Name the mark each one
carries: 1 selective evidence, 2 uncertainty changed, 3 a value written as a finding, 4 an
interest not declared.

a. "Three studies found the option worked." The writer found five studies and left out the two
   that did not find it worked.

b. "The option will cut cases by a fifth." The study it cites gave a range from a twentieth to a
   fifth.

c. "The evidence shows we have a duty to act."

d. The summary was written by a company that sells the product the option would favour, and it
   does not say so.

e. "The evidence on this option is too uncertain to act on." The writer dislikes the option, and
   the studies agree closely with each other.

**3.** Rewrite this sentence so that it informs. Keep whatever in it could be a finding.

"The evidence proves that every school canteen must stop selling sugary drinks."
