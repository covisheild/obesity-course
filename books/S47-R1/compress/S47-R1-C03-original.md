# S47-R1-C03 · Where a body's power comes from

**Definition.** In India every lawful exercise of public power rests on an instrument above it. The chain runs
downward from the Constitution. Under article 245, Parliament may make laws for the whole or any
part of India, and a State Legislature for the whole or any part of its State. An Act made by a
legislature may give a named body the power to make rules or regulations, or to issue
notifications and orders. Each lower instrument must stay within the one above it. The Digital
Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, for example, lets the Central Government make rules "not
inconsistent with the provisions of this Act" (section 40(1)). For the purpose of article 13,
the Constitution counts any "Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification" having the
force of law as law.

A lower instrument normally names the provision it is made under, in words such as "in exercise
of the powers conferred by". So the answer to "under what authority?" is a named provision: an
article, a section, a rule.

Executive power is the second source. Article 73 extends the executive power of the Union to the
matters on which Parliament has power to make laws. Article 162 extends the executive power of a
State to the matters on which its Legislature has power to make laws. The words are "has power to
make laws", not "has made laws". On a matter where both may legislate, the Union's executive power
does not extend into a State unless the Constitution or a law made by Parliament expressly
provides for it (proviso to article 73). The State's executive power on such a matter is subject
to the executive power expressly conferred on the Union (proviso to article 162).

An Act can be passed and not yet in force. Its commencement section says how it comes into force.
Section 1(2) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 leaves the date to the Central
Government, by notification, and allows different dates for different provisions. The Act received
the President's assent on 11 August 2023. Notification G.S.R. 843(E) of 13 November 2025 brought
its provisions into force in three stages: some on the date of publication, some one year from it,
and the rest eighteen months from it.

**In plain terms.** The last section asked who holds the decide job. This one asks the next question: where does that
body's power come from?

Picture a ladder. The Constitution is the top rung. Below it are Acts, made by Parliament or a
State Legislature. An Act can hand a named body the power to make rules or regulations. Below
those are notifications and orders, each issued under some section that allows it. Every rung
stands on the one above. A rung cannot go further than the rung above lets it.

So "under what authority?" has a short answer: a named provision. "Section 92(1) of the Food
Safety and Standards Act" is an answer. "The government's powers" is not.

Here is the useful habit. Most notifications tell you their own rung. Near the start they say
"in exercise of the powers conferred by" and name a section. Read that phrase, open the section,
and you have climbed one rung.

There is a second source of power. A government can also act by executive decision — a decision
of the government itself, with no new Act behind it. Articles 73 and 162 say how far that
reaches: as far as the matters its legislature could make laws on.

Last, the trap. An Act can be passed and still not be in force. Its first section says when it
starts. Sometimes it starts in pieces, on different dates, each set by a notification.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to answer "under what authority?" with "the government's powers". That names no one and can be checked by no one. The answer is a named provision: an article, a section or a rule, with the body it gives the power to.
- Read a notification backwards. Find the words "in exercise of the powers conferred by", open the section they name, and then open the Act that section sits in. Two steps usually take you from an order to the Act behind it.
- An Act that has been passed is not always in force. Before you write "the Act requires", read its commencement section and find the notification that brought your section into force. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act received assent in August 2023, and much of it was still not in force in October 2026.
- When a decision names no Act, look at executive power. Articles 73 and 162 let the Union and a State act in the fields their legislatures may make laws on. Then ask for the order that records the decision and the body that took it. A decision made by executive order can be changed by another.
- Some matters are open to both Parliament and the State Legislatures. On those, do not assume the Union can act inside a State by executive decision. The proviso to article 73 says it cannot, unless the Constitution or an Act of Parliament expressly provides for it. Look for that express provision before you write to the Union.
- The chain tells you that a body claims a power and where the claim rests. It does not tell you that the power was used properly, that the body will use it, or that a court would uphold it. Write "X has power under Y". Do not write "X can lawfully do Z" unless a source says so.
