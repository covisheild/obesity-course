# S47-R1-C01 · A proposal is not a policy

**Definition.** A proposal is a course of action that someone wants a public body to take. It pairs a problem
with a solution. Until a body with the power to decide has decided it, it is only a proposal,
however widely it is supported.

Public policy is what government does about matters of concern to some part of society. One
textbook definition calls it "the relatively stable set of purposive governmental actions that
address matters of concern to some part of society". A legislature passing an Act, an executive
issuing an order and a court announcing a ruling all make public policy. Policy also includes the
outcomes government action creates, and a choice not to act. It has a written form, in Acts,
orders and policy statements, and an experienced form, in what officials and services actually do.
The two can differ.

A programme is the organised activity through which an agency carries a policy out. A law is one
place in which a policy can be recorded, among others such as an executive order or
a policy statement.

A common textbook model divides the policy process into four stages: agenda setting (made up of
problem identification and the specifying of alternative solutions), enactment, implementation
and evaluation, with evaluation feeding back into the others. Analysts who study policy as a
political process do not assume that these stages occur in order, or that every decision passes
through all of them.

Every policy promotes some behaviour and penalises other behaviour, so every policy has people
who gain and people who lose, if only those who pay for it.

**In plain terms.** Here is the setting for this book. You are a postgraduate in community medicine at a medical
college in Chhattisgarh. Your department wants to propose action on obesity among school children
and young adults in the district. For each idea, you must work out who would have to act, under
what power, and how to write about it.

Start with three words that people use as if they meant the same thing.

- A **proposal** is what someone wants a public body to do. "Tax sugary drinks more" is a
  proposal. It stays a proposal until a body with the power to decide has decided it.
- A **policy** is what a government has actually decided and done about a problem. It is
  written down somewhere you can open: an Act, an order, a policy statement. It also includes what
  happens on the ground, which can differ from what is written. Choosing not to act is a policy too.
- A **programme** is the work an office does to carry a policy out: the staff, the money, the
  forms, the visits.

A law — an Act, a rule, a regulation — is one kind of written policy. It is not the only kind.

Textbooks draw the life of a policy as four stages. A problem gets onto the agenda, with options
for solving it. A body decides on one. Offices carry it out. Someone checks whether it worked.

Use the stages as a list of jobs that must happen somewhere. Do not use them as a timetable. Real
decisions skip stages, go back, and happen out of order.

Last, every policy helps some people and costs others. Before you propose anything, write down who
would lose. They will be the first to speak against you.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to call a proposal a policy because many people support it, or because your department has agreed on it. Before you write "policy" about anything, say which body decided it and where the decision is written down. If you cannot, write "proposal".
- Ask three questions of any sentence that claims a policy exists. Who decided it? Does that body have the power to decide it? Where is the decision recorded in a form that binds anyone? A sentence that fails any one of them describes a proposal.
- A policy document can contain options that it does not decide. The National Health Policy 2017 says the Government "could consider" taxes on foods harmful to health. That sets no tax. When someone cites a policy for a measure, find the sentence and check that it decides, rather than considers.
- Policy is also what offices actually do, and that can differ from what the document says. When you describe a policy in a note, say whether you are describing the text or the practice. Do not claim the practice from the text alone.
- A decision not to act is policy too. If a body with the power to act has been asked and has not acted, write that as its current policy. Do not write that "there is no policy".
- Every proposal has losers, if only those who pay. Name them in your own note before anyone else does. Expect those who gain from things as they are to work against the change.
- The four stages are a teaching model, and real decisions do not follow them in order. Use the stages to ask which job is still missing for your proposal. Never use them to predict what happens next, or to argue that a decision "cannot" come before a problem has been agreed.

**Exercise 2** (critique). Each sentence below is made up. Say whether each describes a proposal, a policy, a programme, or
none of these yet. Then say what you would need to check.

1. "The district has a policy of promoting millets, since the collector spoke in favour of them at
   a school function."
2. "Under the National Health Policy 2017, India taxes foods harmful to health."
3. "The state has decided nothing about sugary drinks in school canteens, so there is no policy on
   them."

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A first-year resident has ten minutes and a whiteboard. They say: "Our unit has agreed that we
want a sugary-drinks tax. So it is our policy now." Explain the difference between a proposal and
a policy, and what the unit would have to find out next.
