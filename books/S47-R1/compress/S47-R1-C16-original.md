# S47-R1-C16 · What evidence can settle in a policy choice, and what it cannot

**Definition.** Evidence is a set of facts, known through observation, offered in support of a conclusion. It
is not the conclusion.

In a policy choice, evidence can answer three kinds of question: how big a problem is, and for
whom; what an option does, compared with doing something else; and what it costs. The choice
also turns on questions that evidence does not settle. Which outcomes matter, and how much,
is a question of values. Who gains and who loses can be described by evidence, but whether
that spread is fair is a value judgement. Whether an option can be carried out, with the
bodies, laws and money available, is its feasibility. Whether now is the time is a judgement of
timing.

So a conclusion of the form "body X should do Y" needs at least one value premise besides the
evidence. No study proves a policy. Evidence about one setting also needs a judgement of
whether it applies to another.

Evidence-informed policymaking names a process in which the evidence used, and the
judgements made about it, are found and stated systematically and openly. The decision is
still recognised as weighing other things. The researcher's part in it is to set out the
options with the evidence for each, its uncertainty, and the judgements the choice still needs.

**In plain terms.** The last section asked you to find the judgement in someone else's argument. This one finds it
in your own.

Evidence is facts you can check, offered in support of a conclusion. It is never the conclusion
itself.

Take any policy choice and split it into questions. Some of them, evidence can answer:

- **How big is the problem, and for whom?** A survey can count it.
- **What does an option do, compared with something else?** A study or a model can estimate it.
- **What does it cost?** That can be worked out.

Others, evidence cannot settle on its own:

- **Which outcomes matter most?** That is a question of values.
- **Who gains and who loses, and is that fair?** Evidence can say who. Whether it is fair is a
  value judgement.
- **Can it be done?** That depends on which body holds the power, what the law allows, and the
  money. This is called feasibility.
- **Is now the time?** That is a judgement of timing.

So any sentence that says "the government should" has a hidden premise. It is a value premise —
a premise that says what matters, or what ought to be done. Write it down, and the argument
becomes honest. Leave it hidden, and the evidence seems to say something it cannot say.

There is a name for doing this openly: evidence-informed policymaking. You find the evidence
carefully. You say plainly what it shows and how sure it is. And you say which judgements the
choice still needs, and leave those to be argued as judgements.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to write "the evidence shows we should". Evidence shows what is, or what an option is likely to do. "Should" needs a value premise. Write that premise out in your note, as a premise.
- Before you write a recommendation, split the choice into questions. Put "how big", "what it does" and "what it costs" on one side. Put "what matters", "is it fair", "can it be done" and "is now the time" on the other. Give each side its own sentences.
- A projected effect from a model, or a result from another country, is evidence about that setting. Using it for your district is a judgement that it applies. Say so in the same sentence as the number.
- Summan and colleagues report a modelling study of an extra health tax of {{n:summan_model_tax_range}}. It projects that demand for sugar-sweetened drinks could fall by {{n:summan_model_demand_fall}}. This course has not read that study. Quote it as their report, and never as a fall in obesity.
- When someone in a meeting says "the evidence is not settled", ask which question is unsettled. If it is a question evidence could answer, ask what study would answer it. If it is a value question, say so. Oxman and colleagues name a "spurious impression of uncertainty" as one way evidence is misused.
- Say how sure the evidence is, out loud, in the note. A decision made on admitted imperfect evidence can be changed when the results come in. One sold as certain cannot be changed without someone losing face.
- A trainee's discussion section often ends "hence the government should". Ask them to write the value premise that sentence needs, and to name the body that would act. Usually the sentence shrinks to what the study found.
- Sorting the questions shows which ones evidence can answer. It does not answer the value questions, and it does not tell you whether the evidence itself is any good. Judging a study's quality is a separate skill.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A made-up abstract ends: "Our survey found that most college canteens in the district sell
sugary drinks. Therefore the State must ban the sale of sugary drinks in college canteens."

Lay the argument out as premises and a conclusion. Name each premise the conclusion needs that
the survey does not supply, and say whether evidence could supply it.

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A journalist has three minutes. She asks: "Does the evidence prove that a tax on sugary drinks
works?" Answer her with no jargon, with honest confidence, and give her one sentence she could
quote.

**Exercise 3** (retrieval). From memory: name the three kinds of question evidence can answer in a policy choice, and the
four it cannot settle on its own. Then say what extra premise any sentence containing "should"
needs.
