# S47-R1-C04 · Union, State or local: whose subject is it

**Definition.** Article 246 of the Constitution divides the power to make law by subject. Parliament has
exclusive power over the matters in List I of the Seventh Schedule, the Union List. The
Legislature of a State has exclusive power, for that State, over the matters in List II, the
State List. Parliament and the State Legislatures both have power over the matters in List III,
the Concurrent List. Entry 97 of the Union List gives Parliament any matter, including any tax,
not named in the State or Concurrent List.

Where a State law on a Concurrent matter is repugnant to a law Parliament made, Article 254(1)
makes Parliament's law prevail. The State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy. Under
Article 254(2) a State law that was reserved for the President and received his assent prevails
in that State, until Parliament legislates again on the matter.

Goods and services tax sits outside the three Lists. Article 246A gives Parliament and every
State Legislature power to make laws on the goods and services tax imposed by the Union or by
that State.

Several entries bear on food and health. State List entry 6 is public health and sanitation,
hospitals and dispensaries. Concurrent List entry 18 is adulteration of foodstuffs and other
goods. Concurrent List entry 25 is education, subject to entries 63 to 66 of the Union List.
Concurrent List entry 33(b) is trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and
distribution of, foodstuffs. Union List entry 52 covers industries whose control by the Union
Parliament has declared by law to be expedient in the public interest. Section 2 of the Food
Safety and Standards Act, 2006 makes that declaration for the food industry.

Below the State sit the panchayats and the municipalities. Articles 243G and 243W let the
Legislature of a State, by law, endow them with powers and responsibilities, including for the
matters listed in the Eleventh Schedule (panchayats) and the Twelfth Schedule (municipalities).
Those Schedules list what a State may hand down. They do not themselves give a panchayat or a
municipality any power.

As of 2 October 2026, on the text of the Constitution as on 1 May 2026.

**In plain terms.** The last section climbed the ladder of a body's power to its top rung, the Constitution. This
section reads one part of that top rung: the part that says which government may make law on
which subject.

Book 0 named the three lists in the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution. Here you read them. Each
list is a numbered set of entries, and each entry is a subject in a few words. A subject on the
Union List belongs to Parliament. A subject on the State List belongs to each State's Legislature.
A subject on the Concurrent List belongs to both. If the two clash on a Concurrent subject,
Parliament's law usually wins.

Below the State there is a third tier: the panchayats in villages and the municipalities in towns.
They get their powers from a State law, not straight from the Constitution.

So one proposal can land on three governments at once. Your job is to find every entry it touches,
not the first one that fits.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Health is a State subject, so the State decides" is the commonest wrong answer. Public health is State List entry 6. But food is Concurrent (entries 18 and 33), and the Food Safety and Standards Act puts the food industry under Union control. Check every entry a proposal touches before you name a government.
- On a Concurrent subject, Parliament's Act wins a clash. The State law is void where the two clash (Article 254(1)). The one way round this is a State law sent to the President and signed by him (Article 254(2)). So before you propose a State rule on food, open the Union's food Act and read what it already covers.
- The Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules list what a State may give its local bodies. They do not list what a local body can do today. To say one can act, cite the section of the State Act that gives it the power.
- A tax on sugary drinks is not a Union-or-State question. Goods and services tax sits outside the three Lists. Article 246A gives Parliament and every State Legislature power over it.
- Write "whose subject" as an entry: "Concurrent List, entry 18", not "a Central subject". An entry number can be checked by anyone in a minute. "A Central subject" cannot be checked at all.
- The lists say which legislature may make law on a subject. They do not say whether a law exists, which body acts under it, or which of two overlapping entries governs. When two entries could both cover your proposal, say so in your note. Do not pick one and present it as settled.

**Exercise 1** (retrieval). Without looking back, write down:

1. The Article that divides law-making by subject, and the three Lists it names.
2. The list and entry for public health, and for adulteration of foodstuffs.
3. What happens when a State law on a Concurrent subject clashes with an Act of Parliament.
4. What Article 243G lets a State do, and which Schedule goes with it.

**Exercise 2** (interpretation). A colleague proposes that the district stop the sale of sugar-sweetened drinks within school
premises. Using only the Seventh, Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules and section 2 of the Food
Safety and Standards Act, list every entry the proposal touches. Then say what the entries
cannot tell you, and what you would open next.

**Exercise 3** (critique). A draft note says: "Food safety is on the Union List, so a State cannot make any law about food
sold in schools." Find the errors and rewrite the sentence.

**Exercise 4** (teaching). A first-year resident in your department has ten minutes and a whiteboard. She asks: "Why can't
we send our canteen proposal to the State health department?" Explain.
