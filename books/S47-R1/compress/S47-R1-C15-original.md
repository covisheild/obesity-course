# S47-R1-C15 · Reading the frame in a public argument

**Definition.** Reading the frame of a public argument is a procedure applied to one passage at a time. You
write down five things, and each one carries the passage's own words as its evidence.

The problem is what the passage says is wrong or at stake. The cause is whom or what it holds
responsible. The judgement is what its value words say: how bad the problem is, whose duty it
is, what is fair. The remedy is what it says should be done, and by whom. These four are the
four functions of a frame that Entman described, as Koon and colleagues report them. The fifth
is what the passage leaves out: the causes, remedies and actors that a rival account of the
same problem would put in.

A slot the passage does not fill is recorded as not stated. It is never filled from what the
reader already believes. Cue words and metaphors are clues to the frame, and the whole passage
decides it.

Two further steps complete the reading. The counter-frame is the problem, cause, judgement and
remedy that an opponent would give the same facts. And the procedure is run on the reader's own
writing, which has a frame like any other account.

**In plain terms.** The section on what a frame is gave you four parts: a problem, a cause, a judgement and a
remedy. This section turns them into something you do with a pen, on one passage at a time.

Write five lines. Next to each, copy the words from the passage that put it there.

1. **Problem.** What does the passage say is wrong, or at stake?
2. **Cause.** Whom or what does it hold responsible?
3. **Judgement.** Which value words does it use? Look for how bad, whose duty, what is fair.
4. **Remedy.** What does it say should be done, and who is to do it?
5. **Left out.** What would someone who sees the problem differently put in?

If you cannot copy words for a line, write "not stated". Do not fill the line from what you
already think. Passages often leave a slot empty, and the empty slot tells you something.

Watch for cue words — a word or short phrase that carries a frame in a few letters. A metaphor
is one kind: a "sea" of cheap food puts the cause outside the eater, and a "war" makes the
problem an enemy. A cue word is a clue, not a verdict. The whole passage decides the frame.

Then do two more things. Write the counter-frame — the problem, cause, judgement and remedy an
opponent would give the same facts. Then run all five lines on your own draft. Your note has a
frame too.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to read into a text the frame you expected to find. A rate on sugary drinks is not a health frame until the text's own words make it one. Copy words for every slot, and write "not stated" where there are none.
- For every passage you quote in a note, write five lines first: problem, cause, judgement, remedy and what it leaves out. Put the passage's words beside each line.
- The same idea can be praise in one frame and an accusation in another. "Raising revenue" is a reason for a food tax in the National Health Policy 2017 and a charge against a tax in its opponents' frame. Label the judgement from the words around the idea, never from the idea alone.
- Before you argue for a proposal in a meeting, write its counter-frame: the problem, cause, judgement and remedy an opponent would give. Then answer that frame in your note, not one you made up to be easy to answer.
- Never put a reason in a body's mouth that its own text does not give. If you want to say why a body acted, quote the document that says why, or write that its release states no reason.
- A trainee will often call a paragraph "neutral" because it opens with a fact. Ask them to fill the five lines from the paragraph's own words. The judgement and remedy usually turn up in the last sentence.
- One passage is not a body's frame, and one body is not "the government". Before you say how a government frames obesity, read several of its texts, and say which ones you read.
- The method tells you what a text says, and what it leaves out. It does not tell you whether the cause it names is the real cause, or whether its remedy would work. Those need evidence, and weighing them needs more than evidence.

**Exercise 1** (interpretation). A classmate writes in a draft note: "The Government of India frames obesity as a matter of
personal choice." Here are short passages from three Government of India texts.

- The Prime Minister, in his Mann Ki Baat address of 23 February 2025: "dealing with obesity
  is not just a personal choice but also our responsibility towards the family."
- The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), on its Eat Right India page:
  "FSSAI has embarked on a large-scale effort to transform the country's food system".
- The National Health Policy 2017: "seven priority areas for improving the environment for
  health", one of which is "Balanced, healthy diets and regular exercises."

For each passage, say whether it supports or goes against the classmate's sentence, and which
slot shows it. Then rewrite the sentence so it says only what these passages show.
