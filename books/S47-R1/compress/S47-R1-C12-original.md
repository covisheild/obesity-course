# S47-R1-C12 · What a frame is

**Definition.** To frame an issue is to choose which of its aspects an account makes prominent and which it
leaves out. In the form most used in health policy research, the choice serves four functions.
The account defines a problem, diagnoses its cause, passes a moral judgement and suggests a
remedy. These four are Entman's (1993), as reported by Koon, Hawkins and Mayhew in a 2016
review of framing research on health policy. Entman's paper is not held by this course.

A frame need not show all four. Selection alone is enough to frame. No account of finite
length can include every aspect of an issue, so every account, a researcher's included,
frames. The useful question is therefore which frame an account uses, never whether it uses
one.

The word is used loosely. In the studies Koon and colleagues reviewed, "frame" labelled
values, social problems, metaphors and arguments.

A frame can be episodic or thematic. An episodic frame tells an issue through one case or
event, with its details. A thematic frame takes the broad view: the trend over time and what
led to it.

Two neighbouring ideas are distinct from framing. Agenda setting is the choice of which issues
get attention at all, the part of the policy process that comes first. Priming is the process of predisposing a reader to think a particular way
about an issue, so that what they meet first colours a later judgement.

**In plain terms.** Finding who decides tells you where a proposal must go. It does not tell you how the problem
will be described when it gets there. That description matters as much as the address.

Any account of a problem leaves things out. It has to: no report, speech or note can say
everything. What it keeps in, and what it drops, is its frame.

A frame usually does four jobs. Write them as four questions and you can use them on anything
you read.

1. **Problem.** What does it say is wrong?
2. **Cause.** Whom or what does it say is to blame?
3. **Judgement.** What verdict does it pass: who is at fault, what is fair?
4. **Remedy.** What does it say should be done?

These four come from a writer named Entman, as a 2016 review by Koon and colleagues reports
them. Not every account carries all four. But every account leaves something out, so every
account has a frame. Your own notes do too.

Three more words travel with this one. An **episodic** frame tells the issue through one case:
one child, one shop, one day. A **thematic** frame tells the trend: what changed over years
and why. **Agenda setting** is choosing which issues get talked about at all, before any
frame is chosen. **Priming** is when what you read first tilts how you judge what comes next.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Our report just gives the facts; it has no frame" is a mistake. Every account leaves things out, so every account frames, yours included. When someone claims to be neutral, ask which frame they are using and what it leaves out.
- Before you agree or disagree with a paragraph, write its four labels: problem, cause, judgement, remedy. Then write one line on what it leaves out. Two accounts of the same problem usually part company in the cause row, as the two letters did, so look there first.
- When you cite the four functions, cite them as Entman's, as reported by Koon and colleagues (2016). This course has not read Entman's own paper, and you should not cite a paper you have not read.
- "Frame" is used for values, problems, metaphors and arguments, as Koon and colleagues found. If someone says a debate is "about framing", ask which of these they mean before you answer.
- Keep the three ideas apart in a meeting. Agenda setting decides whether obesity is discussed at all. Framing decides how it is described once it is. Priming is what a first story does to the next judgement. Calling all three "framing" loses the one you need.
- Labelling a frame tells you what a text chose to show. It does not tell you whether its cause is the true cause, or whether the frame changes anyone's mind. Those are questions for evidence. Never answer an argument only by naming its frame.
- The frame of a proposal decides where it gets sent. A remedy aimed at parents goes to schools; a remedy aimed at what canteens sell goes to whoever makes canteen rules. Choose the frame of your own note on purpose, and say it in the first lines.
