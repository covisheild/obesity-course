# S47-R1-C08 · Schemes and budgets: policy without a statute

**Definition.** A scheme is a programme the executive runs, with a name, a period, an amount of money and
guidelines saying how it works. Many Union schemes rest on no Act of their own. The Union
Cabinet approves the scheme for a fixed period with a stated outlay, and the money is spent
through the budget. The National Mission on Edible Oils – Oilseeds was approved by the Union
Cabinet in October 2024 for 2024-25 to 2030-31, with an outlay of Rs 10,103 crore.

Two articles of the Constitution carry the money. Under Article 112(1) the President lays
before both Houses of Parliament, every financial year, a statement of the estimated receipts
and expenditure of the Government of India, the annual financial statement. Under Article
282 the Union or a State may make grants for any public purpose, even where the purpose is
not one on which that legislature may make laws.

A centrally sponsored scheme is carried out by the States with money from both governments,
shared by a stated ratio. Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 is one. Its 2022
guidelines share the general components 60:40 between the Centre and the State in States
and Union Territories with a legislature. The share is 90:10 in the north-eastern and
Himalayan States, and 100:0 in Union Territories without a legislature.

A scheme can also be the way a right in an Act is delivered. The Poshan 2.0 guidelines are
issued under sections 4(a), 5(1)(a), 6 and 7 of the National Food Security Act, 2013. Section
7 has the States run schemes for those entitlements under guidelines, including cost sharing,
prescribed by the Central Government.

A national policy statement is a third kind of document. The National Health Policy 2017 was
approved by the Union Cabinet on 15 March 2017. It was not passed by Parliament, so it is not
a statute. It sets out aims and directions, and treats a legal right to health as a question
for a future bill.

**In plain terms.** Most of what governments in India do for nutrition and health is not done by an Act. It is done
by schemes. The Cabinet approves a scheme for a few years and gives it money. The ministry that
owns it writes guidelines. The States often carry it out and pay part of the cost.

Here is why that matters to you. A scheme is changed the way it was made: by the next approval,
or by the next budget. You do not need Parliament to start one, and nothing in law stops it
from ending.

A national policy, such as the National Health Policy 2017, is different again. The Cabinet
approved it. It says what the government means to do. It does not do it, and it is not a law.

One more turn. Some schemes are the way a right written in an Act reaches people. Then the
scheme can change while the right in the Act stays where it is.

**Must know points for you.**

- A national policy is not a law. The Union Cabinet approved the National Health Policy 2017; Parliament did not pass it. It says only that the government "could consider" taxes on foods having a negative impact on health. Quote it as an intention, never as a commitment or a rule.
- For any programme, write down four things before you argue about it. Who approved it? For what period? With what money, from whose budget? Is there an Act under it?
- "Public health is a State subject, so the Union cannot pay for it" is wrong. Article 282 lets the Union make grants for any public purpose, even outside the subjects Parliament may make laws on. A Union scheme can reach a subject on the State List.
- Under the 2022 Poshan 2.0 guidelines, supplementary nutrition in States with a legislature is shared 50:50 between Centre and State. A proposal to change that food asks two governments to pay, not one.
- Do not write "it is only a scheme" without checking. Poshan 2.0 delivers meals that sections 4, 5 and 6 of the National Food Security Act make a right. Changing the scheme cannot remove that right. Changing the right needs Parliament to amend the Act.
- A scheme's approval has an end date: 2030-31 for the oilseeds mission, 2025-26 for Poshan 2.0 as approved. Before you call a scheme current, find the decision that continued it. A proposal to change a scheme has its best chance at the next approval.
- An approval and an outlay tell you what was decided and budgeted. They do not tell you what was spent, what reached anyone, or whether it worked. Ask for spending and results separately.
