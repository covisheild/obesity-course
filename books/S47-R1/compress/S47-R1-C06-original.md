# S47-R1-C06 · How an Act is made

**Definition.** Every legislative proposal comes before Parliament as a Bill, a statute in draft. A Bill
introduced by a Minister is a Government Bill. A Bill introduced by a member who is not a
Minister is a Private Member's Bill. The Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and
Justice drafts Bills (Allocation of Business Rules, Second Schedule, Legislative Department,
item 1).

Before a Government Bill reaches Parliament, three steps apply inside the Union government. The
first is the Pre-legislative Consultation Policy, a decision of the Committee of Secretaries of
10 January 2014. It asks the department to put the draft, or at least its essentials, in the
public domain for a minimum of {{n:plcp_comment_days}} days. It also asks for a summary of the comments to be put on the department's
website, included in the note for the Cabinet, and placed before the Parliamentary Standing
Committee. Paragraph 11 lets a department record in the Cabinet note why consultation was not
feasible or desirable. The Policy is not a statute. The Transaction of Business Rules require
the Ministry of Law to be consulted on proposals for legislation (rule 4(3)(a)), and send cases
involving legislation to the Cabinet (Second Schedule, entry (a)).

In Parliament, a Bill may start in either House, unless it is a Money Bill (Article 107(1)). It
is introduced on a motion for leave, the first reading, and published in the Gazette. It may be
referred to a Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee. At the second reading the
House discusses its principle, may refer it to a Select or Joint Committee or circulate it for
opinion, and then takes it clause by clause. At the third reading the House passes it. The
other House then takes it through the same stages.

A Bill is passed only when both Houses agree to it in the same form (Article 107(2)). Where they
disagree, the President may call a joint sitting (Article 108), except on a Money Bill. A
passed Bill goes to the President, who assents or withholds assent; except for a Money Bill, he
may return it once for reconsideration (Article 111). It becomes an Act only on assent. An Act
may come into force later, on a date set by notification.

A Money Bill is a Bill that contains only provisions on the matters in Article 110(1), the first
of which is the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration or regulation of any tax. It cannot
be introduced in the Rajya Sabha (the Council of States). The Rajya Sabha may only recommend
amendments, within fourteen days, which the Lok Sabha (the House of the People) may accept or
reject (Article 109). The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill
(Article 110(3)).

A State Act follows its own Legislature's procedure, and a Bill passed there goes to the
Governor, who assents, withholds assent or reserves it for the President (Article 200).

**In plain terms.** The section on where a body's power comes from put Acts on the second rung of the ladder, under
the Constitution. The last section showed that a new Act needs a Cabinet decision before
Parliament sees it. This section follows a Bill from the department's desk to the Gazette.

A Bill is a statute in draft. Most Bills come from a minister's department. The Union's own
policy asks the department to put the draft in public for at least {{n:plcp_comment_days}}
days first, so that anyone can comment. But that policy is not a law, and a department can skip
it if it writes down why.

Then the Cabinet approves the Bill and it goes to Parliament. Each House reads it three times.

In the first reading it is introduced. In the second, members debate it and go through it clause
by clause, and it may be sent to a committee. In the third, the House votes to pass it. Then the
other House does the same. When both Houses have agreed to the same text, the President signs
it. Only then is it an Act. Even then it may not start until a notification says so.

A Bill that only deals with tax is a Money Bill, and the Lok Sabha has the last word on it.

For you, two doors matter most: the public consultation before the Bill, and the committee that
examines it.

**Must know points for you.**

- Do not count on {{n:plcp_comment_days}} days to comment on a draft Bill. The Pre-legislative Consultation Policy is a decision of senior civil servants, not a law. A department may skip it and record its reasons in the Cabinet note. In 2022 the Law Ministry told Parliament it keeps no record of compliance. Watch the department's website, and be ready early.
- Your evidence can enter at two sourced points. One is the pre-legislative consultation, if it is held. The other is a committee examining the Bill, which can take evidence from experts. When a Bill is referred to a committee, send a short memorandum with your evidence.
- "Parliament has passed the Bill" is not yet "the law says". A Bill becomes an Act only when both Houses have agreed to the same text and the President has assented. An Act may then wait for a notification before it is in force. Ask for all three before you quote a new law as binding.
- Parliament had passed 14 Private Members' Bills in its first 60 years, and none since 1970 (PRS Legislative Research, 2012). Do not describe a Private Member's Bill as a likely route to a new law.
- A Bill that only deals with tax is a Money Bill. It starts in the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha can only recommend changes, within fourteen days, and the Lok Sabha may reject them all. The Speaker decides what counts as a Money Bill. On a tax law, the Lok Sabha decides.
- These steps are the Union Parliament's, from a Rajya Sabha booklet of 2005 and PRS figures of 2012. A State Bill goes through the State Legislature and then to the Governor, who may reserve it for the President (Article 200). The State Assembly's own rules are not read here. Open them before you advise anyone on a Bill in Chhattisgarh.
