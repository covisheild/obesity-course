# S01-R1-C08 · Spotting a claim that breaks conservation

**Definition.** A weight-loss claim breaks conservation when the fat change it asserts could not have
happened given the energy that could plausibly have crossed the boundary drawn round the
person over the stated time. Checking a claim against this test has three steps.

First, draw the boundary and list the routes energy can cross it: in, as food and drink;
out, as heat, as work done on things around the person, and in whatever is not absorbed.
An explanation that names no route is not yet an explanation, whatever else it says.

Second, put a number on the claimed fat change, using the static rule's own conversion
from this subject's C07: 3,500 kilocalories for each pound, about 7,716 for each
kilogram. This gives the energy the claim requires to have crossed, if the change is
entirely fat.

Third, compare that required amount with the largest deficit that has plausibly crossed
the person's boundary over the stated time. A deficit is bounded above by the person's
total expenditure over that time, because intake cannot be negative: even eating nothing
at all, the largest possible deficit in a day is that day's whole expenditure. A claim
whose required energy exceeds every plausible deficit for the stated interval is
impossible on energy grounds alone, regardless of what method is claimed to produce it.

One case does not fail this test even though it looks alarming at first. Weight lost in
the first days of a diet, especially one that cuts carbohydrate sharply, is often faster
than the deficit alone would predict. The reason is not a broken boundary. The body
stores some energy as glycogen, and Hall and colleagues note that the energy stored in a
given mass of carbohydrate, fat and protein "varies considerably, especially when
accounting for the intracellular water associated with stored glycogen and protein".
Glycogen is stored together with water. Using up glycogen releases that water as well,
and a loss of water is a loss of mass with no fat energy behind it at all. Fast early
loss that is mostly glycogen and its water is not a violation; it is a different
substance leaving the boundary, at a different energy cost per kilogram than fat.

**In plain terms.** Somebody claims a person lost a certain amount of fat in a certain number of days. Is that
possible? You do not need to know anything about the diet to check. You need three things:
a boundary, a number, and a limit.

Draw the boundary round the person. Food and drink cross in. Heat, work done on things
around the person, and whatever the body does not absorb cross out. If a claim gives no
route at all for how the change happened, that is already a problem, whatever else it
says.

Turn the claimed fat change into an energy number, using the rate from the previous
section: 3,500 kilocalories for a pound, about 7,716 for a kilogram.

Now the limit. Over any stretch of time, the biggest possible deficit a person can run is
their whole expenditure for that stretch, because you cannot eat less than nothing.
Somebody who ate absolutely nothing for a day still could not have burned more than that
day's total expenditure. So if the energy the claim requires is bigger than any plausible
deficit for the time given, the claim cannot be true. It does not matter what method is
claimed. No method gets round the boundary.

One thing looks like a violation and is not. In the first few days of a diet, especially
one that cuts carbohydrate hard, people often lose weight faster than their deficit alone
would explain. That is because the body stores some energy as a starch-like fuel called
glycogen, and glycogen is stored together with water. Burn through the glycogen and the
water that went with it leaves too. That is real weight loss. It is not fat, and it is not
a broken boundary, because it did not need anywhere near as much energy to cross as a
fat-only figure would suggest. Fast, and mostly water, are not the same as impossible.

**Illustration.** Open the US Federal Trade Commission's "Gut Check" reference guide for spotting false
weight-loss claims, at https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/gut-check-reference-guide-media-spotting-false-weight-loss-claims.
It lists seven claim types the regulator says are false on their face, and gives its own
example wording for each. These examples are the FTC's own illustrations of a claim
type, not real advertisements: treat the wording the same way you would treat a specimen
handed to you to test, never as evidence that any real product said it.

Claim 1's own example reads: "Lose up to 2 pounds a day without diet or exercise."

Draw the boundary round a person making this claim work for a week. Turn 2 pounds a day
into an energy requirement using the static rule.

```working
    2 times 3500 = 7000
```

7,000 kilocalories a day, every day, as fat alone.

Now find a real number to compare it with. Thomas and colleagues' pooled study put 103
adults on supervised diets, with every calorie prescribed and measured. The largest
average deficit anyone in that pooled group sustained, across the whole cohort, over
weeks, under total dietary control, was 1,439 kilocalories a day.

```table
what                                     kcal/day
the FTC specimen's implied deficit        7,000
the largest deficit Thomas et al. measured under total supervision   1,439
```

The claim requires roughly five times the biggest deficit ever measured in that
supervised cohort, and it claims this happens "without diet or exercise" — naming no
route at all for where 7,000 kilocalories a day would come from. Whatever a boundary is
drawn round, food in and output out are the only routes there are, and a claim that
invokes neither cannot supply the energy it asserts.

Contrast this with a real case that looks alarming and is not. A person starting a
low-carbohydrate diet loses 3 kilograms in the first five days. Using the static rule,
that would require:

```working
    3 times 7716.18 = 23148.54
    23148.54 divided by 5 = 4629.71
```

About 4,630 kilocalories a day as fat, which is itself an implausible deficit for most
people over five days. But this loss is not a fat claim. Cutting carbohydrate sharply
depletes the body's glycogen store fast, and glycogen carries intracellular water with
it, which Hall and colleagues name directly. Three kilograms lost mostly as glycogen and
its water costs far less energy than three kilograms of fat, so the boundary is not
broken; the mistake would be assuming every kilogram lost is a fat kilogram, which the
static rule's own rate is never entitled to assume for the first days of a diet.

**Where this picture breaks.** This test rules claims out; it does not rule anything in. A claimed fat loss that stays
under the expenditure limit is merely not disproved by this check, and could still be
false for other reasons this test cannot see, such as a made-up study or a biased
measurement. The test also needs a real time interval and a real bound on expenditure to
be worth anything: applied to "over a lifetime" or with no stated period, almost any
total becomes achievable and the check stops discriminating. And it only tests the fat
figure against energy; it says nothing about whether the reported change was fat,
glycogen, water, or measurement error, which is a separate question this concept's
glycogen-and-water case exists to flag before someone applies the energy check to the
wrong substance.

**Must know points for you.**

- A deficit is bounded above by total expenditure for the period, because intake cannot go below zero. If the energy a claim requires exceeds every plausible expenditure for the stated time, the claim is impossible, whatever mechanism it names.
- A claim that names no route for the energy — "without diet or exercise" is the common wording — has not merely failed to explain itself. It has claimed a crossing with no route, which this subject's B0-R0-C33 already rules out for any boundary, a person's included.
- Fast early weight loss, especially on a diet that cuts carbohydrate sharply, is often real and is often mostly glycogen and the water stored with it, not fat. Do not run the energy check on it using the fat rate; doing so manufactures a false violation out of a substance the rule was never about.
- When a journalist brings you a headline figure, ask for the time period before doing anything else. The same total kilocalories looks impossible over a week and unremarkable over a year, and the check cannot even be run without that number.
- This check clears a claim of one specific defect, an energy impossibility. It does not verify the study behind the claim, the measurement, or the honesty of who reported it. Say what the check did and did not do, every time you report its result.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A magazine advertisement, invented for this exercise, reads: "Our patch helped users
lose 1 pound a day for 10 days — with no change to diet or exercise." Say what is

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A first-year resident has ten minutes and a whiteboard. They ask: "A patient tells me
they lost 5 kilograms of pure fat in one week eating normally. How do I know if that's
even physically possible before I ask about their diet?"

**1.** A claimed weight change is 6 units of mass, said to be entirely fat. Using a rate of
3,500 for each unit, work out the total energy the claim requires.

**2.** A claimed energy crossing totals 24,500. Using a rate of 3,500 for each unit of mass,
work out how many units of mass that could account for, if the crossing were entirely
fat.

**3.** The FTC's own Claim 1 specimen wording reads "Lose up to 2 pounds a day without diet
or exercise." This is the regulator's illustration of a false claim type, not a real
advertisement. Using the static rule, work out the daily energy requirement the
specimen implies, in kilocalories, treating the claimed loss as entirely fat.

**4.** Thomas and colleagues' pooled cohort achieved an average deficit of 1,439 kcal a day
under total dietary supervision. Work out what multiple of that deficit the 7,000 kcal
a day figure from the previous problem represents, to one decimal place.

**5.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a claim says a person lost 4 kg of fat in a week eating nothing but water
    the body obeys the laws of physics, and energy is conserved
    so wherever the 4 kg's worth of energy went, it had to come from somewhere
    therefore the claim must be true, because energy cannot simply vanish
```

**6.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke.

```working
    a person starts a strict low-carbohydrate diet and loses 3 kg in the first five days
    the static rule needs 7716.18 kcal per kg
    3 times 7716.18 = 23148.54
    23148.54 divided by 5 = 4629.71 kcal/day required
    nobody can run a deficit that size for five days, so the reported loss must be fabricated
```

**7.** A social media post says: "I lost 8 kg of pure fat in 10 days on this new plan — that
proves calories don't matter, only what you eat does." Decide what to compute, compute
it, and say what your answer does not establish.

**8.** A colleague at a conference says: "A bariatric patient of mine dropped 12 kg in the

just calories in versus calories out, nothing else going on." Decide what to compute,
