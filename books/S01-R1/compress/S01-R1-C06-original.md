# S01-R1-C06 · The first law for a body in one sentence, without a lever

**Definition.** The one-sentence statement: a person's energy stores change only by the energy that crosses
the boundary drawn round them, and the rate of that change equals energy intake minus energy
expenditure. `S01-R1-C05` derives this line; this record fixes what the line is and is not an
argument for.

An identity of this kind constrains the numbers on either side of it and names nothing else.
It does not name which route energy took, why intake or expenditure moved, or which of the two
changed first. Treat "energy in minus energy out" as a premise in the sense `B0-R0-C42` gives
that word: a statement offered as true, from which a further statement is meant to follow by
inference. The inference that most often gets attached to it — that a person should therefore
eat less — does not follow from the identity alone. It needs a further premise, about what
happens to expenditure and appetite when intake changes, that the identity itself is silent
on.

Because the identity fixes only the arithmetic, it is consistent with every account of why
weight changes that respects conservation of energy: a body that overeats because of appetite
signalling, a body whose expenditure falls in response to a change in intake, a body whose
food environment changed around it. All of them can be written so the two sides balance. The
identity cannot be used to choose among them, and treating it as though it already had is the
fault this record exists to name.

**In plain terms.** Here is the whole identity again, in one line.

```working
    the change in a person's energy stores = energy intake minus energy expenditure
```

Read it as a rule about arithmetic, because that is all it is. It says the two sides have to
match. It does not say which side moved first, or why, any more than "the amount inside a
boundary changes only by what crosses it" said why a burner was lit.

A step people take next, all the time, sounds almost like it follows from the line above. "So
if you want your stores to stop rising, eat less." That step is a separate claim. It is an
inference, in the sense the section on arguments gave that word: a move from a premise to a
conclusion. The premise here is true. The inference needs something the identity never supplied
— that cutting intake actually lowers the left-hand side, rather than expenditure also falling
to keep the difference where it was.

Nothing said here claims that inference is wrong. It says the identity by itself cannot tell you
whether it is right, because the identity is silent about what happens to expenditure when
intake changes. Whatever the true answer is, it has to come from evidence about bodies, not from
the accounting line.

And that silence cuts both ways. Anyone who tells a different story about why weight changes —
appetite driven by hormones, expenditure that falls when a person eats less, an environment that
makes overeating easy — can write their story so the two sides of the line still balance. The
identity does not rule any of them out. It rules out only a story where the two sides fail to
add up, and almost nobody is telling that story.

**Illustration.** Lay the "eat less" step out the way `B0-R0-C42` teaches, as a premise, an inference, and a
conclusion, and test the inference before asking whether the premise is true.

*Premise:* the change in a person's energy stores equals energy intake minus energy
expenditure.

*Conclusion:* if this person eats less, their energy stores will fall.

Grant the premise. It is the identity from `S01-R1-C05`, and nothing about it is in dispute
here. Now ask the inference test's question: does the conclusion follow?

It does not, not on the premise alone. The premise names two quantities, intake and
expenditure, and says how their difference relates to the change in stores. It says nothing
about what happens to expenditure if intake is reduced. If expenditure stayed exactly where it
was, the conclusion would follow. If expenditure fell by the same amount intake fell, the
difference would stay where it was, and the conclusion would fail, while the identity held
exactly as before.

Both of those are compatible with the one sentence in the premise. The identity cannot tell
you which one happens in a real body. That is a separate, empirical question, and it belongs
to concepts later in this rung, not to this one.

Picture the missing premise written out loud, the way `B0-R0-C42`'s own illustration does it:
"and expenditure does not change when intake does." Nobody who makes the "eat less" argument
usually says that sentence. It is doing the work anyway, hidden, and it is a claim about
physiology, not a consequence of the accounting line above it.

**Where this picture breaks.** This illustration tests one inference, from one premise, and it does not settle whether "eat
less" works as an instruction. It may or it may not; that is an empirical question about how
expenditure and appetite respond, and this record does not carry the evidence either way.

It also does not mean every conclusion drawn from the identity is unsupported. "If a person's
energy stores fell over a stretch of time, then intake was below expenditure over that
stretch, once both are counted correctly" does follow from the premise alone, because it says
nothing about what caused either side to move. The inference that fails is the one that adds a
direction of control the identity never supplied.

And the missing-premise move stops working once somebody states the extra premise openly and
defends it with evidence. At that point the argument no longer rests on the identity doing
work it cannot do; it rests on whatever evidence was offered, which is a different thing to
evaluate.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Energy in minus energy out" is a premise, not a conclusion. The step from it to "so eat less" is a separate inference, and the identity alone does not carry it, because the identity says nothing about what expenditure does when intake changes.
- When you hear the identity used to defend a specific instruction, ask what has been assumed about expenditure that was never stated. Naming that hidden premise is usually the whole of the disagreement.
- The identity is consistent with every serious account of why weight changes, because every one of them can be written so the two sides balance. It cannot be used to choose between them, and citing it as if it settles a causal dispute settles nothing.
- In front of a patient or in public, keep the arithmetic and the explanation separate on purpose. Say what changed, in energy terms, if you know it. Do not let the identity stand in for an account of why it changed, which you may not have.
- A trainee who has just learned this identity will reach for "energy in, energy out" as an explanation the first time someone asks why a patient's weight moved. Watch for the sentence doing the work of an inference nobody has actually made.
- When a journalist repeats "it's just calories in, calories out", the accurate short reply is that the arithmetic is real and settles nothing about cause. That sentence is calibrated honesty, not a hedge, and it is more defensible in the room than either agreeing outright or dismissing the arithmetic.
