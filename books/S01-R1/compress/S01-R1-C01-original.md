# S01-R1-C01 · Body fat as stored chemical energy; fat mass and fat-free mass

**Definition.** Fat is stored in the body as triglyceride, packed inside fat cells, adipocytes, which
together make up adipose tissue. Absorbed fat leaves the gut inside chylomicrons and, once
it reaches the circulation, is either used at once or taken up and stored this way. Adipose
tissue is the body's fat depot, and it holds a large amount: several months of energy
expenditure, held as fat.

Body weight is not one substance on a scale. Split it into two compartments. Fat mass is
the triglyceride held in adipose tissue. Fat-free mass, also called lean mass, is
everything else the body weighs: muscle, bone, organs, blood and the water inside them.
Fat-free mass is the metabolically active part of the two, and it is larger, not smaller,
in a person with more body fat: a person with obesity carries more fat-free mass alongside
more fat mass, not fat mass in place of it.

So a kilogram on the scale is a kilogram of some mixture of fat mass and fat-free mass, and
the two compartments are not interchangeable: they are different tissue, doing different
work, and (rung 2 gives) holding different amounts of energy for the same weight. Naming a
change in body weight is not yet naming a change in fat.

**In plain terms.** Where does fat go once it is eaten? Nowhere exotic. It ends up inside fat cells, and the
form it is held in there has a name: triglyceride. Pack enough fat cells together and you
have adipose tissue, the fatty layer and the pockets of fat scattered round a body. That is
the store.

It is a large store. For most people, the fat sitting in adipose tissue holds more spare
energy than anything else in the body does.

Now a question that sounds too simple to need an answer. When somebody weighs 70 kilograms,
is all 70 kilograms fat?

No. Split the number in two.

One part is fat mass: the triglyceride in the adipose tissue, exactly what the last two
paragraphs described.

The other part is fat-free mass, sometimes called lean mass. It is everything that is left
once you take the fat out. Muscle. Bone. The organs. Blood. The water sitting inside all of
it.

A person carrying more body fat is not simply carrying fat mass in place of fat-free mass.
They carry more of both. A bigger body, in the sense of more fat, tends to come with more
muscle and more everything else as well, not less.

So weight on a scale is one number covering two different things. A kilogram gained or lost
could be mostly fat, mostly the other compartment, or some mixture, and the scale alone
cannot tell you which.

**Illustration.** You are handed a weighing scale and a number: 90 kilograms.

Ask what that number is made of before you ask anything else about it.

Open the review, Hall and Guo, "Obesity energetics", *Gastroenterology* 2017, at
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568065/. Open your browser's find box and search
for fat-free mass.

> It has long been recognized that fat-free mass comprises the metabolically active tissues
> of the body and therefore contributes more to REE than body fat.

Read on, in the same paragraph.

> Fat-free mass is elevated in obesity, along with body fat, resulting in increased REE
> compared with lean subjects.

Two people can carry the same 90 kilograms and split it differently between fat mass and
fat-free mass. Neither split is visible in the number 90. It has to be measured separately,
by a method this section does not give you.

Now search the same page for vast majority.

> Body fat represents the vast majority of energy stores in the body compared with the
> energy content of body protein and glycogen.

So the fat-mass part of the 90 kilograms is where almost all the spare energy sits. The
fat-free part is where almost all of the metabolically active tissue sits. They are not two
labels for the same thing. They are two different compartments, doing two different jobs,
and a number on a scale reports only their sum.

**Where this picture breaks.** This record gives you the split, fat mass against fat-free mass, and nothing about how to
measure it in a real person, and nothing about what fraction either compartment usually is.
It does not say how much of adipose tissue's own weight is fat rather than water and other
tissue: that needs a source this book does not hold, and no figure for it is given here.

It does not say how much energy either compartment holds for each kilogram. That is the
next concept.

"More fat mass tends to come with more fat-free mass" is the pattern Hall and Guo report
for obesity. It is not a rule that a kilogram gained by any one person, on any one day, must
split in a fixed proportion between the two compartments.

**Must know points for you.**

- A kilogram of body weight is not a kilogram of fat. It is some mixture of fat mass and fat-free mass, and the two are different tissue doing different work. So never read a change on a scale as a change in fat without saying what you know, or do not know, about which compartment moved.
- A person carrying more body fat generally carries more fat-free mass too, not less. Fat mass does not simply replace fat-free mass as weight rises. Do not picture obesity as one compartment growing while the other stays fixed.
- This record names the two compartments and says fat-free mass is the metabolically active one. It gives no number for how big either compartment usually is and no method for measuring the split in a person in front of you. Ask for a method before you accept a number for either compartment.
