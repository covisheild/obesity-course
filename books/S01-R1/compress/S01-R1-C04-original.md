# S01-R1-C04 · What expenditure is made of, and why a bigger body spends more

**Definition.** Daily energy expenditure is made of three components, and they are not equal in size.

Resting energy expenditure, the energy spent keeping the body alive and running while it
does no physical work, is the largest of the three. The basal metabolic rate is the same
quantity measured under a stricter set of conditions: awake, at complete rest, in a
thermally neutral setting, after a fast long enough that digestion is finished. Resting
energy expenditure is not fixed for a person. It rises with fat-free mass, because
fat-free mass is the metabolically active tissue, and it is also higher, in absolute terms,
in a person carrying more body fat, because that person generally carries more fat-free
mass as well.

The thermic effect of food is the energy spent digesting, absorbing and processing what was
eaten. It is the smallest of the three components, and it varies with what was eaten:
protein raises it more than carbohydrate, which raises it more than fat.

Physical activity expenditure is the energy spent moving, whether as deliberate exercise or
as the ordinary movement of a day. Its size is set by how long and how hard the movement is,
scaled to body weight: carrying a heavier body through the same walk costs more energy than
carrying a lighter one.

All three depend on the body doing the spending, chiefly on its size and its composition.
Because of that, a bigger body's maintenance requirement, the daily intake that would hold
its weight steady, is higher than a smaller body's, and that requirement falls when weight,
and with it fat-free mass, is lost. No equation for computing any of the three from a
person's measurements is given here; the point is what each component is and that it moves
with body size, not how to calculate it.

**In plain terms.** Every day, a body spends energy on three different kinds of work, and it does not spend the
same amount on each.

Most of it goes on staying alive, doing nothing else. Keep the heart beating, the brain
running, the cells replacing themselves, the body warm. That is resting expenditure, and it
is the biggest of the three by a wide margin. It is bigger in a bigger body, because more
tissue means more of that background work. The last section split the body into fat mass and
fat-free mass, and fat-free mass is the part doing most of this work. A person with more
fat-free mass spends more at rest, whether or not they move at all that day.

A smaller share goes on the meal itself. Eating and digesting food costs energy, and the size
of that cost depends on what was eaten. Protein takes more effort to process than
carbohydrate, and carbohydrate takes more than fat. This is the smallest of the three shares.

The rest goes on movement: everything from a deliberate workout to walking to the bus. Its
size depends on how long you move and how hard, and it scales with how much body you are
moving. Carry more weight through the same walk and it costs more.

Put the three together and a pattern falls out with no extra assumption needed. A bigger body
spends more, mostly because resting expenditure and movement both scale with size. And when a
body gets smaller, whether from illness or from a deliberate change, that same logic runs
backwards: the energy it takes to keep that smaller body where it is, its maintenance
requirement, falls too.

None of this tells you a formula. It tells you what is inside the number "daily expenditure",
and why it is not the same number for every body.

**Illustration.** Two patients come to the same clinic on the same day, both adults, both sedentary. One
weighs 55 kilograms. The other weighs 95 kilograms, of which a larger fat-free mass is part.

Ask which of them has the higher daily energy expenditure, before checking anything.

Open the review, Hall and Guo 2017, at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568065/,
and search for linearly related.

> Across a wide range of weights, REE is linearly related to both fat-free mass and body
> fat, such that the elevated REE with obesity is generally in line with what is expected
> for the body weight and composition.

The heavier patient's resting expenditure, the largest of the three components, is expected
to be higher, because a heavier body generally carries more fat-free mass, and fat-free mass
is what resting expenditure tracks.

Now search the same page for proportion to overall body weight.

> The energy expended in physical activities is determined by their duration and intensity
> in proportion to overall body weight.

Send both patients on the same ten-minute walk, same pace. The heavier one spends more
energy doing it, for the same reason a heavier delivery costs more to carry the same
distance: the same movement, done on more mass, costs more.

Now imagine the heavier patient loses ten kilograms over the following year, some of it
fat-free mass along with the fat. Search the same page for declines with weight loss.

> physical activity energy expenditure declines with weight loss unless its quantity or
> intensity increases to compensate.

A smaller body doing the same daily activities spends less doing them. That is not a
failure of anything. It is the same size-dependence running in the other direction, and it
is one reason the intake that once held a person's weight steady stops holding a lower
weight steady, once the body doing the spending has changed.

**Where this picture breaks.** This illustration compares two bodies at one point in time and one body before and after a
change; it gives no formula for predicting either patient's actual expenditure from their
weight, and no number for how much resting expenditure or physical activity expenditure
changes for a given change in weight.

"Bigger body, more expenditure" holds for resting expenditure and physical activity
expenditure, which this record has sourced. It is not established here for the thermic
effect of food, which this record ties to what was eaten, not to body size.

The pattern describes what is generally expected for a given body weight and composition.
It is not a claim about any one named person, and it says nothing about appetite or intake,
which rung 2 of this subject takes up separately.

**Must know points for you.**

- Daily expenditure is not one thing. It is resting expenditure, the largest share; the thermic effect of food, the smallest; and physical activity expenditure. Naming "metabolism" as if it were a single dial ignores that three different processes make it up, with different sizes and different things that move them.
- Resting expenditure rises with fat-free mass, and a person carrying more body fat generally carries more fat-free mass too. So do not assume a person with obesity has a slow metabolism; the sourced pattern is the opposite; their resting expenditure is generally higher in absolute terms than a leaner person's, in line with their larger body.
- Both resting expenditure and physical activity expenditure scale with body size. So a maintenance requirement worked out for a person at one weight does not hold once their weight, and the fat-free mass carried with it, has changed. Expect the number that once held their weight steady to fall as their weight falls.
- This record names the three components and says each depends on body size. It gives no equation for basal metabolic rate and no number for how much any component changes with a given change in weight. Do not use it to compute a patient's requirement; use it to say what a computed requirement would have to account for.
