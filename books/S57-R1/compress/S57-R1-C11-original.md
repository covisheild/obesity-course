# S57-R1-C11 · Running one session around retrieval rather than transmission

**Definition.** A session built around retrieval is one in which the learners spend much of the time
bringing material back from memory, each alone and in writing, and seeing the correct answer
afterwards. The teacher explains in short parts between those attempts. It is set against a
session built around transmission, in which the learners mostly listen.

The session plan is a written table of the session's parts, in order. Each row gives the
minutes, what the teacher does and what every learner does. A plan for one session built
around retrieval has these parts.

1. The objective, stated to the learners at the start.
2. A pre-test on one of two parallel forms.
3. Short teaching parts. Each is followed by one question that every learner answers alone,
   in writing, without notes, and then by the correct answer.
4. One question on material from an earlier session, answered from memory.
5. A closing free recall: notes away, each learner writes down all they can about the topic.
6. A post-test on the other form.
7. A delayed test, booked before the session, on a date written into the plan.

The questions ask for the objective's action, in the same format as the tests, on cases not
used in the teaching.

**In plain terms.** Most sessions are built around the teacher talking. The learners listen, and at the end
someone asks "any questions?". This section builds a session the other way round. The learners
spend much of the hour pulling what they have just been taught back out of memory, in writing,
one at a time. You explain in short bursts between their attempts.

The earlier sections gave you the pieces. An objective that can be checked. A test that
checks it, in two forms. The finding that trying to recall teaches more than hearing it again,
if the learner sees the right answer afterwards. The finding that spreading practice out
helps. This section puts them in order on one page, the session plan, with the minutes for
each part.

Then you count. How many of the 60 minutes did every learner spend answering from memory? In a
lecture the answer is close to none. In this plan it is 40.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Any questions?" and a question to one student are not retrieval practice for the room. One learner recalls and the rest listen. Ask a question every learner answers alone, in writing, before anyone speaks.
- Write the plan as a table before the session: minutes, what you do, what every learner does. Then add up the minutes in which every learner answers from memory. If the total is small, change the plan, not the count.
- A pre-test on material the learners never met measures where they start. It is not practice. Weinstein and colleagues report that retrieval with very low success is unlikely to help memory, especially without feedback. Do not count it as part of the retrieval in your plan.
- Questions spread through a session have not been shown to beat the same questions at its end. In the one study Weinstein and colleagues report, which used online learning modules, the placement made no difference on a later test. Spread them for other reasons: short teaching parts, attention, a turn for every learner. Do not tell colleagues the spreading itself is proven.
- Give the in-session questions the same format as the tests. In the classroom studies, effects were larger when practice and final test matched in format. Change the case, not the kind of question.
- Book the delayed test before the session and write its date in the plan. A session with no delayed test can show what learners did on the day, never what lasted.
- A plan that meets every part here says what the learners did. It does not show that they learned more than they would have from a lecture. Only the delayed test, and a comparison, can show that. Present your plan as a design, not as a result.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A senior resident runs a journal club for eight residents. Her plan: she presents the trial for
30 minutes. Then she picks residents one at a time and asks each a question about it. Their
answers are marked, and the marks count in their formal assessment. The session ends there.

Name each place where this plan departs from a session built around retrieval. For each, say
what you would change.

**Exercise 2** (build). This book's build: deliver one teaching session built around retrieval, with a written
objective. Choose a real group: a batch of MBBS students, residents, or a session for ASHA
workers. Before the session, prepare and date these.

1. The objective, with all six parts.
2. Two parallel forms of the test, and how the group splits between them.
3. The session plan as a table: minutes, what you do, what every learner does.
4. The questions for each teaching part, each with its answer.
5. The date of the delayed test.

Deliver the session. Afterwards, keep every learner's pre-test and post-test. Give the delayed
test on its date. Write three lines on what departed from the plan.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A district health officer asks whether the district's one-day refresher trainings for ASHA
workers should switch from lectures to sessions built around retrieval. One page, answer first,
no methods section.
