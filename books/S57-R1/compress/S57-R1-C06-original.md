# S57-R1-C06 · Learning styles: a popular claim that fails its own test

**Definition.** "Learning styles" is the idea that people differ in which mode of teaching or study works
best for them. Tests of learning style mostly ask people which kind of presentation they
prefer: words, pictures or speech, for example.

Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) keep two claims apart. The first is that people
will state preferences about how they like to take in new material. That is not in dispute.
The second, the learning-styles hypothesis, is that teaching fitted to a learner's style gives
a better learning outcome. Its commonest version is the meshing hypothesis: teaching works
best in the format that matches the learner's preference, pictures for a "visual learner",
say.

They set four conditions an experiment must meet before it supports the hypothesis. Learners
are divided into two or more groups by a measure of style. Within each group, they are
allocated at random to one of two or more teaching methods. Everyone takes the same test; if
the tests differ, no support is possible. And the method that gives one group its best test
score is not the method that gives another group its best. They call that last pattern a
crossover interaction, drawn with the style groups along the bottom axis of a graph.

With two styles and two methods, the mean scores make a 2 × 2 table, read "two by two": two
rows and two columns. Each row is a style group and each column a method. Within one row, the
two cells are two groups allocated at random, so the gap between them is a mean difference,
MD, as in `S57-R1-C02`. Take the MD the same way round in both rows, say the first method
minus the second. A crossover is present when one row's MD is positive and the other's is
negative. The best column then differs from row to row.

A crossover can be present even when one style group scores higher than the other on both
methods. It can also be present when both groups do equally well on one of the methods.
Pashler's criterion does not ask that each group's best method be its matched one. Meshing
asks that as well. So a crossover the wrong way round, each group best with the method that
does not match its label, supports learning styles in the broad sense and contradicts
meshing.

When both MDs have the same sign, the same method is best for every learner, and the result
is no evidence for learning styles, however different the two gaps are in size. Read down
the columns instead of across the rows, and such a result can look like a crossover. Pashler
and colleagues warn of exactly this: which variable goes along the bottom axis can decide
whether a graph appears to cross.

Searching a literature of several thousand articles, they found one study that could even
potentially meet the four conditions, and judged its evidence tenuous. Studies with the right
design found no support, one of them with 123 internal medicine residents. They conclude
that applying learning styles in classrooms is unwarranted. They add that the negative
results are not a conclusive refutation, and that many versions have never been tested.

Belief in the idea is common. In a survey of 114 academics in UK universities, Newton and
Miah (2017) found that 58% agreed that individuals learn better when taught in their preferred
learning style. They cite a 2012 study in which 93% of 137 UK schoolteachers agreed with the
same statement.

What would overturn this: a replicated experiment meeting the four conditions, in which the
best method changes with a style group measured by a named instrument.

**In plain terms.** The idea is attractive. Some people learn best from pictures, some from words, some by doing.
Find out which kind each learner is, and teach each one their way.

Two separate claims hide in that. The first is that people have preferences. They do: ask,
and they will tell you. The second is that teaching to the preference makes them learn more.
That is the one that matters to a teacher, and it can be tested.

The test is easy to describe. Take learners of two styles. Split each group in two by
lottery, and teach one half one way and the other half the other way. Test everyone the same
way. If matching matters, visual learners should do best with pictures and verbal learners
should do best with words. The best method has to change from one group to the other. That
change is the crossover.

Notice what does not count. Pictures working better for everyone does not count, even if they
help visual learners more. Visual learners scoring higher than verbal learners does not count
either.

A review in 2008 looked for the crossover in a very large literature. It found one doubtful
study. The studies that ran the test properly, including one with medical residents, found
no support. Yet in one survey of British university teachers, 58% still agreed with the idea.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Learners have learning styles, so teaching to them helps" joins two claims. That people state preferences is well shown. That matching teaching to them improves learning has virtually no support from the experiment that could show it. Accept the first; refuse the second.
- To test any claim that matching helps, ask for all four cells. Two style groups, each split at random between two methods, and the same test for everyone. Take the MD within each group, the same way round. Only opposite signs make a crossover.
- Comparing down a column, visual learners against verbal learners on the same lesson, is not the test. Those are different people, and a graph drawn that way can seem to cross when one lesson is best for all. Compare the two lessons within one group.
- A lesson that is better for one group by a lot and for the other by a little is still better for both. Teach everyone with it. A bigger gap in one group is no reason to sort learners.
- Rejecting learning styles is not a vote for teaching everyone one way. The best method varies with the content, and a learner's prior knowledge changes what suits them. Choose methods for the material and the level. Some methods, such as retrieval practice (`S57-R1-C03`), appear to help every kind of learner.
- When a colleague says medical learners differ in style, you can name the test run on them. Cook and colleagues, reported by Pashler, split 123 internal medicine residents by style and by method. They found no support for matching.
- Expect the idea among colleagues. In one survey, 58% of 114 UK academics agreed with it. Shown the missing evidence, 32% said they would keep using it (Newton and Miah 2017). Lead with the content argument: a murmur has to be heard and a rash has to be seen.
- Say "there is no adequate evidence for it", not "it has been proven false". Not every version has been tested. A crossover in one small table can also arise by chance: the MD check reads the pattern, not whether it is real. Even a real one must be large and reliable enough to pay for testing every learner and teaching each group apart.

**Exercise 1** (design). A colleague wants a learning-style questionnaire to decide how your department teaches
community medicine to MBBS students. Specify the study that could settle it for two styles,
visual and verbal, and two ways of teaching one topic. Say what result would support
matching, and what result would not.

**3.** Row P: X = 55, and X minus Y = -6. Row Q: Y = 66, and X minus Y = 4. Find the missing Y in

**4.** Made-up numbers, from no study. Sixty MBBS students take a learning-style questionnaire.
Within each style group, a lottery sends half to a lesson on reading a food label taught with

test.

Mean scores: visual group, diagram 80%, text 72%. Verbal group, diagram 55%, text 63%.
A colleague says: "The verbal group did worse on both lessons, so this tells us nothing about
matching." Is there a crossover, and is the colleague right?

**5.** Made-up numbers, from no study. The same design as before. Visual group: diagram 75%, text
60%. Verbal group: diagram 64%, text 62%. Is there a crossover, and what should the teacher

**6.** Made-up numbers, from no study. Forty ASHA workers answer a questionnaire that labels them

Visual group: picture cards 58%,

**7.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke. Made-up numbers: visual group, diagram
68%, text 57%; verbal group, diagram 62%, text 59%.

```working
on diagrams, visual 68 beats verbal 62
on text, verbal 59 beats visual 57
each group wins on its own method
so there is a crossover, and matching works
```

**8.** Here is a worked answer. Find the step that broke. Made-up numbers.

```working
verbal learners taught with text: mean 69%
both matched groups scored well above the 50% pass mark
so matching teaching to style works
```
