# S57-R1-C08 · Working memory is small: cognitive load, and effort that helps

**Definition.** New information is handled first in working memory, which can hold and work on only a
limited number of new elements at a time, and only for a short time. What has been learned is
kept in long-term memory, which has no known limit. When information comes back from
long-term memory, the limits of working memory effectively disappear. So the same material is
many interacting elements for a novice and a single element for an expert.

Cognitive load is the demand a learning task makes on working memory. Cognitive load theory,
set out by Sweller and colleagues, separates two sources of it. Intrinsic load comes from the
complexity of the material, judged against what this learner already knows; it changes only
if the material or the learner's knowledge changes. Extraneous load comes from how the
material is presented and what the learner is made to do; the teacher can change it. If the
load becomes too high, learning and transfer to new problems suffer.

Several teaching effects follow from this and have been tested, mostly in randomised
controlled trials. For novices, studying a worked example, a problem shown with its full
solution, can teach more than solving the same problem. A diagram alone has taught more than
the same diagram with text that repeats it. Spoken information is gone in seconds, so the
learner must hold it in working memory, which adds load and reduces learning.

These effects shrink, then vanish, and can reverse as learners gain expertise. Worked examples
should be faded out, through partly worked problems, and replaced by whole problems. Once a
learner can handle the material, effort that makes them work on it helps. On simple material,
students who had to generate an answer learned more than students shown it. Varied problems
raise the load and allow more to be learned, provided working memory has room. Interleaving,
mixing different problem types in one session instead of doing one type in a block, gave
college students better test results a week later.

Two limits. Many of these experiments did not measure cognitive load directly; the theory was
judged by whether the teaching methods it predicted worked. And working memory is not fixed:
it is depleted by effort and recovers with rest. The passages of the review this section
quotes say which way each effect runs, not how big it is.

**In plain terms.** Your working memory is the part of your mind that holds what you are thinking about right now.
It is small. It can juggle only a few new things at once, and it drops them within seconds.

Your long-term memory is where learned things are kept, and it has no known limit. Anything
already learned comes back from it as one piece. For you, "percentage" is one piece. A
learner meeting it for the first time has several pieces to hold. There is a part and a whole.
One is divided by the other, and the answer is multiplied by a hundred. All of them have to be
held in working memory together.

Overload working memory and less is learned. Some of the load comes from the material itself,
and you cannot remove it without changing what is taught. Some comes from how you teach it, and
that part is yours to cut. Talk is an example. What you say is gone in seconds, so the learner
has to hold it while they work. What you write on the board stays.

For a beginner, a problem shown worked out, step by step, often teaches more than being made to
solve it cold. As they learn, take the help away a step at a time.

Once the basics are held, effort helps. Mix different kinds of problems in one session. Make
learners produce answers rather than read them. The same effort that swamps a beginner is what
moves a more advanced learner on.

**Figure.** In one study, students answered "why" questions about facts on Canadian provinces and German states. Over reading alone, they gained 24% on the facts they knew well and 12% on the facts they knew less: twice as much where knowledge was greater.

**Must know points for you.**

- Saying more is not teaching more. Speech is gone within seconds, so a learner must hold it in working memory while they work, and that load reduces learning. Put the steps where they stay: on the board, a chart on the wall, a handout.
- For beginners, show a worked example before you set a problem. Then give a problem with part of the solution done, then a whole problem. Take the help away as they gain skill.
- What helps a first-year student can hold back a resident. Worked examples and extra explanation stop helping, and can start to harm, once learners know the material. For them, set problems.
- Before you put the same content on a slide twice, as a diagram and as text that repeats it, ask what the second copy adds. In one study of blood flow through the heart, the diagram alone taught more than the diagram with repeating text.
- Once the basics are held, make learners work: mix related problem types and have them produce answers rather than read them. Do not mix unrelated subjects in one sitting; that has not been shown to help.
- Cognitive load is seldom measured in the studies behind this theory, and no number for working-memory capacity is given here. Do not tell a colleague a session "overloaded" learners as though you measured it. Say what the learners could and could not do afterwards.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A colleague plans a one-hour session for 30 ASHA workers. The topic is reading a weighing
scale and recording a child's weight. For the first 40 minutes the colleague talks through
every step. Each slide shows a labelled picture of the scale. Beside it, a paragraph says the
same thing in words.

In the last 20 minutes each worker weighs a child alone and records the
weight. Nobody has seen a weighing worked through first. Name what in the plan adds load that
could be cut, and say what you would change.
