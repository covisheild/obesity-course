# S57-R1-C02 · Testing a teaching method: the comparison, the delayed test and the size of the difference

**Definition.** A fair test of a teaching method compares two groups of learners who differ in one thing
only: the method. Both groups learn the same content, in the same time, and sit the same
test after the same delay. That delay is the retention interval, as the last section
defined it. The group taught the usual way is the comparison group; OpenStax, a statistics
textbook, calls it the control group.

The learners are allocated to the two groups at random: a chance process decides who goes
where, not the teacher or the learners. Anything else that affects the score is what OpenStax
calls a lurking variable. Random allocation spreads the lurking variables across the groups
by chance. What is left of the difference, beyond what chance alone could produce, can then
be put down to the method.

The mean difference, MD, is the mean score of one group minus the mean score of the other.
It is in the test's own unit; for scores in per cent, that unit is percentage points. Its
sign says which group did better, so you always state which group was subtracted from which.
In this book, the MD is the new method's group minus the comparison group, unless it says
otherwise.

The standardised mean difference, SMD, is the MD expressed as a number of standard
deviations. The standard deviation, SD, is that of the individual learners' scores. In words,
the SMD is the mean difference divided by the standard deviation, written

```working
SMD = MD ÷ SD
```

The SMD has no unit, because the unit of the MD and the unit of the SD cancel. Two studies
whose MDs are the same share of their SDs have the same SMD, whatever tests they used. The
choice of SD is part of the definition. The SMD the Cochrane Handbook uses, Hedges' adjusted
g, divides by an SD pooled from both groups. Glass's delta divides by the comparison group's
SD alone. Papers often report an SMD as an "effect size", and some name the version, such as
Hedges' g or Cohen's d.

One group tested before and after its teaching, with no comparison group, gives a change,
not a difference between methods. It cannot separate the method from everything else that
happened between the two tests.

**In plain terms.** Here is the trap with judging your own teaching. You try a new method with a batch. Their marks
go up. It feels like proof.

But marks go up after almost any teaching. They go up more when the learners have seen the
questions before. A rise tells you something happened. It cannot tell you that your method did
it.

So you need two groups, and they must differ in one way only: the method. Same topic, same time,
same test, taken after the same gap. Then you decide who goes in which group by chance, for
example by drawing roll numbers from a box. Chance does not favour the keen students or the late
ones. So whatever else affects marks lands in both groups, roughly evenly.

Now compare the two groups' average marks. Subtract one mean from the other. That is the mean
difference. Say which way round you subtracted, and keep its unit: percentage points, for
scores in per cent.

A gap of 8 marks can be large or small. It depends on how spread out the marks are. If nearly
everyone scored within a few marks of the mean, 8 marks is a big gap. If the marks run from
near zero to near full, 8 marks is small.

So divide the gap by the standard deviation. The answer is the gap in standard deviations. That
is the standardised mean difference. It lets you compare studies that used different tests.

**Must know points for you.**

- A batch's rise from a pre-test to a post-test is not evidence that your method works. Any teaching raises it, and so does sitting the same questions twice. Call it the change in this batch, and run a comparison before you claim more.
- To compare two ways of teaching, let chance decide who gets which. Draw roll numbers, keep the topic, the time and the test the same, and give the test after a gap of a week or more. Then check the two groups' earlier marks, to see how evenly chance split them.
- Never quote an MD without saying which group was subtracted from which, and in what unit. A positive 6 and a negative 6 are opposite findings.
- An SMD of 0.5 does not mean "50% better". It means the groups' means sit half a standard deviation apart. To turn it into marks, multiply it by the SD of the scores on your own test.
- Freeman and colleagues pooled 158 comparisons of active learning against lecturing and found an SMD of 0.47, just under half an SD. Use that as your yardstick when someone offers you a new method's "effect size". It came from volunteer teachers in university science courses.
- The SMD shrinks when learners' scores are widely spread and grows when they are bunched, even if the gap in marks is the same. A batch picked for being alike will show a bigger SMD. When the two things you compare come from batches with different spreads, compare the MDs in marks as well.
- Random allocation makes the groups alike on average, not identical. With a small batch, chance can still leave one group stronger. If the groups' earlier marks differ by a lot, say so before you report any difference after teaching.
- When a committee is shown an "effect size" for a teaching method, ask three things. Compared with what? Who decided who got which? How long after the teaching was the test? An SMD from a test taken at the end of a class measures performance, not learning.

**5.** In Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment, the SSSS group read a passage in four study

**6.** Deslauriers and colleagues allocated physics students to two groups at random. Before the

SD. Say what the two SMDs tell you about the random allocation.

**7.** Roediger and Karpicke report d, an SMD, for two of their comparisons. After 5 minutes, SSSS

find the SD each d implies. Why is the second MD nearly twice the first, while d hardly moves?

**8.** Here is a worked answer. The numbers are made up. Find the step that broke.

```working
comparison group mean 58%, new method group mean 66%, SD 16 percentage points in each group
MD = 66 minus 58 = 8 percentage points
SMD = 8 divided by 58 = 0.14
so the new method's group scored 0.14 SD higher
```

**9.** Here is a worked answer. The numbers are made up. Find the step that broke.

```working
usual method group mean 64%, new method group mean 58%, SD 12 percentage points in each group
MD = 64 minus 58 = 6 percentage points
SMD = 6 divided by 12 = 0.5
so the new method is half an SD better
```

**10.** Here is a worked answer. The numbers are made up. Find the step that broke.

ASHA workers are accredited social health activists: village health workers.

```working
ASHA workers each plot ten children's weights on a growth chart; the score is the number of mistakes
question-based group: mean 2.1 mistakes, SD 1.4; usual talk group: mean 3.5 mistakes, SD 1.4
MD = 2.1 minus 3.5 = -1.4 mistakes

the SMD is negative, so the question-based session did worse, by one SD
```
