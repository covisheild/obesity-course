## Part 2. The three tasks, from the text alone

### Why how a session feels is not evidence that learners learned

Learning, as this book defines it, is what a person can still do on a test taken after a delay. What you see during and just after a session is performance, and what learners tell you is a feeling. Neither premise speaks about the later day, so "they enjoyed it and answered well, so they learned" does not carry.

Worse, these signals can point the wrong way. Rereading and smooth explanation produce fluency, and fluency feels like knowing. Effortful work, such as recalling instead of rereading, feels worse and teaches more.

In Roediger and Karpicke's Experiment 2:
- Students who read a passage four times beat read-once-test-three-times by 12 points at 5 minutes.
- The same students lost by 21 points a week later.
- They were also the most confident, 4.8 against 4.0 on a 1-to-7 scale.

Learners who nod have told you how they feel. If they cannot explain a step, the illusion of explanatory depth is showing. Book a short test on the same objective, with new questions, a week or more later. That test is your evidence.

### How I would test whether one method beats another

1. Pick one question and one outcome, a test on the objective, and fix the interval you care about. Use at least a week, and the real interval if you can.
2. Allocate learners to the two methods by a chance process, for example by drawing roll numbers from a box. A before-and-after rise in one batch is only a change.
3. Keep everything else the same: content, time, teacher, and one identical test with new questions, not the quiz items.
4. Check the groups' earlier marks, to see how evenly chance split them.
5. Report the MD, new minus comparison, with its unit and the direction of "better". Then report the SMD, MD ÷ SD, saying which SD. Set it against the 0.47 yardstick. Say what it does not establish: other settings, other intervals, and whether chance alone could have produced it.

*What the text did not give me:* how many learners are needed, and how to tell a real difference from chance. D6's standard error is never connected to this.

### Review schedule for an exam two months (60 days) away

From C04: one review, done as closed-book retrieval followed by checking against the answers, placed so that the gap from teaching to review is 20 to 30% of the interval from review to exam.

For material taught on day 0 with the exam on day 60, the review falls between day 10 and day 14 (g = 0.2 × 60 ÷ 1.2 = 10 and g = 0.3 × 60 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 13.8). If unsure, err to the later end, day 14.

Each topic taught later gets its own review, worked out from its own teaching date. A topic taught on day 20, 40 days from the exam, gets its review on day 27 to 29 (6.7 to 9.2 days after teaching).

Add frequent light, low-stakes quizzes with feedback ("test often and lightly"). Check with a delayed test that the schedule worked.

*Where I had to go beyond the text:*

- The 20 to 30% rule was stated for tests "a few weeks away"; 60 days is inside the tested range of 35 to 70 days.
- It does not cover topics taught close to the exam: a 7-day interval gave 14% in the data.

## Part 3. What it taught me that would change what I do, say, accept or refuse

**Do**
- Book a delayed test on the same objective, with new questions, a week or more later, before I call a session a success.
- End sessions with low-stakes retrieval: each learner answers alone, without notes, then sees the answer.
- Place reviews by the 20 to 30% rule, counted from the date the learning must be used. Make reviews retrieval, not rereading.
- Show worked examples to novices and fade the help as they gain skill. Set problems for advanced learners.
- Cut text that repeats a diagram. Put steps somewhere they stay.
- Write out my own explanation before teaching a topic, and check it where I stall.

**Say**
- "The quiz shows performance and the form shows feeling. Neither shows learning."
- "An SMD of 0.5 is half a standard deviation, not 50%."
- "There is no adequate evidence for learning styles", rather than "proven false".
- "MD, new minus comparison, in percentage points."

**Accept**
- That learners state preferences.
- That effortful study feels worse and can teach more.
- That low utility is not zero effect.

**Refuse**
- Pre-to-post rises as proof that a method works.
- Feedback ratings as a measure of teaching.
- "Effect size" claims without answers to: compared with what, who allocated, and how long after.
- Column-wise comparisons as evidence of matching.
- "64% better recall" turned into "64% higher marks".
- Fixed-share spacing rules beyond a year.
- Claims that "why" questions give lasting learning without a delayed test.
- A nod as evidence of understanding.

### (d) Leading, managing, and being the most competent member, in the book's words

- **Managing.** "Management copes with complexity." It is *planning and budgeting* ("setting targets, laying down detailed steps to reach them, and allocating resources"), *organizing and staffing* ("creating a structure of jobs, filling the jobs with qualified people, communicating the plan … delegating responsibility … setting up systems to monitor it"), and *controlling and problem solving* ("monitoring results against the plan, identifying where they depart from it, and planning and organizing to fix that").
- **Leading.** "Leadership copes with change." To lead is "to get a group to move towards a change and to keep them at the work it takes": *setting a direction* ("a vision of the future … with strategies"), *aligning people* ("communicating that direction to everyone who can help carry it out or can block it"), and *motivating and inspiring* ("keeping people moving in that direction, despite obstacles, by appealing to their needs, values and emotions"). The most knowledgeable resident is made chief and "everyone assumes the skill carries over. Often it does not." Expertise is one source of *power*; it is not *authority*, which "comes with a post".

## Part 3. What this taught me that would change what I do, say, accept or refuse

1. Never write *understand*, *know* or *appreciate* in an objective. Use one watchable action, plus a condition, a standard and a by-when. Test the objective by asking a colleague to write the question that checks it.
2. Refuse "diagnose and manage" in one objective: split it into two.
3. Write the test straight after the objective, before planning the teaching.
4. Refuse a written quiz as evidence of a hands-on skill. SH-level skills must be observed.
5. Refuse "use the same test before and after". Use parallel forms and split the group in half between the two orders.
6. Do not judge one learner from one pair of forms, or from a short test. One item is a big step.
7. Keep practice quizzes out of CBME internal-assessment marks, or they stop being low-stakes.
8. Replace "any questions?" with a question that every learner answers alone, in writing, before anyone speaks, followed by the answer.
9. Do not count a pre-test as retrieval practice.
10. Do not tell colleagues that spreading questions through a session is proven better than asking them at the end.
11. Book the delayed test before the session and write its date in the plan.
12. Report the normalised gain beside the raw gain. Say whether it is the class g or the mean of individual g. Compare only the class g with Hake, and only as a rough scale.
13. Report the four C12 numbers plus the item table. Cut time spent on items already known, and investigate items that did not move.
14. Call a pre/post rise "the change in this batch", not proof that the method works.
15. Read any "participants improved" claim for its Kirkpatrick level, 2A or 2B. Refuse feedback forms as evidence of learning, but do not dismiss Level 1 either.
16. Ask any programme report which level its good news came from, and what it compared against.
17. Say that the IMG has seven roles, not five. Check a department's large-group hours against the one-third cap.
18. Do not tell an Assistant Professor that the Basic Course is required to teach. Ask for the date of any "2023 guidelines" document. When a student's case turns on a limit, name both papers (nine years in GMER 2023, ten years in the CBME Curriculum 2024).
19. Expect to learn a second skill when put in charge because I know the most. Refuse "make the best doctor head and it will run itself".
20. Do not say that leadership training is shown to change practice.
21. Ask "what happens to it the day I leave?" Write the rules and name a successor for anything meant to last.
22. When people do not do what I ask, work out whether I lack authority or power. Meet the people I have no authority over first.
23. Delegate to the person and the task, not to the rank.
24. Write down who decides, who does the work and who is consulted before work starts.
25. Keep work conflict separate from personal conflict.
