# S57-R1-C04 · Spacing: the same practice, spread out, and how far apart

**Definition.** Spacing, which Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) call distributed practice, is a schedule: the
same material is studied or retrieved on two or more occasions spread out in time. Massed
practice, often called cramming, puts the same occasions back to back.

The gap is the time between two study sessions of the same material. The retention interval,
from `S57-R1-C03`, is the time from the last study session to the test.

The spacing effect is the finding that, for the same total study time, spaced practice gives
better long-term retention than massed practice. Dunlosky and colleagues rated distributed
practice as having high utility. They report a review by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) of 254
studies: students recalled 47% after spaced study and 37% after massed study. On an
immediate test, cramming can do as well or better.

Cepeda and colleagues (2008) taught 1,354 people 32 trivia facts. After a gap of 0 to 105
days the learners went through the facts once more, answering each question and then seeing
the answer. After a retention interval of 7 to 350 days they took a final test. Final recall
first rose as the gap grew, then fell. Of the gaps tested, the best were 1, 11, 21 and 21
days for retention intervals of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days.

So the best gap grew with the retention interval, but its share of the retention interval
fell. It was about 20% to 30% for a test a few weeks away. For a test a year away it was
about 5% to 7%.
At the best gap, final recall was 64% higher than with no gap. A gap longer than the best cost
less than a gap shorter than the best.

What would overturn this for your setting: comparisons in Indian classrooms, with equal study
time and a delayed test, that find spaced practice no better than massed practice.

**In plain terms.** Take the same three hours of study. Spend them in one evening, or spread them over three weeks.
The spread-out hours leave more behind a month later. That is the spacing effect, and it is one
of the best-tested findings about learning.

It does not feel that way. Straight after a cramming session, you remember a lot, and on a test
that day cramming can win. `S57-R1-C01` warned you that how learning feels and what lasts can
point opposite ways. This is the same warning again, now about the calendar.

How far apart should the sessions be? It depends on when the learning has to be used. Decide
that first.

Say the test is a few weeks away. Then make the gap before the review about 20% to
30% as long as the wait from the review to the test. For a test a year away, the share is smaller,
about 5% to 7%. If you are unsure, err on the long side. A gap a little too long costs less
than one too short.

Make the review a retrieval, not a reread (`S57-R1-C03`): close the book, write what you
remember, then check.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Three hours is three hours, however you spread them" is the error. With study time held equal, spaced practice leaves more behind on a delayed test. In Cepeda and colleagues' study, recall after the best gap was 64% higher than after no gap.
- Never judge a schedule by how the practice sessions go. In one classic experiment, the widest-spaced sessions showed the most forgetting in between and the weakest practice scores. They won on a test a month later. In one study, students rated their learning higher after massed study, even after they had seen spacing work.
- Before you pick a review date, write down when the learning must be used: the test date, or the day you will teach it. Then set the gap from that. For a test a few weeks away, the gap is about 20% to 30% of the time from the review to the test.
- The best gap grows as the test moves away, but its share falls: 23 days, 7%, for a test 350 days away. A fixed share, such as "10 to 20% of the retention interval", overstates the gap for long intervals. Dunlosky and colleagues give that fixed share, and Cepeda's own data bend away from it.
- When you are unsure of the best gap, choose the longer one. In Cepeda and colleagues' data, a gap longer than the best cost much less than a gap shorter than the best.
- Test often and lightly, so that students space their own study. In one study, when tests came only every 3 weeks, students left their study until just before each test. With daily tests, their study was spread out.
- These gaps were measured for trivia facts, one review and tests up to 350 days away. Dunlosky and colleagues note that few studies cover large bodies of connected material. Past a year, treat any gap figure as a starting guess. Do the same for a whole subject or a clinical skill. Then check it with a delayed test of your own (`S57-R1-C02`).
