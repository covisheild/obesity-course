# F5 · How government works, in the shape you need to read a regulation

**Definition.** In India the Constitution divides the power to make law from the power to apply it.

Parliament and the State Legislatures enact statutes, each called an Act. The Constitution is
the founding law that every statute must fit.

The executive carries statutes out. The courts read statutes and decide whether the executive
has acted within them.

The Central Government may make rules. A body that the statute creates may make regulations.

**In plain terms.** Three different jobs, done by three different sets of people.

A legislature makes law — Parliament for the country, a State Legislature for its own state.

Parliament also amends the Constitution, but only by a special majority in each House. That means a
majority of all its members, and two-thirds of those present and voting. Changes to some parts,
among them the Seventh Schedule's lists, must also be ratified by at least half the State
Legislatures. That is why the Constitution is the hardest law to change.

Below the legislature sits the executive — the day-to-day running of government. It is held by
the President under Article 53. It is carried out beneath the President by ministries and
departments.

A statute often leaves its detail blank on purpose, then hands the power to fill that blank
onward. Neither a rule nor a regulation comes from a legislature. Yet both bind you, because a
statute said they could.

Above all this sit the courts, reading statutes and testing whether the executive stayed inside
its permission.

**Illustration.** Pick up a packet of biscuits from your kitchen. Turn it over. Read the nutrition panel on the
back.

Search for the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, on the India Code website and open it.

Now read section 23(1) of the Act. It says no one may sell a packaged food that is not labelled
"in the manner as may be specified by regulations". Stop there. The Act hands you a duty, not
the detail. The detail sits in a regulation, written by a body
the statute set up, not by Parliament.

Section 4 sets up the body that writes it, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India.
Section 92 gives that body the power to make regulations.

Now suppose you think the rule on this packet is wrong. Do not go to a court expecting a new
label. Write instead to the Authority that wrote the regulation, because that is where the power
to change it sits.

**Where this picture breaks.** A programme may rest on nothing but an executive decision and a budget line. Look for the
statute; if there is none, that is what it rests on. No section to open, no list of amendments
to check. That makes such a programme quick to start and just as quick to stop.

**Must know points for you.**

- A statute needs a legislature and a debate. A regulation under the Food Safety and Standards Act needs a draft published and the Central Government's approval. It also needs a notification in the Gazette (the government's official newspaper). It is then laid before Parliament.
- Four levels, hardest to easiest to change: the Constitution, a statute, a rule or a regulation (one level), and a notification. A notification here is an order issued under a power a statute gives, and published in the Gazette. Lawyers call any one of them an instrument.
- Before repeating a label rule to anyone, open the regulation itself. The Act tells you a label is compulsory; it does not tell you what goes on the label. For the energy figure, that is regulation 5(3)(b) of the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020. The section on energy units used it.
- Food is not one subject belonging to one government. Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule split subjects between Union, State and Concurrent lists, so both Parliament and the State Legislatures act in this space. Expect two authorities where you assumed one.
- When you want something changed, find the instrument first. Ask a minister to change a regulation and you get sympathy. Ask the body that wrote it, and you get a process with dates you can chase.
- Before relying on a programme, look for the statute behind it. If there is none, it rests on an executive decision and a budget line, and can be stopped as quickly as it was started.
- Asked who regulates food labelling in India, the weak answer is "the government". The strong answer names the body, the statute that created it, the section granting the power, and the kind of instrument it issues.
- This tells you who may act and through which instrument. It says nothing about whether the instrument is sensible, whether anyone enforces it, or whether it works.
