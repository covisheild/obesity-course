# A5 · Ratios, rates and proportions

**Definition.** A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division, and is written a to b. It has no
unit, because the units cancel.

A proportion is a part divided by the whole it came from. It runs from zero to one, and a
percentage is a proportion multiplied by one hundred.

A rate is a count divided by the quantity of something else it was counted over: time, or
people, or both at once.

Two rates may be compared only when their denominators are the same.

**In plain terms.** Twelve grams against twenty grams is a ratio of twelve to twenty, or three to five.

If three of every five children in a room had the meal, the proportion is three fifths, or 0.6.
A proportion can never be more than one.

Five kilograms a month is a rate. Fifteen deaths per thousand people per year is a rate.

The bottom of a rate is not a footnote. It is part of the number. Change it and you have a
different quantity, even when the digits look the same.

**Illustration.** Go back to section 3(1) of the National Food Security Act, 2013, which you opened two sections
ago. It gives a person in a priority household five kilograms of foodgrains per person per
month. This time read the sentence after it.

A proviso just below sets a different figure for households covered by the Antyodaya Anna
Yojana. It is thirty-five kilograms of foodgrains per household per month. That figure applies
to the extent the Central Government specifies for each State under that scheme.

Here is the sentence somebody writes.

> Antyodaya households get seven times as much as priority households.

Look at where that seven came from. Thirty-five divided by five. The arithmetic is perfect and
the claim is meaningless, because the five is per person and the thirty-five is per household.
Two different denominators went into one division.

Make them the same and the picture changes. Take a household of six people.

```working
    5 kilograms per person per month, times 6 people
    = 30 kilograms per household per month
```

```working
    35 against 30
    35 minus 30 = 5 kilograms more
    5 divided by 30 = 0.1666..., so about 17 per cent more
```

Seventeen per cent more, not seven times as much.

Now change the household and watch the answer move.

```working
    household of 7:   5 times 7 = 35, so the two are identical
    household of 3:   5 times 3 = 15, so 35 is more than twice as much
    household of 10:  5 times 10 = 50, so 35 is less
```

The comparison does not have one answer. It depends entirely on the denominator that the
original sentence dropped.

One more thing to take from this, because it comes up in every table you will read. Rates for
uncommon events are written per thousand or per hundred thousand people rather than per person.
The only reason is to avoid a string of zeros after the point. Fifteen per thousand and 0.015
per person are the same rate.

**Where this picture breaks.** Making the denominators match is arithmetic. Deciding which denominator is the right one is not,
and this section cannot help you with it.

So the conversion above is a tool for catching a false comparison. It is not a way of deciding
who is better off.

And a rate per thousand people says nothing about which thousand. One figure for a state can
hold districts that differ several times over. The rate will not show you that. It was never
built to.

**Must know points for you.**

- Two rates with different denominators cannot be compared, and dividing one by the other still produces a number.
- Say the denominator out loud before you use any rate: per what, and over how long. If either half is missing you do not have a quantity yet.
- Indian entitlements and scheme figures move between per person, per household and per beneficiary without warning. Read the whole provision, including the proviso, before you take a number out of it.
- When two schemes are stated on different denominators, find the point at which they are equal and quote that.
- Converting a per-household figure to a per-person one using an average household size assumes every household is average.
- Asked to say which of two programmes delivers more, do not answer with the larger number. Ask what each is divided by.
- Rates per thousand or per hundred thousand exist only to keep the zeros off the page. Convert freely, and check the denominator first whenever two tables disagree.
