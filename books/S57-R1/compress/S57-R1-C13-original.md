# S57-R1-C13 · Liking a session is not learning from it

**Definition.** To evaluate teaching is to measure some outcome of it. Kirkpatrick's four-level model sorts
those outcomes by what is measured. As Frich and colleagues (2015) set it out, the four
levels are reaction (Level 1), knowledge (Level 2), behavioural change (Level 3) and system
results (Level 4).

Reaction is how the learners feel about the teaching and how satisfied they are with it.
Knowledge is what they learned by the end of it. Behavioural change is what they now do
differently at work. System results are changes in the organisation or the service around
them, such as the quality of care.

Each level is a different response variable: the quantity an evaluation measures to see what
the teaching changed. A measurement at one level is not a measurement at another.

Frich and colleagues split Levels 2, 3 and 4 by how the outcome is measured. Subjective
means stated by the learner or someone near them, as an opinion or a rating (2A, 3A, 4A).
Objective means measured by a test, an observation or a record (2B, 3B, 4B).

A feedback form given at the end of a session measures Level 1. An item on it such as "I
learned a great deal" measures Level 2A. A test that asks for the objective's action,
scored against a key, measures Level 2B.

Reaction and learning can move in opposite directions. In a randomised comparison, students
rated passive lectures more highly and scored lower on a test of learning than after active
classes on the same content.

**In plain terms.** At the end of a session you hand out a feedback form. The forms come back warm. It is
tempting to write "the session went well" and stop there.

Ask what the form measured. It measured whether the learners liked the session. That is one
thing you can find out about teaching, and it is the easiest. There are three more. Did they
learn what you set out to teach? Do they now do their work differently? Did anything change
for the people they serve?

These four questions are called Kirkpatrick's levels. Each needs its own measure. A form
answers the first. A test answers the second. Watching people at work answers the third.
Records of the service answer the fourth.

The first answer does not stand in for the others. The first section of this book showed
learners rating a lecture higher and learning less from it. So when someone says a session
worked, ask which of the four they measured.

Watch for one disguise. A form that asks "Did you learn a lot?" looks like a question about
learning. It is still the learner's opinion. Only a test the learner sits tells you what they
can do.

**Figure.** Frich and colleagues' review of 45 studies of leadership programmes for physicians: how many studies reported an outcome at each level. One study can report several levels. 36 studies reported self-assessed knowledge; 7 tested it. 2 observed behaviour at work, and 6 reported system results.

**Must know points for you.**

- A warm set of feedback forms tells you the learners liked the session. It does not tell you they can do anything new. When you report a session, name the level you measured, and do not let a Level 1 result stand in for Level 2.
- An item that asks "Did you learn a lot?" or "Do you feel confident?" is still the learner's opinion, Level 2A. In Frich's review, 36 of 45 studies reported self-assessed knowledge and 7 tested it. When you read "participants' knowledge improved", find out which of the two it was.
- This book's gate asks that your learners can do something they could not do before, and that you measured it. That is Level 2B: a test you score, on the objective's action. Give it a week or more after the session, and plan it before you teach. A feedback form cannot pass the gate.
- Reaction and learning can point opposite ways. In Deslauriers's randomised comparison, students rated passive lectures more highly and scored lower on the test. So never switch methods, or judge a colleague's teaching, on ratings alone. Ask for a test result at the same time.
- "Every study reported positive outcomes" means little until you know the level. Frich found exactly that across 45 studies, resting mostly on satisfaction and self-assessment, with 6 reaching system results. Ask any programme report which level its good news came from.
- The levels say what an evaluation measured, not what caused the result. A Level 3 or 4 change after a course, in one group with nothing to compare it with, is still only a change. Before you credit the course, ask for the design as well as the level.
- Level 1 is not worthless. It tells you whether learners could hear, kept up, and would come back, and a session nobody attends teaches nothing. Use the form for those questions and a test for learning. Do not rewrite a session on the form alone.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A department's report on its teaching sessions for second-year MBBS students reads: "Feedback was
collected from all students. The mean rating for 'I have learned a great deal' was high,
showing the sessions achieved their learning objectives."

Sort what was measured by level. Say what the conclusion needs that the report does not
have, and what you would add next year.
