# S57-R1-C03 · Retrieval practice: pulling it out beats putting it in again

**Definition.** Retrieval practice is an attempt by a learner, during learning, to bring material they have
already met back to mind from memory: to recall it or to recognise it, without looking at it.
Restudy is the alternative it is compared with: reading or hearing the same material again,
for the same time.

The testing effect is the finding that a retrieval attempt improves memory on a later test
more than restudy does. It holds on a delayed test. On a test a few minutes later, restudy
can come out ahead.

Roediger and Karpicke's (2006) first experiment, which `S57-R1-C01` described, shows both
halves. Each student reread one passage and tried to recall the other, with no feedback. On
a test 5 minutes later, the reread passage was ahead, 81% to 75% recalled. A week later the
passage they had tried to recall was ahead, 56% to 42%. The paper gives that week's
difference as d = 0.83, a standardised mean difference (`S57-R1-C02`).

In classrooms, Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt (2021) screened nearly 2,000 abstracts and kept 50
experiments in which each student retrieved alone, closed-book, during class time. Of the 49
effect sizes they could compute, each a Cohen's d, 28 were above 0.50. 46 favoured retrieval
practice and 3 favoured the comparison. Ten of the 50 experiments were in medical schools. None was in
India.

Feedback is showing the learner the correct answer after the attempt. Practice testing with
feedback beats restudy more reliably than practice testing without it. A low-stakes test is
one whose score counts for little or nothing.

Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing as having high utility, the top of
their three ratings.

What would overturn this for your setting: well-run comparisons in Indian classrooms, with a
delayed test on new questions, that find no gain.

**In plain terms.** Most of us were taught that a test measures learning. It also causes it. Pulling something out
of memory makes it easier to pull out again later. Reading it again does much less, even
though it feels like more.

The catch is timing. Straight after a session, what was reread looks better. Days later,
what they tried to remember looks better. `S57-R1-C01` warned you that how a
session feels and what lasts can point opposite ways. This is the same warning turned into a
method.

Three things make the method work in a classroom. Every learner answers, alone, without notes.
The score counts for little or nothing, so the test is practice and not judgement. And after
they have tried, they see the right answer.

The classroom evidence is large and mostly from the United States and Europe. Ten of
the experiments were in medical schools. None was in India. It is a good bet that it works in
your college too, but it is a bet you can check.

**Must know points for you.**

- "A test is for finding out what they know; the teaching happens before it" is the error. The attempt to recall is itself a learning event. In Roediger and Karpicke's first experiment, a passage students had tried to recall was 56% remembered a week later. A passage they had read again was 42% remembered.
- Run retrieval so that every learner answers alone and without notes, the score counts for little or nothing, and the correct answer follows the attempt. Drop any one of the three and you are running something the classroom evidence did not test.
- Keep quizzes that are for learning low-stakes. Weinstein and colleagues report a study of quizzes under high and low pressure. Both groups did as well on the quiz itself. On a later test, the high-pressure group did worse.
- In classrooms, 28 of 49 effect sizes were above 0.50 and 46 of 49 favoured retrieval practice (Agarwal and colleagues, 2021). When someone says retrieval practice is unproven in real classrooms, that is the answer. When someone quotes one large effect as what to expect, point to the 18 below 0.50.
- None of the 50 classroom experiments in that review was run in India. Three were run outside the United States and Europe. The effect is likely to hold in your college, but nobody has shown it there in this evidence. Measure it in your own batch, with a delayed test.
- Retrieval with no feedback and little success is where the method stops paying. Classroom studies without feedback showed mostly small or very small effects. When most of the room cannot answer, give the answer and reteach; do not count the silence as practice.
- If your final test repeats the quiz questions word for word, a gain may be memory of those questions. In the classroom review, effects were generally smaller when final-test questions were rephrased. To find out whether they learned the idea, test it with new questions.

**Exercise 1** (design). Take one hour you will teach in the next month: a batch of MBBS students, a group of
residents, or a meeting of ASHA workers. Plan its last ten minutes.

Write down four things. What will each learner try to recall? How will you make sure each one answers alone? How
will you keep the stakes low? When will they see the right answer? Then write down when they
will recall it a second time, and on what day you will test whether it lasted.

**Exercise 2** (teaching). A journalist rings you for three minutes. She has read that "tests help students learn" and
wants to know whether that is true, and whether it has been shown in India. Say what you
would tell her, including one quotable sentence.
