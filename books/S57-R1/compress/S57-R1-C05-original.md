# S57-R1-C05 · What does not work: rereading and highlighting

**Definition.** Rereading is studying a text again after a first reading: the restudy of `S57-R1-C03`, done
with a text. Highlighting is marking the parts of a text that seem important while reading
it. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) treat underlining as the same technique.

Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten study techniques and gave each an overall utility
rating: low, moderate or high. The rating asks two things. How widely does the benefit hold,
across learners, kinds of material, kinds of final test and real classrooms? And how does
the technique compare with the others? They rated both rereading and highlighting low
utility. Practice testing, the retrieval practice of `S57-R1-C03`, and distributed practice,
the spacing of `S57-R1-C04`, were rated high.

Highlighting, in most situations studied and with most learners, did little to boost
performance. It may help when learners know enough to pick out what matters, or when the
text is hard. In one study, students who underlined did worse on questions that needed
inferences; the reviewers say that result needs repeating.

Rereading does improve recall over a single reading. Most of that gain comes from the second
reading, and spaced rereading holds up at modest delays. But in direct comparisons, rereading
has consistently done worse than practice testing and than two techniques of explaining.
The reviewers name that relative disadvantage as the largest strike against it. No
experiment has tested rereading's effect on learning in a real course.

So low utility is not zero effect. It means the same time spent another way does more.
Dunlosky and colleagues recommend practice testing in place of rereading.

Fluency is the ease with which a text reads or a fact comes to mind. A text read a second
time reads more easily and feels familiar. Pashler and colleagues (2008) warn that this ease
and familiarity can come from things unrelated to understanding. `S57-R1-C01` met the same
trap in the smooth lecture.

What would overturn this for your setting: well-run comparisons, with equal time and a
delayed test, in which rereading or highlighting does as well as practice testing.

**In plain terms.** Ask a batch how they revise, and you will probably hear two things. They read their notes again. They
mark the important lines with a highlighter. Both feel like work, and both feel like they are
working.

A large review of the evidence rated both low. Not useless: a second reading does help, and
highlighting sometimes helps a reader who already knows what matters. Low, because the same
hour spent another way does more.

Why do they feel so good? Read a page a second time and it goes down easily. The words look
familiar. That ease is fluency, and it feels exactly like knowing. But it only tells you the
page is familiar. It does not tell you that you could say it back with the book shut.

The swap is the one `S57-R1-C03` taught. Read once. Then close the book, write down what you
remember, and check it against the page. It feels harder, and it is the part that lasts.

**Must know points for you.**

- "I read it three times and it all looks familiar now" describes fluency, not learning. A page read again reads more easily, and that ease can come from things unrelated to understanding. When a learner says this, ask them to shut the book and say it back.
- Replace the second reading with a test. Dunlosky and colleagues' own advice is practice testing in place of rereading. Teach your batch the order: read once, close the notes, write what you remember, then check it against the notes.
- Do not tell learners that rereading does not work. A second reading does improve recall over one reading, and spaced rereading holds up at modest delays. It is rated low because practice testing does more with the same time. Say that instead.
- A heavily highlighted book is a sign of time spent, not of learning. In most studies, highlighting as students do it added little to reading alone. Many students mark almost everything, and then no line stands out. Check what a learner can recall with the book shut.
- Expect rereading to be your learners' default. At one highly selective university, 84% of students listed it among their study methods. Over half, 55%, named it the one they used most (Karpicke and colleagues, reported by Dunlosky). Ask a batch how they revise before you teach them how.
- These ratings come mostly from laboratory studies of undergraduates. No experiment has tested rereading's effect on learning in a real course, and your own batch was in none of them. A batch may seem to do well on rereading. Compare it with practice testing on a delayed test before anyone concludes anything (`S57-R1-C02`).
