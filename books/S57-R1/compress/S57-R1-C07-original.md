# S57-R1-C07 · Explaining is how you find out whether you understand

**Definition.** People feel they understand how things work in far more detail than they do. Rozenblit
and Keil named this the illusion of explanatory depth in 2002. The illusion is much stronger
for knowing how something works than for knowing facts, procedures or stories.

It is measured in three steps. A person rates how well they understand something. They
write out a detailed, step-by-step explanation of how it works. Then they rate their
understanding again.

In Rozenblit and Keil's first study, 16 graduate students rated 48 things taken from books
on how things work. They used a scale from 1, the lowest understanding, to 7, the highest.
Each then explained four devices, such as a zipper or a flush toilet, and rated again. The
ratings fell after explaining. Many of the students were surprised at how much less they
knew than they had thought.

The drop has held up. In a second study, 33 undergraduates showed the same pattern. When
students were warned at the start that they would have to write explanations, the drop was
smaller, but it did not go away. It was large for devices and for natural processes, small for facts such as
capital cities, and absent for procedures and for the plots of films. The paper plots the
mean ratings in its figures and does not print them, so no size of the drop is given here.

Expertise does not reliably remove the illusion either. A later study from the same
laboratory found that people with more education show it on topics from their own subject.

Two study techniques put explaining to work. In self-explanation, a learner explains how new
information relates to what they already know, or explains the steps they take while solving
a problem. In elaborative interrogation, a learner works out why a stated fact is true. A
2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated both of moderate utility, below the high
utility of practice testing.

Self-explanation has helped across different materials, a wide range of ages, and tests of
memory, understanding and transfer to new problems. Elaborative interrogation is firmly
shown only on tests of memory taken soon after learning. Almost all the studies of both
tested learners within minutes, and few were run outside the laboratory. Where time was
reported, self-explanation took 30% to 100% longer than the comparison.

An explanation written from memory, with the notes put away, is also retrieval practice
(`S57-R1-C03`).

**In plain terms.** Ask yourself whether you understand how a ceiling fan works, and you will probably say yes.
Now write down, step by step, what happens between pressing the switch and the blades turning.
Then ask yourself the first question again. Your answer is likely to come down.

That drop is the illusion of explanatory depth. You felt you understood. Trying to explain
showed you where you do not. The first section of this book (`S57-R1-C01`) showed that a
learner's feeling of learning can point the wrong way. The feeling of understanding is the
same kind of rating, and it fails in the same way.

It happens to the people you teach, and it happens to you. Knowing a subject well does not
protect you. Being told that you will have to explain does not fully protect you either.

The illusion lives in "how does it work" and "why". Ask people to list the steps of a
procedure, like the steps for weighing a child, and they judge what they know about right.
Ask them why each step is there, and you are asking how something works. That is where to
look for the gap.

So use explaining as a check. When a student nods, ask them to explain the step. Before you
teach a topic, write out your explanation from memory. The places where you get stuck are what
you read up before you face the class.

Explaining also helps people learn, not only check. Asking "why is this true?" of a fact, or
explaining each step of a worked problem, has helped learners in many studies. The evidence is
moderate, not strong: most of those studies tested people minutes later, not weeks later.

**Figure.** One introductory biology class reading a textbook passage on human digestion. Students who met a "Why is this true?" prompt about every 150 words got 76% of 105 true-or-false questions right. Students who read without prompts got 69%. The test was not reported as delayed.

*What the figure shows:* Two bars: with "Why is this true?" prompts, 76% correct; reading only, 69% correct.

**Must know points for you.**

- A learner who nods, or says "yes, understood", has told you how they feel, not what they can do. Ask them to explain the step in their own words, aloud or on paper. The gap shows at the step they cannot give.
- Knowing your subject well does not protect you from the illusion, and neither does knowing that you will be asked. Before you teach a topic, write your explanation out from memory, and read up on the places where you stall.
- When you ask learners to explain, have them check the explanation against the text or against you. A fluent explanation that is wrong can hurt learning. Learners told to explain often retell the text instead, which is not explaining.
- Asking someone to list the steps of a procedure will not show you the illusion, because people judge their knowledge of steps about right. To find the gap, ask why each step is there or how the thing works.
- Do not claim that "why" questions or self-explanation produce lasting learning. Most studies of them tested learners within minutes, and few ran in real classrooms. If you use them, test your learners days later before you say they worked.
- Explaining costs time: 30% to 100% more in the studies that reported it. In one study a group that solved twice as many problems without explaining scored the same as a group that explained. Weigh fewer problems explained against more problems solved.

**Exercise 1** (teaching). A first-year resident tells you: "I read the chapter on anthropometry twice last night. I
understood all of it, so I can take the MBBS batch on Monday." You have ten minutes and a
whiteboard. Explain why "I understood it" is not yet evidence. Then give them one thing to
do tonight instead of a third reading.
