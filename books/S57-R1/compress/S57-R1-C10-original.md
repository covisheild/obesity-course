# S57-R1-C10 · A test that matches the objective

**Definition.** An item is one question or task on a test, marked on its own. A test matches a learning
objective when every item asks the learner to carry out the objective's action, on its
content, under its condition, and is marked against its standard.

An item is at the objective's level when it cannot be answered by repeating something said or
shown in the session. For an objective above remember, that means the item hands the learner a
case they have not met before.

Constructive alignment is Biggs's name for designing three things as one: the objectives, the
teaching and learning activities, and the assessment. The objective names a performance. The
activities are chosen to make learners carry out that performance. The assessment asks for it.
Chatterjee and Corral call the same idea instructional alignment. Their example is
intubation, passing a breathing tube into a patient's windpipe. If the objective is to intubate
a normal adult successfully every time, testing it with a written exam misaligns the objective
and the test.

A test is used formatively when its result is used while the teaching is still going on, to
decide what to teach or practise next. It counts for little or nothing. A test is used
summatively when its result is recorded to judge or report what a learner has achieved, as in
an examination. The same item can be used either way. The use, not the item, makes the
difference.

Two forms of a test are parallel when each item on one form has a partner on the other. The
partner asks for the same action, on the same kind of case, at the same level, and is meant to
be as hard. Only the case or the numbers differ.

The pre-test is taken before the teaching and the post-test at its end. On parallel forms, a
rise from one to the other cannot come from remembering the pre-test's questions. If one form
is easier than the other, the difference is a systematic error in every learner's gain.
Giving half the learners each order of the two forms cancels it in the mean gain of the whole
group, when the two halves are the same size. It does not cancel it for any one learner.

**In plain terms.** A test can be well made and still test the wrong thing. Suppose your objective says students
will classify a new adult's BMI. A test that asks them to write the BMI formula checks something
else: whether they remember a line from your session.

So build the test from the objective. Take its action, its condition and its standard, and turn
each into an item. Then ask of every item: could a student answer this by repeating something I
said? If so, the item is testing memory of the session, not the objective.

Write the test before you plan the teaching. Then plan activities that make the learners do
what the test will ask. The objective, the teaching and the test become one design.

To see change, you test before and after. Use two forms with different cases, so the second
score is not a memory of the first test.

**Must know points for you.**

- A test of recall cannot show that learners reached an objective above remember. Adams puts it plainly: recall of facts is not evidence of the next level up. Before you use a test, ask of each item whether a learner could answer it by repeating something from your session. If yes, it is testing memory of the session.
- Write the test straight after the objective, before you plan the teaching. Then choose activities that make learners do what the test will ask. If a session's activities never have learners carry out the objective's action, the test will find that out, too late.
- "Use the same test before and after, so the comparison is fair" is the error. A repeated item can be answered from memory of the first attempt. Use two parallel forms: partner items that ask for the same action on different cases.
- Two forms are rarely exactly as hard as each other. Give half the group each order of the two forms, with the halves the same size. A difference in difficulty then cancels in the group's mean gain. It does not cancel for one learner, so never judge one learner from one pair of forms.
- A short test moves in big steps. On five items, each item is 20 percentage points. One lucky guess or one slip moves a learner a whole step. Before you decide anything about one learner, add items, or look at the group instead.
- India's MBBS course follows the Competency Based Medical Education (CBME) Curriculum 2024, from the National Medical Commission. Its tables give each skill a level: K (knows), KH (knows how), SH (shows how) or P (performs). Each row also suggests how to test it. A skill at SH cannot be tested on paper: the learner must show you. Read the level before you write the test.
- The CBME Curriculum 2024 builds internal assessment from day-to-day tests, and quizzes can count. A student needs at least 50% of those marks to sit the university exam in that subject. So a quiz whose marks count there is not low-stakes. Keep your practice questions out of it.
- A matched test shows what learners can do when they take it. It does not show that your teaching caused it. For that you need the comparison of `S57-R1-C02`. Do not report a well-matched post-test as proof that the method works.

**Exercise 1** (critique). A trainer writes this objective for a half-day session with accredited social health
activists (ASHAs), village health workers:

> At the end of this session, each ASHA, given a child and the scale used at her centre, will
> weigh the child and record the weight in the register, completing every step on the
> programme's checklist, observed by the trainer.

Her test is a written quiz of ten multiple-choice questions on the steps of weighing a child.
Say whether the test matches the objective. If not, describe a test that does, and say how you
would make a second form of it for the end of the session.

**Exercise 2** (design). Take the objective you wrote for your build session. Write a pre-test of at least five items
and a parallel post-test, each item with its partner. For each pair, say what makes the item
hard and how the partner keeps it. Then write down how you will split your learners between
the two orders of the forms.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). Most ASHAs scored well on the written quiz at the end of last year's training. A state health
secretary asks why that is not evidence that they now weigh children correctly. One page,
answer first, no methods section.
