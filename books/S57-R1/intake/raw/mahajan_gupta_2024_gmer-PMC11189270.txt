The National Medical Commission (NMC), established by an act of parliament, came into effect on September 25, 2020, following a gazette notification dated September 24, 2020. Consequently, the Board of Governors (BoG), replacing the Medical Council of India (MCI), was dissolved.[1] NMC comprises four autonomous boards, with the Undergraduate Medical Education Board (UGMEB) being one of them.[2] According to the NMC Act, UGMEB has two primary functions: to develop a competency-based dynamic curriculum at the undergraduate level and to establish the minimum requirements and standards for conducting courses and examinations for undergraduates in medical institutions. UGMEB is responsible for crafting the curriculum for the Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) program in India and formulating regulations for its implementation across medical institutes in the country.

Although the NMC was established in September 2020, the competency-based curriculum was introduced from the MBBS admission batch of 2019, with the curriculum being released in three volumes by the BoG in the supersession of the MCI.[3] This curriculum remains in effect to this day. The planning for its implementation has been ongoing for many years, and both the BoG and its predecessor, the MCI, along with the NMC, have released various modules to facilitate the smooth implementation of this curriculum [Table 1], which are accessible on the NMC website (https://www.nmc.org.in/information-desk/for-colleges/ug-curriculum/).

As evident from Table 1, all regulatory bodies in their time have designed and released modules to guide colleges in the smooth implementation of various aspects of the competency-based curriculum. To address the challenges posed by the COVID-19 crisis and to impart competencies for managing similar situations in future pandemics, a separate pandemic module was released. In addition, an online learning and assessment module was released for managing teaching during such pandemics [Table 1].

These extensive curricular reforms must be complemented by regulatory reforms. As mentioned earlier, it is the responsibility of the UGMEB to formulate such regulations necessary to uphold minimum standards for conducting the MBBS course, including the examination process.

Curriculum Regulatory Reforms

Despite being operational for <4 years at the time of writing this editorial, the NMC has already issued a significant number of regulations to fulfill its regulatory mandate. However, for this editorial, we will focus solely on the regulations governing graduate medical education.

Until the admission batch of 2018, graduate medical education in India was governed by the “Regulations on Graduate Medical Education, 1997” (amended up to May 2018), as released by the MCI.[4] To regulate the competency-based curriculum, the BoG released the “Regulations on Graduate Medical Education (Amendment), 2019,” which were incorporated as Part II of the “Regulations on Graduate Medical Education, 1997,”[5] which were in force at that time. Last year, the NMC released the “Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) Curriculum Guidelines,” which have been in effect since August 1, 2023.[6] Although other regulations, such as minimum standard requirements and teacher eligibility qualification regulations, have also been formulated and released at various times, only the aforementioned three regulations directly governed the implementation of the MBBS curriculum during different time periods. For the sake of clarity, the first two regulations will be referred to as Graduate Medical Education Regulations (GMER) in this editorial, namely GMER 1997 and GMER 2019, and the third as CBME Guidelines 2023.

Due to the prevailing COVID-19 conditions, the MBBS training needed to be streamlined. Consequently, during the period between the release of GMER 2019 and CBME Guidelines 2023, three detailed circulars/advisories were issued. The first circular, released as “News ID 193” on November 25, 2020, outlined the cutoff date for the reopening of medical colleges after the pandemic and the time distribution for the admission batch of 2020.[7] The second circular, released as “News ID 315” on March 31, 2022, provided guidelines for the implementation of the new CBME course curriculum for the MBBS batch of 2021.[8] The third circular, released as “News ID 420” on December 7, 2022, contained the academic calendar and curriculum for the MBBS batch of 2022.[9] These circulars/guidelines were specifically tailored to regulate the training of the MBBS admission batches of 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively. For simplicity, they will henceforth be referred to as CBME Circular 2020, CBME Circular 2021, and CBME Circular 2022, respectively. Subsequently, a corrigendum to the CBME Guidelines 2023, amending the passing criteria in the CBME curriculum, was released as “News ID 542” on September 1, 2023 (hereafter referred to as the corrigendum).[10] In addition, a Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) document was released as “News ID 558” on September 13, 2023, to facilitate stakeholders’ understanding of various aspects of the CBME Guidelines 2023.[11] This document will be further referred to as the FAQs document.

It is worth mentioning the document titled “Graduate Medical Education Regulations 2023,” released by the NMC on June 8, 2023 (notified in the Gazette of India on June 2, 2023).[12] Although this is a generally applicable document, it specifically elaborates on the maximum time period allowed to complete the MBBS course, which will be discussed further in this editorial. This document will henceforth be referred to as GMER 2023. We have deliberately omitted mention of the document named “Guidelines under Graduate Medical Education Regulations 2023,” released by the NMC on June 12, as these guidelines were subsequently withdrawn on June 23, 2023. However, as these guidelines are still available on the NMC website as “News ID 502,” new users must exercise caution while searching for guidelines in force, which are the CBME Guidelines 2023 (News ID 523).

Since GMER 1997 applied to pre-CBME batches, we will further analyze and compare GMER 2019 and CBME Guidelines 2023, referencing the CBME Circulars 2020, 2021 and 2022, the corrigendum, and the FAQs document where necessary. Many documents referenced in this editorial have been identified by their “News ID” numbers, which can be easily accessed from https://www.nmc.org.in/all-news/ using their respective “News ID.”

Roles of Indian Medical Graduate

The GMER 2019 marked the first recognition of the term “Indian Medical Graduate (IMG),” defining them as “a medical graduate possessing requisite knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and responsiveness to function appropriately and effectively as a physician of first contact of the community while being globally relevant.”[5] To fulfill the goals of the IMG training program, the IMG was expected to perform five roles – clinician, leader, communicator, lifelong learner, and professional [Figure 1].

Figure 1Roles of Indian Medical Graduates as per 2019 and 2023 regulations

The CBME Guidelines 2023 have expanded on the recognition and definition of the IMG, increasing the roles from five to seven.[6] These seven roles include clinician, leader, communicator, lifelong learner, professional, critical thinker, and researcher [Figure 1]. This addition of critical thinker and researcher roles is welcomed, as critical thinking skills are essential for patient care decision-making, and the role of IMG as a researcher will enhance research activities during undergraduate medical training. However, the required competencies for the IMG to fulfill these roles have not been documented in the CBME Guidelines 2023; hopefully, they will be notified soon.

Curriculum, Time Period, and Time Distribution

Regarding the time period and time distribution, both documents advocate for the implementation of the CBME curriculum in MBBS, which has been approved from the admission batch of 2019 onward. The total time period for MBBS, comprising 4½ years, followed by 1 year of mandatory internship, remains the same in both documents. However, in GMER 2019, these 4½ years were divided into nine semesters, whereas in CBME Guidelines 2023, they are divided into four professional years. This adjustment eliminates confusion, as referencing semesters in the document was creating ambiguity. Each academic year in GMER 2019 had a minimum of 240 teaching days, while according to CBME Guidelines 2023, each academic year will have at least 39 teaching weeks. With well-defined vacation weeks and examination periods, this distribution in weeks facilitates institutes in designing the academic calendar and yearly timetable more efficiently.

However, there is a significant change in the time distribution within each professional year in the two documents. Changes in the time distribution were implemented for admission batches of 2020, 2021, and 2022 through CBME Circulars 2020, 2021, and 2022, respectively.[789] Changes in time distributions were also made for phase 2 to the final phase part II training for the batch of 2019 through CBME Circular 2020.[7] Therefore, the actual delivered time distribution differed from the documented time distribution for the batch of 2019. It is noteworthy that the actual total course duration of MBBS for the admission batch of 2019 was extended by 1 month due to COVID-19, whereas it has been curtailed by 5 months and 3.5 months for admission batches of 2020 and 2021, respectively [Table 2]. In some documents, the time period for electives has not been shown separately. Consequently, the time period from the final phase part I (additionally from part II for the 2022 batch, as per the document) has been deducted to show the time period for electives.

Clinical Rotation

It is prudent to discuss the changes in clinical rotations reflected in various documents alongside changes in time distribution. Interestingly, adjustments to the time allocated for phase II, final phase part I, and part II for admission batches of 2019 and 2020 were made through CBME Guidelines 2020;[7] however, no circular or advisory mentioned any changes in clinical rotation weeks for these phases. Consequently, the clinical rotations as originally outlined in GMER 2019 were implemented for both batches, with adjustments made to the schedule for vacations and internal examinations. Through the CBME Circular 2021, several noteworthy changes were introduced in the academic calendar. It was the first time that the calendar mentioned that the final phase part I examinations would only cover the subjects of Forensic Medicine and Community Medicine, indirectly implying that examinations for otorhinolaryngology and ophthalmology would be held in the final phase part II. In addition, the National Exit Test was mentioned in the calendar for the first time. Another significant highlight of this circular was the introduction of the “Family Adoption Program [FAP]” in the MBBS curriculum.[8] Although many changes in the clinical roster were necessitated for this batch, unfortunately, CBME Circular 2021 did not provide any details on the clinical rotations. Despite verbal advisories from the NMC through various platforms that for batches before 2023, colleges should follow the circulars issued over time, confusion persists. This confusion is compounded by the cessation of the practice of approving institute timetables from nodal/regional centers when the batch of 2021 entered the clinical rotation phase. Under these circumstances, it is likely that a uniform schedule of clinical rotation is not being followed across the country, with each institute designing its own clinical schedule.

Supplementary Examinations and Course Duration

Regarding Supplementary Examinations and Course Duration, there are differences in the time frame within which supplementary examinations are to be conducted after regular examinations, ranging from “not later than 90 days” to “4–6 weeks for conduct and results within the next 10 days” to “by the end of 1 month with the next 15 days for declaration of results.”[589] The current CBME Regulations 2023 mandate conducting supplementary examinations and declaring results within 3–6 weeks from the date of the main (regular) examination. While this arrangement has been made for supplementary students to join the main batch and avoid supplementary batches, conducting supplementary examinations at such short notice for one Health Professions University across all medical colleges in a state presents a considerable challenge.

The consequences of failing in the main examination of the first phase, supplementary examination of the first phase, and regular examination of other phases remain the same across all regulations released from time to time. GMER 2019 includes two peculiar clauses that warrant mention: first, no more than four attempts are allowed for a candidate to pass the first professional examination, with the total period for successful completion of the first professional course not to exceed 4 years; second, a learner shall not be entitled to graduate later than 10 years from joining the first MBBS course. This clause has withstood the legal scrutiny in various courts of the country. Interestingly, both these clauses are absent in CBME Guidelines 2023. However, CBME Guidelines 2023 apply to all students currently undergoing training under the CBME curriculum from August 1, 2023, onward and have replaced all existing regulations/guidelines. To address this, NMC included questions 6, 7, and 8 in the FAQs document.[11]

As a reply to these questions, the following points have been mentioned:

The maximum total duration of MBBS course permitted to complete the MBBS course, is 10 years including the period of Compulsory Rotating Medical Internship

No student shall be allowed more than four attempts for 1st
year (first professional MBBS). In these four years, the maximum number of attempts permitted shall be four which include supplementary examination also

If a candidate fails to qualify/complete the course/internship in prescribed time limit, he shall not be allowed to continue the MBBS course under any circumstances.

It is worth noting that the opening disclaimer in the FAQs document states that “These FAQs are in the nature of explanation for ease of understanding of the general public and cannot be upheld in a court of law. Please refer to the relevant guidelines for legal validity.” However, if an institute/university discharges a candidate after four failed attempts in the first professional, how they will defend this action in court remains a question.

The GMER 2023, as previously mentioned,[12] contains interesting provisions regarding the maximum duration to complete the MBBS course. Chapter V, clause 21 states that under no circumstances shall a student be allowed more than four attempts for the 1st year (first professional MBBS), and no student shall be allowed to continue the undergraduate medical course after 9 years from the date of admission into the course. These regulations, gazette notified and never withdrawn, remain in force. This document clarifies the “discharge clause” missing from CBME Guidelines 2023. However, further clarification is needed on this clause, given the differing time periods for completing MBBS as per different documents – CBME Guidelines 2023 are silent on the issue, FAQs document mentions it as 10 years, and GMER 2023 mentions it as 9 years. All these documents are currently in force.

Question number 3 and its reply in the FAQs document warrant further discussion and are quoted here:

“Whether a student can appear in the supplementary examination if the attendance is inadequate at the time of the annual examination?”

Reply – “No, if the student's attendance is <75% for theory and <80% for practical/clinical training, the student cannot appear in supplementary examination following the regular annual examination. Such a student is required to take classes with the junior batch commencing in the next academic year to compensate for his attendance deficit, especially the course, he has missed. He will only be eligible to appear in the examination in the next academic year.”

This “detention clause” requires further consideration. First, why should not a student be given a chance to make up for attendance shortages during the period between regular and supplementary examinations? In any case, for the students who join the second phase after passing the supplementary examination of phase 1, extra classes to cover the syllabus will be arranged for them. Students detained due to academic record shortcomings in regular phase 1 examination can also attend extra classes with them after becoming eligible for and passing supplementary examinations. Second, this clause of refusal for students with attendance shortages to appear in the supplementary examination is not in CBME Guidelines 2023 and has been introduced in the FAQs document only, which is legally defunct as claimed in the opening disclaimer.

A well-drafted gazette notification on the above mentioned “discharge clause” and “detention clause” is urgently required to be released by NMC, to give these clauses legal sanctity.

Criteria for Passing a Subject

According to GMER 2019, the passing criteria were a minimum of 50% marks in university-conducted examinations, separately in theory and practical (which includes practical/clinical and viva voce). This entailed obtaining a minimum of 50% marks in theory, 50% in practical, and 50% in total (referred to as the 50%-50%-50% criteria) to pass a particular subject. For subjects with two theory papers, a minimum of 40% in each paper was required. These passing criteria remained unchanged until the implementation of CBME Guidelines 2023, effective from August 1, 2023. This means that students of batch 2019 passed phase 1, phase 2, and final phase part 1; batch 2020 passed phase 1 and 2; while batch 2021 passed phase 1, based on 50%-50%-50% criteria.

Initially, the CBME Guidelines 2023 caused confusion. It was stated on page 58 that “a candidate shall obtain 50% marks in university conducted examination separately in theory and in practical (practical includes: practical/clinical and viva voce) to be declared as passed in that subject.” It further clarified that “in subjects that have two papers, the learner must secure minimum 50% of marks in aggregate (both papers together) to pass in the said subject.” From these lines, an impression was made that the condition of minimum 40% marks in each paper, for subjects having two papers, has only been removed, while the minimum pass percentage in theory, practical and the total has been kept the same, i.e., 50%-50%-50% criteria as in GMER 2019. However, page 75 of CBME Guidelines 2023 reflects passing criteria as “mandatory to get 40% marks separately in theory and in practical; and totally 50% for theory plus practical.” This page depicts a 40%-40%-50% criteria.

To address this confusion, a corrigendum was issued on September 1, 2023,[10] specifically amending the passing criteria mentioned on page 58. The corrigendum stated that:

In subjects that have two papers, the learner must secure minimum 40% of marks in aggregate (both papers together) to pass in the said subject

A candidate shall obtain 50% marks in aggregate and 60:40 (minimum) or 40:60 (minimum) in University conducted examination separately in theory and in practical (practical includes practical/clinical and viva voce) in order to be declared as pass in that subject.

From the purpose of this corrigendum, one can deduce that the intention is to clarify that the passing criteria are a minimum of 40% in theory and practical components separately and a minimum of 50% in total, following the 40%-40%-50% criteria already stated on page 75 of CBME Guidelines 2023. However, the addition of the 60:40 or 40:60 wording has led to confusion for many. In most subjects, the theory paper accounts for 200 marks and the practical for 100 marks. Therefore, if one achieves 40% in theory (80 out of 200) and 60% in practical (60 out of 100), the total will not be 50% (it will be 140 out of 300). Similarly, if one scores 40% in practical (40 out of 100) and 55% in theory (110 out of 200), they will have a total of 50% (150 out of 300), but the 40:60 ratio is not fulfilled as stated in the corrigendum. Thus, the clear criteria outlined on page 75 of CBME Guidelines 2023 became obscured with confusion following the release of the corrigendum.

It was expected that the FAQ document would provide clarification.[11] The first paragraph of the reply to question number 10 of the FAQs document did explain the passing criteria clearly as a 40%-40%-50% for passing a subject. However, the second part of the reply, which is explained here with an example, is again confusing. The second part reads as follows: “If the maximum allotted marks are 100, the student must score at least 40 marks out of 100. When there are two papers of 100 marks each, the combined total of both papers shall be 40%, meaning that a student has to score a combined total of 80 marks in both papers out of 200 to pass the subject, provided they score at least 60% in the practical examination. Vice versa shall also be applicable.” However, as explained above, after obtaining 80 marks out of 200 in theory, one cannot pass a subject by achieving 60% in the practical examination. This fixation on 60% is perplexing. We hope that better judgment prevails, and that all universities understand that the intention is to adhere to the 40%-40%-50% scheme, i.e., a minimum of 40% in both theory and practical separately and a minimum of 50% in the total of theory and practical.

Appointment of Examiners

Regarding the appointment of examiners, GMER 2019 specified eligibility criteria for examiners, while CBME Guidelines 2023 introduced new requirements, rendering MSc-PhD faculty members ineligible. This clause has been challenged in various High Courts, with NMC admitting in court that MSc-PhD faculty who were previously appointed as examiners are eligible, but no clarification has been provided on the NMC website.

The GMER 2019 outlined requirements for the number and rotation of examiners, whereas CBME Guidelines 2023 reduced the burden on universities by specifying a minimum of four examiners for every student. In addition, the requirement for external examiners from different institutions was removed.

Other changes include the elimination of migration allowances, removal of grace marks, and adjustments to the duration of foundation courses and electives. The allocation of subjects to different phases has also been modified, with otorhinolaryngology and ophthalmology examinations shifted to the final phase, part II. Furthermore, separate orthopedics examiners are no longer required for assessing orthopedics clinical portions during surgery and allied examinations.

Conclusion

The CBME curriculum has been recognized as a dynamic framework, yet apart from the introduction of FAP and the pandemic module, there have been no significant changes to the overall or subject-specific curriculum. The MBBS curriculum has remained largely unchanged for the past five sessions. However, the regulations governing MBBS training have undergone constant modifications each session, rendering them more dynamic. As indicated in the CBME Guidelines of 2023, the academic calendar is titled “Proposed Academic Calendar for CBME 2023-2024 Batch.” It is hoped that this same calendar will be used for future batches, and that no more changes will be made. The issues surrounding the clinical roster for the 2021 batch and the passing criteria have largely been resolved. It is now up to the NMC to determine whether proper gazette notifications are necessary to provide adequate legal coverage for the “discharge clause” and “detention clause,” or if the FAQs document suffices.
