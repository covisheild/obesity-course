# S01-R1-C04 · What expenditure is made of, and why a bigger body spends more

**Definition.** Daily energy expenditure is made of three components, and they are not equal in size.

Resting energy expenditure (REE), from `S01-R1-C01`, is the energy spent at rest. It is
usually the largest of the three. The basal metabolic rate (BMR), from `S01-R1-C03`, is not a
fourth part. It is resting expenditure measured under set conditions: lying down, after a
fast, in a room neither warm nor cold.

The thermic effect of food is the energy spent digesting, absorbing and processing what was
eaten. Intake in this book is counted as metabolizable energy, not net metabolizable energy
(`B0-R0-C32`). So the cost of digestion is counted once, here, as expenditure.

Physical activity expenditure is the energy spent moving, whether as deliberate exercise or
as the ordinary movement of a day.

Resting expenditure depends mainly on age, sex, body size and body composition. Physical activity
expenditure depends on how much the body moves, in proportion to its weight. The thermic
effect of food depends on what was eaten.

**In plain terms.** Every day, a body spends energy on three different kinds of work, and it does not spend the
same amount on each.

Much of it goes on staying alive, doing nothing else. That is resting expenditure, and it is
usually the biggest of the three. It is bigger in a bigger body, because more tissue
means more of that background work. A person with more fat-free mass spends more at rest,
whether or not they move at all that day.

A smaller share goes on the meal itself. Protein takes more effort to process than
carbohydrate, and carbohydrate takes more than fat. This is the smallest of the three shares.

The rest goes on movement: everything from a deliberate workout to walking to the bus. Carry
more weight through the same walk and it costs more.

A bigger body spends more, mostly because resting expenditure and movement both scale with
size.

**Illustration.** Two patients come to the same clinic on the same day, both adults, both sedentary. One
weighs 55 kilograms. The other weighs 95 kilograms and carries more fat-free mass than the
first.

Ask which of them has the higher daily energy expenditure, before checking anything.

Open the review, Hall and Guo 2017, at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568065/,
and search for linearly related.

> Across a wide range of weights, REE is linearly related to both fat-free mass and body
> fat, such that the elevated REE with obesity is generally in line with what is expected
> for the body weight and composition.

So the heavier patient's resting expenditure is expected to be higher. Fat-free mass counts
for more of resting expenditure than body fat does, though both add to it.

Now search the same page for proportion to overall body weight.

> The energy expended in physical activities is determined by their duration and intensity
> in proportion to overall body weight.

Now imagine the heavier patient loses ten kilograms over the following year, some of it
fat-free mass along with the fat. Search the same page for declines with weight loss.

> physical activity energy expenditure declines with weight loss unless its quantity or
> intensity increases to compensate.

A smaller body doing the same daily activities spends less doing them. That is not a failure
of anything.

**Where this picture breaks.** "Bigger body, more expenditure" holds for resting and activity expenditure. It does not hold
for the thermic effect of food, which follows what was eaten.

The pattern describes what is generally expected for a given body weight and composition.

**Figure.** If the cost of an activity is in proportion to body weight, as Hall and Guo (2017) put it, the same walk, or any activity that carries the body, costs the 95 kg patient 95/55, about 1.73 times what it costs the 55 kg patient. After losing ten kilograms, at 85 kg, it costs about 1.55 times: less, for the same activity.

*What the figure shows:* A straight line through three points: 1 at 55 kg, about 1.55 at 85 kg and about 1.73 at 95 kg, the cost of the same activity relative to the 55 kg patient.

**Must know points for you.**

- Daily expenditure is not one thing. It is resting expenditure, usually the largest share; the thermic effect of food, the smallest; and physical activity expenditure.
- Resting expenditure rises with both fat-free mass and body fat. A person with more body fat generally has more fat-free mass too. So do not assume a person with obesity has a slow metabolism. The source reports the opposite. Their resting expenditure is generally higher in total than a leaner person's, in line with their larger body.
- Both resting expenditure and physical activity expenditure scale with body size. The energy it takes to keep a person at a steady weight is their maintenance requirement. It is set by that weight. So it does not hold once their weight has changed, and their fat-free mass with it. Expect it to fall as their weight falls.
- Knowing the three components does not give you a patient's requirement. Use them to check what any computed requirement has left out.
