# B4 · Energy units: joule, kilojoule, calorie, kilocalorie

**Definition.** The joule is the SI unit of energy.

A kilojoule is one thousand joules. The prefix kilo means one thousand.

The calorie is not an SI unit. Its size in joules is fixed by agreement, not by measurement,
and two agreements are in use at once. The thermochemical calorie is 4.184 joules exactly. The
International Table calorie, settled at the Fifth International Conference on the Properties
of Steam in July 1956, is 4.1868 joules.

A kilocalorie is one thousand calories. It is the calorie used to express the energy content
of foods, and in food writing the prefix kilo is usually dropped.

In India, where a pre-packaged food has to carry nutritional information, the energy is
declared in kilocalories. Regulation 5(3)(b) of the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and
Display) Regulations, 2020 requires the label to carry the energy value in kcal. Those
regulations require no kilojoule figure anywhere.

Regulation 5(3)(e)(i) of the same regulations fixes the factors by which the declared energy
is calculated rather than measured.

**In plain terms.** Energy is measured, so energy has a unit. The SI unit is the joule.

A thousand joules is one kilojoule. Kilo means a thousand, here and everywhere else.

The calorie is not part of the SI. Its size was decided by committees, and two of them decided
differently.

Both numbers are exact, because both are decisions. Nobody went to a laboratory and got 4.184.

A kilocalorie is a thousand calories. It is the unit used for the energy in food.

In food work people write calories when they mean kilocalories. So when a packet, a paper or a
scheme document says calories, read kilocalories. Then check the size of the number to be sure.

**Illustration.** Start with the mistake, because it is the one waiting for you.

Open the National Food Security Act, 2013, and go to Schedule II. Find the row for lower
primary classes. The hot cooked meal is set at 450.

Take that 450 as calories and turn it into joules.

```working
    450 times 4.184 = 1,882.8
```

A meal of 1,882.8 joules. Something has gone wrong, and it is not the arithmetic.

Go back to the table and read the head of the column instead of the number under it. It says
Calories, and then, in brackets, Kcal.

So the meal is 450 kilocalories. Do it again properly, one line at a time.

```working
    450 kilocalories, first into calories
    450 times 1,000 = 4,50,000
    now into joules
    4,50,000 times 4.184 = 18,82,800
    and into kilojoules
    18,82,800 divided by 1,000 = 1,882.8
```

The digits are the same and the unit is not. Kilocalories
to kilojoules uses the same 4.184 as calories to joules, because both sides of the sum were
multiplied by a thousand.

Take the upper primary figure from the same table, 700 kilocalories, and convert it with each of
the two calories in turn.

```working
    700 times 4.184 = 2,928.8
    700 times 4.1868 = 2,930.76
```

Two answers, from one figure, both correct.

```working
    2,930.76 minus 2,928.8 = 1.96
```

Just under two kilojoules apart. The gap is in the fourth digit.

If
their kilojoule figure and yours part company in the fourth digit, neither of you has slipped.
You used different calories.

**Where this picture breaks.** The two conversion factors are exact and they stay exact.

What a conversion cannot do is improve the number you started with. It renames an amount.

**Must know points for you.**

- Nobody measured the calorie. Committees fixed it, and both of their values are exact by definition.
- One thermochemical calorie is 4.184 joules. That is the factor to use unless a paper tells you otherwise, and the same 4.184 turns kilocalories into kilojoules.
- A second calorie is in use. The International Table calorie is 4.1868 joules. Check which calorie each of them used before you tell either of them they are wrong.
- When food material says calories, read kilocalories, because the prefix is usually left off in practice. Then check the size. A meal in the hundreds is kilocalories.
- Say the unit out loud with every energy figure you quote, in clinic and in a meeting.
- Converting a figure never improves it. A two-digit figure written out to six digits after conversion has gained nothing but the look of precision.
- Schedule II of the National Food Security Act, 2013 heads its energy column Calories, with Kcal in brackets.
- An Indian food packet that must show nutritional information gives its energy in kilocalories. The rule is regulation 5(3)(b) of the labelling regulations of 2020. It asks for no kilojoule figure at all, so you must be able to convert.
- The energy printed on an Indian label is worked out, not measured. Regulation 5(3)(e)(i) of the same 2020 rules fixes the factors. Carbohydrate and protein are 4 kcal/g each, fat is 9 kcal/g and alcohol is 7 kcal/g. So take the grams printed on the label, multiply each by its factor, and add them up.
