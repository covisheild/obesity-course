# E4 · Heat and temperature; how heat moves

**Definition.** Temperature is a measure of how hot or cold something is. Heat is the spontaneous transfer of
energy due to a temperature difference. Heat is measured in joules, the SI unit of energy.
Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or in kelvin.

The specific heat of a substance is the heat required to change the temperature of one kilogram
of it by one degree Celsius. Its unit is joules per kilogram per degree Celsius.

The letter Q stands for the quantity of heat transferred. The letter m stands for the mass of
the substance. The letter c stands for its specific heat.

```working
    Q = m times c times ΔT
```

The change in temperature is the final temperature minus the initial temperature.

Every process involving heat transfer takes place by only three methods. Conduction is heat
transfer through stationary matter by physical contact. Convection is heat transfer by the
macroscopic movement of a fluid. Heat transfer by radiation occurs when microwaves, infrared
radiation, visible light, or another form of electromagnetic radiation is emitted or absorbed.

**In plain terms.** **Temperature** is how hot a thing is. A thermometer reads it. It is a level, not an amount. It does not care how much of the thing there is. A spoonful of boiling
water and a bucket of boiling water are at the same temperature.

**Heat** is energy moving, because one thing is hotter than another. It is an amount, and it is
measured in joules, like every other energy in this book.

So the bucket and the spoonful are at the same temperature and hold very different amounts of energy.

Energy goes from the hotter thing to the colder thing, and it keeps going until the two are at the same temperature. Then it stops.

The letter Q stands for the quantity of heat. The letter m stands for the mass. The letter c stands for the number belonging to the material. The Greek capital
letter delta, written Δ, is how mathematics writes "the change in". So the pair ΔT stands for the change in temperature, and it is read "delta T".

```working
    Q = m times c times ΔT
```

That number belonging to the material has a name: the specific heat. It is the joules you need for one kilogram of the stuff to go up by one degree.

```table
material         joules for one kilogram, one degree
water            4186
glass            840
iron and steel   452
```

Water needs more than nine times as much energy as steel for the same rise. A bigger temperature rise does not mean more heat went in. It means the two things were
different things.

One more word, because the section on the calorimeter used it without naming it. Heat capacity is the same idea for one particular object rather than for a
material. It is the joules that object needs to go up by one degree. A calorimeter has one, and you have already used it. The energy equivalent from that section
is the calorimeter's heat capacity. It is also why a calorimeter's degrees can be trusted: inside one instrument the object never changes, so the number never
changes.

The change in temperature is the temperature at the end minus the temperature at the start. If the thing cooled, that subtraction gives a negative number, and so
does Q. It means the energy went out rather than in.

Last, how heat actually gets from one place to another. There are exactly three ways, and that is not a list somebody stopped writing. It is all of them.

**Conduction** is touching. Energy moves through matter that is staying put, by contact. A steel
tumbler of hot chai and your fingers.

**Convection** is carrying. A fluid — a liquid or a gas — moves, and the energy goes with it. Hot
air rising off a tawa. Blood carrying warmth from the middle of you out to your skin.

**Radiation** is shining. Energy crosses as light, or as the invisible light below red that
bodies give off, and it needs nothing in between. The warmth on your face from an open fire
across the room.

Anything that keeps something warm or cool acts on one of them directly. So when somebody tells you their product keeps heat in, ask two questions. Which of the
three does it act on? And what happens to the other two?

**Illustration.** In the calorimeter, a bigger temperature rise meant more energy.

Go and break it, with two things you have in the kitchen.

Take a steel tawa that weighs one kilogram. Take one kilogram of water in a pan. Put them on two identical burners and give each exactly the same amount of energy.

Use 4,186 joules, because it makes the first sum come out at one.

Multiply the mass by the specific heat to get the joules it needs for one degree.

```working
    1 times 4186 = 4186
```

You are giving it exactly that, so the water goes up by one degree Celsius.

Now the steel, the same way.

```working
    1 times 452 = 452
```

So 4,186 joules will take the tawa a long way up.

Divide the energy by what it needs for one degree. That is 4,186 divided by 452, which comes to
9.26 degrees Celsius, to two decimal places. Check it backwards.

```working
    452 times 9.26 = 4185.52
```

That is within half a joule of 4,186.

One thermometer reads a rise of one degree and the other reads a rise of more than nine. The temperature rise did not measure the heat. It measured the heat divided by
what the object needed per degree, and the two objects needed very different amounts.

Inside one calorimeter the object never changes. So the joules it needs per degree never change either, and that is the energy equivalent you were handed.

Within one instrument, twice the rise is twice the energy. Across two instruments it is worth nothing, because the two heat capacities differ and nobody has said by how
much.

Two hundred grams of chai are poured at 95 degrees Celsius and left. An hour later they are at 30. The change in temperature is the end minus the start.

```working
    30 minus 95 = -65
```

Now the energy, taking 0.2 kilograms of a liquid that is mostly water.

```working
    0.2 times 4186 = 837.2
    837.2 times -65 = -54418
```

Minus 54,418 joules. The minus sign says it left.

Pour chai into a steel tumbler and pick it up. Your fingers know within a second. The steel stayed where it was and the energy came through it into your hand. Now pour
the same chai into a clay kulhar and pick that up. You can hold it. Nothing about the chai changed. The matter between the chai and your fingers did.

Hold your palm a foot above a hot tawa, not touching it. The air over the tawa is being heated, it rises, and it brings the energy up to your hand.

Stand three feet from an open tandoor and face it. Your face warms, and the air in between did not have to carry anything.

Now the part that makes the three names useful rather than decorative. Read what the same section says about controlling them.

> We can control rates of heat transfer by choosing materials (such as thick wool clothing for
> the winter), controlling air movement (such as the use of weather stripping around doors), or
> by choice of color (such as a white roof to reflect summer sunlight).

Choosing the material acts on how easily heat gets through it. Stopping the air moving acts on convection. Choosing the colour acts on radiation.

Take a sheet of paper and write the three routes down the left side. Then put the claim against each one in turn and ask what it does about that route.

Try it on a white roof, which is the book's own example. Colour acts on radiation, so the roof absorbs less of the sunlight falling on it. That is the only route the
colour touches. The other two are downstream of it. A cooler slab conducts less heat down into the ceiling, and a cooler ceiling warms the air under it less. So naming
the route tells you where the control bites, not which routes end up different. Whether the white roof is enough depends on how much of the heat was arriving as
sunlight on the roof in the first place.

**Where this picture breaks.** The heat equation holds only while the substance stays the same substance in the same phase. Boil the water and it stops. The energy then goes into turning liquid into
steam, and the temperature sits still while that happens. Any calculation that crosses a boiling point or a melting point has left this section behind.

Specific heat is not truly a constant. Over the ranges used here the variation is small enough to ignore.

A person is not one substance and has no single specific heat you can look up. Nothing here gives you a figure for heating or cooling a person, and none of these numbers
should be carried across to one.

All three routes run at once in every real situation. Naming the route a control acts on does not settle what the other two then do.

This section gives no rates. It says by which routes heat moves and never how fast.

Sweating is not one of the three, and the book does not treat it as one. Any claim that turns on evaporation is outside what you are holding.

"Only three methods" is a claim about routes across a boundary. It is not a promise that every warming or cooling you meet is explained by naming one. A person getting
warmer may be doing it by running a chemical reaction inside their own boundary. That is not a transfer across the boundary at all.

**Must know points for you.**

- A bigger temperature rise does not mean more heat went in. Give a kilogram of steel and a kilogram of water the same energy. The steel rises more than nine times as far. So a thermometer reading is not an energy.
- Heat is energy on the way, not a thing an object holds, so refuse the sentence "this object contains so much heat". Heat is the name of a transfer.
- When anyone turns a temperature reading into an energy, ask two things. What heat capacity did they use? Where did it come from? Inside one machine the heat capacity is fixed, so the reading can be trusted. Across two machines a degree is not a degree. Rises set side by side with no heat capacity named are not energies set side by side.
- Water needs more than nine times the energy that iron or steel needs, for the same rise in the same mass. The figures are 4186 joules per kilogram per degree Celsius at 15.0 °C, against 452.
- The heat equation holds only while the substance stays the same substance in the same phase. It breaks at every boiling point, every melting point and every chemical reaction. When a calculation reaches one of those points, stop rather than substitute.
- A temperature is not an amount of energy, so never read a patient's temperature as a statement about energy gained or lost. One is in degrees and the other in joules.
- Heat moves by conduction, convection and radiation, and by nothing else, so make every claim about keeping warm or cool name its route. Once the route is named, the question becomes how much was arriving that way, and that can be answered.
- Teach the two-objects demonstration before the formula. After that they will not read a rise as an energy again.
