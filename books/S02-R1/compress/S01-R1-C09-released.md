# S01-R1-C09 · Intake and expenditure are outputs of a regulated system

**Definition.** Energy intake and energy expenditure are not two independent dials a person sets and then
leaves alone. They are outputs of a system that senses body weight, or a signal that tracks
it, and pushes back when weight falls. Cut intake, and two things move against the cut.

Expenditure falls by more than the loss of fat and lean mass alone accounts for. After a
person sustains a loss of 10% or more of body weight, 24-hour energy expenditure falls by
about 20% to 25%, and expenditure ends up about 10% to 15% lower than the change in fat and
lean mass alone would predict. The extra fall is the adaptive part: a formerly heavier
person now needs roughly 300 to 400 fewer kilocalories (kcal) a day than a person of the same
weight and composition who was never heavier. How long this lasts is still disputed.

Appetite rises. Per kilogram lost, eating rises by about 100 kcal a day, more than three times
the fall of about 30 kcal a day in expenditure. So the appetite response, not the metabolic
one, is the larger of the two.

Both responses run in the direction that returns stored energy toward where it was.

**In plain terms.** Cut what you eat, and two things start moving against you.

Lose a tenth of your body weight and keep it off, and your daily expenditure drops by about a
fifth to a quarter. Some of that drop is exactly what a smaller body should cost to run. The
rest is extra: expenditure ends up about 10 to 15% lower than a body of that new size would
need.

At the same time your appetite rises. Appetite, not metabolism, does most of the pushing back.

Neither of these is the person's character failing.

How large the pushback is probably varies between people. The trial behind the appetite estimate
reported only a group average.

**Illustration.** Take the trial behind the 100 kcal figure, analysed by Polidori and colleagues (2016). It ran
for 52 weeks, in 153 people with type 2 diabetes, a condition in which blood glucose runs
high. Each took a drug that makes the kidneys pass glucose out in the urine instead of
keeping it.

Body weight was measured repeatedly over the year. The researchers worked back, from how
weight actually moved, to how much people must have been eating. They found intake climbing
as weight fell: close to 100 kcal a day of extra eating for every kilogram already lost.

In the studies Rosenbaum and Leibel review, people holding a loss of a tenth or more of their
body weight needed 300 to 400 fewer kcal a day than someone who had always weighed that much.
That is the expenditure side of the same loop.

Now compare the two, kilogram for kilogram. Take a made-up person
3 kg lighter than they started, within the several kilograms the trial's average loss
reached. Before any loss, both changes are 0. At 3 kg, extra eating is 3 times 100 kcal, about 300 kcal a day. The fall in expenditure, at
about 30 kcal a day per kilogram, is 3 times 30, about 90 kcal a day. Eating has moved more
than three times as far.

**Where this picture breaks.** The 100 kcal per kg figure is a slope fitted to one trial's group average, in adults with
type 2 diabetes losing weight slowly over a year through glucose loss in urine, not from
dieting. The trial did not measure expenditure: the 30 kcal per kg comes from other studies,
of losses of 10 to 20%. Its authors do not yet know whether the slope holds over a range of
weight losses, so the worked case stays within the several kilograms the trial saw.

**Figure.** Per kilogram lost, extra eating rises by about 100 kcal a day and expenditure falls by about 30 kcal a day. For the made-up person 3 kg lighter, that is about 300 against about 90 kcal a day. Both lines are fitted slopes, not measurements of one person.

*What the figure shows:* Two straight lines from 0 at 0 kg lost. Extra eating reaches about 300 kcal a day at 3 kg lost; the fall in expenditure reaches about 90 kcal a day.

**Must know points for you.**

- "Cut intake and weight keeps falling at the same rate" is incomplete. Treat a plateau in weight loss as the expected output of this loop, not as proof the patient has stopped trying.
- A conversation about "slower metabolism" that never mentions rising hunger is describing the smaller half of what the patient is fighting.
- A formerly heavier person needs roughly 300 to 400 fewer kcal a day than someone who was always that weight, to hold the same weight. How long that lasts is still disputed.
- Do not quote the 100 kcal per kg or the 300-400 kcal figures to a patient as what will happen to them.
