# S36-R1-C05 · Open, closed and leading questions

**Definition.** A closed question fixes the possible answers before the respondent speaks. The answer is yes
or no, a number, or one of a set of options the question supplies. Britten's example, from a
structured interview, is "Is your health: excellent, good, fair, or poor?"

An open question leaves the form of the answer to the respondent. It invites their own account,
in their own words. Britten's examples, from a semi-structured interview, are "What do you
think good health is?" and "How do you consider your own health?"

Open and closed describe the form of the answer. Whether a question is leading is a separate
property. A leading question shows the answer the asker expects, or takes for granted part of
what it is asking about. A question of either form can be leading. "Don't you find the diet
hard?" is closed and leading. "How hard is the diet for you?" is open in form and leading,
because it takes for granted that the diet is hard.

A double question asks two things in one sentence. Whatever the respondent answers, you cannot
tell which of the two questions it answers.

The standard for the questions of a semi-structured interview, in DeJonckheere and Vaughn's
words: "All questions should be open ended, neutral, clear and avoid leading language."
Britten, following Patton, gives the same list with "sensitive" added. A closed question is
still the right form for a plain fact, such as a person's age.

The word "leading" has a second use. In the list of probing techniques DeJonckheere and Vaughn
reproduce, "leading" names a follow-up that asks the interviewee to explain their reasoning.
That is not the leading question defined here.

**In plain terms.** The last section set out the semi-structured interview: a short list of questions, asked in a
way that lets the respondent talk. This section is about the questions themselves. Every
question you write can be put through three checks.

**Check 1: the form of the answer.** Could the person answer with yes, no, a number, or one of
the options you gave them? Then it is a closed question. "Do you travel here by bus?" is closed.
If they have to answer in their own words, it is an open question. "Tell me how you get here" is
open.

**Check 2: what the question takes for granted.** Book 0 called a statement that an argument
rests on a premise (`B0-R0-C42`). Some questions carry a premise the respondent never offered.
"How hard is the diet for you?" rests on "the diet is hard". Any answer at all seems to grant
that.

Other questions show which answer you would like: "Don't you think the tablets help?" Both
kinds are leading questions. A leading question can be open or closed. Check 1 does not catch it.

**Check 3: how many questions are in it.** "Did the doctor explain the diet, and did you follow
it?" is two questions. If the person says "no", you do not know which one they answered. That is
a double question. Split it.

One more trap, about the word "why". "Why did you stop coming?" is open. It also asks the person
to give reasons for what they did, as if they owed you an explanation.

It carries the premise that they decided to stop. In a clinic where staff have already told them to come back, it can
sound like the start of a telling-off. Ask what happened instead: "Tell me about the last time
you came here."

Closed questions are not wrong. "How old are you?" should be closed. The rule is about the
questions that carry your study: those should be open, neutral, clear, and in words the
respondent uses.

Last, a clash of words to know about. DeJonckheere and Vaughn's guide to interviewing lists a
probe called "leading". There it means asking someone to explain their reasoning, as in "Tell me how you came
to that decision". That is a good probe. It is not a leading question.

**Must know points for you.**

- An open question can still lead. "How hard is the diet?" is open in form and hands the respondent your word, "hard". Run the premise check on every question, including the ones that start with "how" or "what".
- Refuse the quick fix of deleting "don't you". A rewrite that removes the signal words and keeps the premise is still leading. Ask about an event ("tell me about the last time...") rather than a judgement you have already made.
- Ask "what happened" before "why". "Why did you stop coming?" asks a patient to defend a decision you have assumed she made. In a clinic it can sound like the start of a telling-off. The reasons usually arrive on their own in the account of what happened.
- A yes to a leading question tells you the respondent did not contradict you. Before you quote any answer as a finding, go back to the question that produced it. If the question carried the premise, the answer cannot be evidence for it.
- The three checks tell you whether a question gives the respondent room. They do not tell you whether it asks about the right thing, or in words the respondent uses. A guide that passes every check can still miss the reason people stop coming. Only trying it on real respondents shows that.
- When a trainee's list of questions is all closed, do not tell them "use open questions". Take one of their questions, ask them what answer they expect, and show them that the question already contains it. Then rewrite it together as a question about an event.
