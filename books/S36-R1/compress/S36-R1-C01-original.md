# S36-R1-C01 · Two kinds of question: how many, and why and how

**Definition.** A research question of the first kind asks how many or how much: how common something is, how
large a quantity is, or how large a difference a treatment makes. It is answered by counting and
measuring across a sample, in surveys and trials. Research of this kind is called quantitative
research.

A question of the second kind asks what something is, what it means to the people involved, how
it comes about and why. It is answered by qualitative research: research that gathers people's own
words and actions, by interview, group discussion or watching, and analyses them without turning
them into counts.

The two kinds answer different questions, and neither is a weaker version of the other. A design is
strong or weak only for the question it is used on.

Evidence-based medicine is the practice of basing treatment decisions on the results of research.
For the question "does this treatment work, and by how much?", it gives most weight to the
randomised controlled trial and to the pooled result of several such trials. In a
randomised controlled trial, chance decides who receives the treatment. The ranking of designs built on this, the evidence
hierarchy, is a ranking for that one kind of question. It says nothing about which design best
answers a question of the second kind.

A thing cannot be counted until someone has said what it is. So qualitative work can come before a
count, to find what should be counted and in what words, and after one, to explain how a number came
about. Studies that combine the two kinds by design are called mixed methods; a later book in this
subject teaches them.

**In plain terms.** Some questions ask how many or how much. How many adults with obesity seen at a clinic last year
came back for a second visit? By how much does a reminder message raise that number? You answer
these by counting and measuring across a sample, as Book 0's sections on average and on sampling
taught. Research that counts and measures is called quantitative research.

Other questions ask what, how and why. What is a clinic visit like for a woman who has to bring her
grandchild along? How does a person decide that the next visit is not worth the trouble? Why do
patients stop coming? You answer these by asking people and listening to what they say, in their
own words. That is qualitative research.

Neither kind is a weaker version of the other. A trial cannot tell you why patients stop coming. An
interview cannot tell you how many do. Each is strong for its own question and weak for the other.

You may have met a ladder of study designs, called the evidence hierarchy, with the randomised
controlled trial near the top. In such a trial, chance decides who gets the treatment, and the
groups are then compared. That ladder ranks designs for one question: does this treatment work, and
by how much? It has no rung for "why do patients stop coming?"

The two kinds also feed each other. You cannot count a thing until someone has said what it is, so
asking often comes first. And a number, once you have it, often needs asking to explain it.

**Illustration.** Here is the setting this book uses from start to finish. It is made up. A district hospital in
Chhattisgarh runs a non-communicable disease (NCD) clinic. Non-communicable diseases are
long-lasting conditions that do not pass from person to person, such as diabetes and high blood
pressure. Many of its adult patients
with obesity come once or twice and then stop coming. You want to know why.

Start with an attempt that fails. Suppose the clinic adds a question to its follow-up phone call:
"Why did you not come back? Distance, cost, felt better, or other." A month later, a staff member
counts the answers. Each reason now has a number beside it.

Ask where the four choices came from. The clinic staff wrote them, before anyone had asked a
patient. A reason nobody on the staff thought of can only land in "other". And "other" carries no
words, so you cannot tell what is in it. The count is exact. It is an exact count of the staff's
guesses.

Pope and Mays put the missing step plainly: until something has been sorted into what it is, it
cannot be measured. The tick-list skipped that step.

Now sort four questions about the same clinic yourself, before you read the table. For each one,
say whether it asks how many or how much, or what, how and why. Then say what would answer it.

```table
| Question | Kind | What answers it |
| How many adults with obesity seen last year came back for a second visit? | how many | Count the clinic register |
| Does a reminder text message the day before raise the share who come back? | how much | A trial: chance decides who gets the message, then the groups are compared |
| Why do adults with obesity stop coming back? | why | Find people who stopped, ask them, and listen |
| What is a clinic day like for someone who works for a daily wage? | what and how | Ask such people, and listen |
```

Look hard at the third row. No count answers it, because you do not yet know what to count. No
trial answers it either, because a trial tests a fix you already have, and you do not have one yet.

Suppose you go and ask. In a made-up example, one woman says the clinic is on the first floor. Her
knees hurt on the stairs, and she waits standing because the benches are full. Nobody on the staff
had put "stairs" on the tick-list. Now it can go on one. And now a count means something again: how
many patients find the stairs a problem is a how-many question.

So the two kinds pass the work back and forth. Asking finds what matters and in what words.
Counting says how common it is. A trial says whether a fix works. Studies built to do both on
purpose are called mixed methods, and a later book in this subject teaches them.

**Where this picture breaks.** The line between the two kinds is not always this clean. "Which patients stop coming, and why?"
asks for a count and a reason at once. Split a question like that into its two halves before you
choose a method, and give each half its own method.

A qualitative report may also contain small counts, such as "several people mentioned the
fare". That is a summary of what was heard, not an answer to a how-many question. The next section
shows why.

**Must know points for you.**

- The error is to treat an interview study as a trial that could not afford enough people. It is not a small trial. It answers a question a trial cannot, such as why patients stop coming. A trial answers one it cannot, such as whether a reminder works.
- Before you choose a method, write the research question as one sentence and look at its first words. "How many" and "how much" send you to counting and measuring. "What", "how" and "why" send you to asking people. If the sentence holds both, split it into two questions.
- Someone in a meeting will call interview findings "anecdotal" because they sit low on the evidence hierarchy. Answer that the ladder ranks designs for testing whether a treatment works. It has no rung for why patients leave. Then say how the study differs from a story told in passing. The people were chosen on a stated plan. What they said was worked through step by step. Other explanations were looked for.
- A drop-out number tells a programme how big its problem is. It does not say what to change. When you are asked to fix a drop-out rate, ask whether anyone has asked the people who dropped out before a fix is chosen.
- Asking people tells you which reasons exist and how they work. It cannot tell you how common each reason is, or whether a fix works. The moment a decision turns on "how many" or "does it work", go back to counting or to a trial.
