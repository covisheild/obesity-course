# S36-R1-C03 · The interview: structured, semi-structured, in-depth

**Definition.** A research interview is a conversation in which a researcher asks a respondent about a topic in
order to gather the respondent's account of it. Interviews are sorted into three kinds by how much
is fixed before the interview begins.

A structured interview administers a questionnaire. The questions, their order and their wording
are fixed, most answers are chosen from a fixed list, and interviewers are trained to ask every
question the same way. A questionnaire read aloud is a structured interview, and its answers are
counted.

A semi-structured interview follows a short guide of open-ended questions that define the area to
be explored. The interviewer changes the order and wording of the questions to fit the respondent,
adds questions that arise from what the respondent says, and may leave the guide to pursue an idea
in more detail.

An in-depth interview fixes less still. It covers one or two issues in much greater detail, and
after an opening question the interviewer's questions are built from what the respondent has said,
mostly asking for clarification and detail. No interview is wholly without structure, so
"unstructured interview" is a misleading name.

A research interview is not a clinical consultation. The clinical task is to fit the patient's
problem into a medical category in order to choose treatment. The research task is to find the
respondent's own way of seeing the matter, and to avoid imposing the researcher's categories and
assumptions on it. The interviewer therefore does not diagnose, advise or correct.

The most common qualitative interview is semi-structured: one respondent, face to face, in one
sitting, usually audio recorded with the respondent's agreement.

**In plain terms.** An interview, in research, is one person asking another about a topic and listening to the answer.
Interviews come in three kinds. What separates them is how much is decided before you sit down.

In a structured interview, everything is decided. You read out a questionnaire. Every respondent
hears the same questions, in the same order, in the same words. Most answers are picked from a
list. A questionnaire read aloud to someone who cannot read it is a structured interview, and its
answers get counted.

In a semi-structured interview, the topics are decided and the path is not. You carry a short guide
of open questions: questions the person answers in their own words, not by picking from a list. You
change the order and the wording to suit the person. When they say something you did not expect,
you follow it, then come back to the guide.

In an in-depth interview, almost nothing is decided. You open with one broad question about one or
two issues. Everything after that comes from what the person has said: "What happened then?" "What
do you mean by that?"

Most qualitative studies use the semi-structured kind: one person, one sitting, face to face,
recorded with their agreement.

If you are a doctor, one thing will feel wrong at first. A research interview is not taking a
history. In a history, you steer the patient's story toward a diagnosis and a plan. In a research
interview, you want the person's own way of seeing it, and you try not to push your categories onto
it. You do not diagnose, you do not advise, and you do not correct.

**Must know points for you.**

- A research interview is not a history with a recorder running. In a history, you steer toward a diagnosis and a plan. In a research interview, steering is the error, because it swaps the respondent's account for your categories.
- Choose the kind of interview from the question. A how-many question with known answer options takes a structured form whose answers you count. A why or how question takes a semi-structured guide. One story told in full, such as one family's experience of a diagnosis, can take an in-depth interview.
- When a respondent mentions a symptom, you will feel pulled back into doctor mode. Do not ask the diagnostic question and do not advise. Ask her to say more, and offer to come back to her health question when the interview ends.
- A questionnaire read aloud is still a questionnaire. If your "interviews" read out fixed questions with fixed answers, report them as a structured survey and count the answers. Do not present them as qualitative findings.
- The labels are used loosely in papers, so the name of the interview tells you little. Look in the methods for what the interviewer fixed in advance and what they followed. If the paper does not say, you cannot tell which kind you are reading, and you should say so.
- When you teach a resident to interview, have them play back their own recording. They mark every place they diagnosed, advised or corrected. Britten advises new interviewers to review recordings of their own interviews critically and to ask others for comments.
