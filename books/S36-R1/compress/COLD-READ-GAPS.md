## 1. Exercises and practice problems

### C01 · Two kinds of question
**Ex 1.**
1. Share also told they have diabetes: how many. Count from the clinic register or records (or a survey).
2. How patients decide whether a follow-up date is worth keeping: how and why. Ask patients and listen (interviews).
3. Does moving to the ground floor raise the return share: how much. A trial, where chance decides which patients get the change, then compare.
4. What patients understand "follow-up" to mean: what. Ask them and listen.
5. Which patients stop, and why: mixed. Split it. "Which / how many" goes to the register; "why" goes to interviews.

**Ex 2.** Premise 1: interviews are the weakest kind of evidence. Premise 2 (unspoken): the weakest evidence should not be used when a trial is possible. Conclusion: wait for a trial before looking at why patients leave. The inference fails even if premise 1 is granted. "Weakest" is on a ranking of designs for testing whether a treatment works (C01 calls it "the ladder"). The question "why do they leave" is not on that ranking, and a trial cannot answer it at all. So the wait is for evidence that will never come. (Gap: I am leaning on "the ladder", which I was never shown; see gap C01-1.)

**Ex 3.** "Qualitative research asks people why things happen and listens to their answers in their own words. It cannot tell you how many people think something. It can tell you what the reasons are, which a count cannot find until someone knows what to count." Quotable: "A count can only count what somebody already thought to put on the list; asking people is how you find out what belongs on it."

### C02 · Who you talk to
**Ex 1.** Fifteen chosen women cannot give a percentage of anyone; "60 per cent" (9 of 15) is presented as a rate. Rewrite: "A study that interviewed 15 women with obesity in Raipur, chosen for what they could say about clinic visits, found that long waiting times were one reason some of them avoided visits, and described how the waiting put them off. It did not measure how common this is."

**Ex 2.** Men to choose (my invention, the text gives only "far villages" as a dimension):

- men on a daily wage (lost earnings);
- men who run their own shop or farm (who covers for them);
- older men with knee or walking trouble (stairs, standing);
- men who stopped after being told their numbers were fine;
- a man who lives far away but has kept coming. This row may break my first guess (that distance drives stopping).

**Ex 3.** Answer first: "The study cannot size a bus-pass budget. '14 of 20' describes the 20 people the researchers chose, not the district. What it shows is that fares are one real reason, and how they stop people. To size a budget, count: take a random sample of registered patients, or the complete register, and ask how many stopped and how many name fares." (Gap: the "answer first" page format is not taught anywhere I have read.)

### C03 · The interview
**Ex 1.** (1) Structured: printed form, 24 fixed questions, answers marked on a scale. (2) In-depth: one opening invitation, then only what the respondent raised. (3) Semi-structured: a guide of six open questions, order changeable, follow-ups allowed.

**Ex 2.** The interviewer leaves the research interview at the second line, "Fine? What was your last reading? You know, sugar can be high without any symptoms." That asks a diagnostic question and corrects her. Rewrite: "Your sugar was fine by then. Tell me more about that."

**Ex 3.** What I could teach from C03 alone: a history steers the story toward a diagnosis and plan; a research interview must not, because steering replaces the respondent's account with your categories. You do not diagnose, advise or correct; you follow what she says. Beyond this, the "new skills" (probes, waiting, echoing) are only taught in C08, so at this point I can only say what not to do. (Gap C03-5.)

### C05 · Open, closed and leading questions
**Ex 1.** (1) Closed (yes/no), leading: carries the premise that monthly visits keep sugar under control, and it teaches. (2) Open, but leading: assumes she decided to stop, and "why" asks her to defend it. (3) Closed (options supplied by me), and it supplies my categories. One opening: "Tell me about the last time you came to the clinic."

**P1.** "Do you travel to the clinic by bus?" Closed (yes/no); leading, since it assumes she travels to the clinic and names the bus; not double.

**P2.** a. Open, not leading, not double. b. Closed (a number), not leading, not double. c. Closed, leading ("don't you" shows the answer), not double. d. Leading (premise: the walking is hard). Open or closed: I cannot decide. "How hard" can be answered "very", "a bit" or with a story; the text's test (yes, no, a number, or one of the options you gave) does not say what a degree word is. (Gap C05-4.)

**P3.** Closed (yes/no each half), double; the second half assumes she was given a diet to follow, so mildly leading. Split: "What did the doctor tell you about food?" "What happened when you tried it at home?"

**P4.** Closed (yes/no), leading (names distance, assumes she stopped for one reason), not double. Replace: "Tell me about the time you last came, and what happened after that."

**P5.** Open in form, leading: she said the strip is still there, not that she stopped taking them. She may hear an accusation and defend herself. Better: "A whole strip is still there?" (echo), or "Tell me more about the tablets."

**P6.** 1 closed, not leading, not double. 2 open, not leading, not double. 3 closed, not leading?, not double (assumes rice exists in the diet; I'd call it not leading). 4 closed, leading, not double. 5 open, leading (assumes the family supports her), double (diet and walking). 6 open, leading (assumes she eats after the others). **P7.** Check 1 broke. Check 1 is about the form of the answer, not the opening words. "Do you eat rice at night?" can be answered yes or no, so it is closed.

**P8.** The last line broke. Removing "don't you" did not remove the premise that the diet is hard; "How hard do you find the diet?" still leads.

**P9.** "Tell me about the food the doctor asked you to eat, and what happened when you tried it." The answers still cannot show how many patients find the diet hard, or whether the diet is why they stop.

**P10.** "How do you get to the clinic?" is the only one that does not lead. "How much does your weight worry you?" assumes worry. "How did the nurse's advice help you?" assumes it helped. "How often do you miss the evening walk?" assumes there is an evening walk and that it is missed; it may also be closed (a number). Starting with "how" does not make a question open or neutral. What this does not establish: whether the questions ask about the right thing, or in the respondent's words, and whether the interviews as a whole were steered.

### C06 · Topic guide
**Ex 1.** Study question: how do families in which one adult has obesity decide what is cooked for the evening meal?
- Warm-up (5 min): Tell me about your household: who lives with you? (factual, easy)
- Main 1 (8 min): What does a usual evening meal at home look like? Prompts: who cooks; what is cooked; when.
- Main 2 (10 min): Tell me about how yesterday's evening meal was decided. Prompts: who chose; what shaped it.
- Main 3 (10 min): Tell me about a time when people at home wanted different things for dinner. Prompts: what happened; who gave way.
- Main 4 (8 min): What, if anything, has changed in your cooking over the last few years? Prompts: reasons in their words.
- Main 5 (5 min): What do the others at home say about the food you make?
- Closing (4 min): Is there anything I have not asked about that I should have?
Total 50 minutes. Three checks: all main questions open; Main 4 uses "if anything" to avoid assuming change; none double. (I had to invent the format of prompts and the minute split; no full example guide is shown. Gaps C06-2, C06-3.)

**Ex 2.** In order of importance: (a) the warm-up asks for name, address and income: names should be kept off recordings (C07), income is personal and should be late, and it is five closed questions in one; (b) Q4 "Why don't you follow the diet?" assumes she does not and asks her to defend it; (c) Q2 is closed and uses the clinic's word "obesity" and a debate question unrelated to why patients stop; (d) no prompts, no time plan, and main questions not tied to a stated study question; (e) "Thank you" is not a closing question. Q3 is good and should be the opening.

## 2. Synthesis from the text alone

**What a transcript is, and how it differs from the recording.** The recording is the audio record of what was said. The transcript is a written version of that recording, made by a person or a program, and it is what analysis reads, marks, counts and quotes. It is not the same thing: whoever types makes choices. Full verbatim keeps fillers, repeats, broken-off words, pauses, laughs and the interviewer's "mm-hm"; cleaned transcription tidies to readable sentences and drops them, so a reader sees a smooth list where the speaker hesitated. Neither holds what the recording and field notes hold: the room, who was near, the moment someone went quiet (and the recording also lacks what was seen). Putting it into English is a second round of choices. So: keep the recording until analysis ends; return to it when a quote or reading turns on a line; mark inaudible places with their time on the recording; say which version any count or quote came from.

**Why "7 of 12 respondents said X" cannot be read as a rate.** The 12 were chosen on purpose for what they could tell you, not drawn by chance. A rate needs a denominator that stands for a population (A5, D6); here the denominator is the researcher's own choices. Pick more people from far villages and the 7 changes. It describes the twelve, not patients. In addition, what they "said" depends on who asked, where, and whether the question named X; a leading question or a doctor-interviewer pushes answers one way, and more interviews repeat that push rather than cancel it. What carries beyond the study is the reason and how it works, for others to test.

**One pilot interview, end to end.**
Before:
- Study question: e.g. how families with an adult with obesity decide the evening meal. Respondents: people I can reach who know the topic from their own lives, not my patients.
- Topic guide: warm-up, five to ten open main questions each tied to part of the question, prompts, closing question, time plan that adds up; every question through the three checks; dated version 1.
- Ethics: send the plan to an ethics committee and let it decide the review, before recording anyone. Get its answer in writing.
- Plan storage: where the recording lives, who hears it, labels (R1) on recording, file name and transcript; name list kept separately; ask the committee where audio may be kept before using any speech-to-text service. Plan to keep transcripts for later analysis and say so at consent.
- Book transcription time: 3 to 8+ hours per hour of audio.
Consent:
- Who I am and am not; why I am talking to this person; what happens; time; that saying no or stopping changes nothing; limits of confidentiality; ask if understood; read silence or restlessness as possible no. Then a separate question about the recorder; if no, take notes or do not proceed.
Running it:
- Somewhere private where they are at ease, no staff nearby. Recorder on after the separate yes. Easy open first question. Follow their words with probes: wait, echo, "tell me more", "what do you mean by...". Do not judge, teach, advise or correct; hold medical questions to the end. Return to the guide when a thread ends. Close with the closing question and thanks, then answer held questions.
Afterwards:
- Field notes the same day, before listening: what happened, where, who could hear, how it felt, surprises, and about myself: who I was to them, where I sat, moments I steered.
- Transcribe full verbatim, in the language spoken, with I/R1 labels and the mark set; note the choice at the top; check any machine draft against the audio.
- Read once pen down, then code, memo, mark any case that does not fit.
- Count talk share in words (and write how I counted).
- Listen for steering, leading questions, missed offers, not waiting.
- Revise the guide, date it, note what changed and why, before the next pilot.
- Report nothing about the topic as a finding.

## 3. What would change what I do, say, accept or refuse

Do:
- Write the research question as one sentence and split mixed "which and why" questions before choosing a method.
- Choose respondents on purpose, write down how and why, and include someone likely to break my first guess.
- Put every question through the three checks (answer form, premise, one question); ask "what happened" before "why".
- Carry a guide, not a script; easy first, personal late; use the respondent's words and switch to theirs.
- Send any plan to an ethics committee before recording; ask about the recorder separately; labels not names; separate name list.
- Probe with their words; wait; hold medical questions to the end; never correct beliefs (they are the data).
- Write field notes the same day, including about myself.
- Keep the recording; transcribe full verbatim for pilots in the language spoken; time-stamp inaudible parts.
- Count talk share in words; check each interview, not only the mean.
- Read transcripts before the next interview; keep deviant cases; memo; date guide versions.
- Check any quotation against its transcript lines and the line before it.

Say:
- "Two respondents described..." and who they were, never "two in five patients".
- The limits of confidentiality, not a promise of perfect secrecy.
- "I am not your doctor; nothing you say will change your care."
- In meetings: the evidence ladder ranks designs for whether a treatment works; it has no rung for why patients leave.
- Which transcript version, and which of share or ratio, I am quoting; how I counted.

Accept:
- A small number of respondents as not by itself a weakness.
- That a trial answers "does it work", a count "how many", interviews "why and how"; each passes work to the others.
- That group data are neither more nor less authentic than interview data.

Refuse:
- Percentages or rates from interview or focus group counts; "the group agreed"; "five of six said".
- Sample-size or power calculations as a demand on a qualitative study.
- "It's only a practice interview" as a reason to skip an ethics committee; recording after a no.
- Interviewing my own patients where avoidable; staff in patient groups.
- Clinic words in questions and codes ("compliance", "non-compliance", "follow-up", "BMI").
- "More interviews will average out the interviewer."
- A single vivid quotation as grounds to change a service; quotations with the counter-case removed.
- A low talk share as proof I did not lead; a pilot "finding" about the clinic.

## 4. Gaps, file by file

Each entry: the sentence that left the gap (quoted), what I had to supply, and what a reader without my background could not have done.

### C01 (4 gaps)
1. **Referent never introduced.** "That ladder ranks designs for one question: does this treatment work, and by how much?" No ladder, hierarchy or ranking of study designs appears in C01 or any released file. The must-know then says interviews "sit low on the evidence hierarchy". I had to supply the idea of a ranked list of study designs with trials near the top. A cold reader cannot tell what "that ladder" is, cannot answer Ex 2 (whose premise is "Interviews are the weakest kind of evidence") except by repeating the must-know line, and cannot rebut the "anecdotal" charge in their own words. **Serious.**
2. **Term used before explained.** "A trial cannot tell you why patients stop coming." "Trial" is first used here and is explained only later, inside a table cell ("chance decides who gets the message, then the groups are compared"). The reader carries an undefined word for two paragraphs.
3. **Definition incomplete.** "A question of the second kind asks what something is..." The Definition names the first kind "quantitative research" but never names the second; "qualitative research" first appears in plain terms. Minor, but the Definition is where a reader looks for the pair of terms.
4. **Evidence hierarchy in a must-know, untaught.** "Someone in a meeting will call interview findings 'anecdotal' because they sit low on the evidence hierarchy." New term ("evidence hierarchy"), never defined; I assumed it means the same as "that ladder".

### C02 (8 gaps)
1. **"Third way" without the first two.** "A qualitative study uses a third way." The file never names the first two ways. I supplied random sampling and convenience sampling from D6. A reader who missed or forgot D6 cannot tell what the three are.
2. **Pronouns with no antecedent (read twice).** "They do not estimate how common any of these is in a population." "They" and "these" refer to nothing in the preceding two sentences (which are about choosing people and convenience samples). I inferred "such studies" and "the reasons found". It reads as if a sentence was cut.
3. **Dangling "It".** "It can say what the reasons are, how each one works, and how they connect." The paragraph before is about a price of choosing; there is no "it" (study) nearby, and the contrast it answers ("it cannot say how common") is missing from plain terms. Inferred.

8. **Output format untaught.** Ex 3: "Write the page: answer first." The one-page, answer-first brief is not taught in any file I have (it recurs in C13 Ex 2 with "no methods section"). I wrote one by guessing the convention.
