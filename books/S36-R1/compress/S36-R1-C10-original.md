# S36-R1-C10 · Recording and transcribing

**Definition.** An audio recording of an interview is the record of what was said. A transcript is a written
version of that recording, made by a person or a program, and it is the text that analysis
works on.

Making a transcript involves decisions: what to include, whether to correct slips and grammar,
whether to keep repetitions, and how to mark pauses, laughter, interruptions, overlapping
speech and words that cannot be heard. Two transcribers can hear the same recording
differently. A transcript is therefore an interpretation of the recording, not a copy of it.

Full verbatim transcription (also called denaturalised) keeps everything said, including
fillers, false starts, repetitions and grammatical errors. Cleaned transcription (also called
intelligent verbatim, or naturalised) adapts speech to the norms of written text, removing
fillers and repetitions. There is no single correct style; the choice follows from the study's
question, is made before transcribing starts, and is applied to every transcript in the study.

A transcript labels each speaker's turn, marks what could not be heard and where in the
recording it falls, and replaces names and other identifying details as it is typed.
Transcribing one hour of audio takes several hours of work. A transcript is checked against
the recording before it is used.

**In plain terms.** The recording is what was said in the room. The transcript is what you will read, mark and quote
from for the rest of the study. They are not the same thing.

Someone has to turn the sound into lines of text. That person decides, over and over, what goes
in. Do the "um"s go in? The false start, where the respondent began one sentence and switched to
another? The four seconds of silence before a hard answer? The laugh? Each choice changes what a
later reader sees.

There are two broad ways to do it. Full verbatim keeps everything: every filler, every repeated
word, every broken-off sentence. Cleaned transcription tidies speech into readable sentences and
drops the fillers and repeats. Neither is the right one in general. Choose before you start, write
the choice down, and use it for every interview.

Transcribing is slow. McMullin puts it at three to more than eight hours of work for one hour of
audio. So the transcript you rely on is worth checking against the recording before you use it.

**Illustration.** Suppose you are interviewing a woman at the district hospital NCD clinic in Chhattisgarh. NCD
means non-communicable disease, the clinic for long-term conditions such as diabetes and high
blood pressure. She has stopped coming for follow-up, and you want to know why. She speaks
Chhattisgarhi. What follows is a made-up stretch of that interview, about twenty seconds of it,
written in English.

Here is the trap. Two people can type those twenty seconds and produce two different
transcripts, and both can say they wrote down "what she said".

First, the full verbatim version. `I` is you, the interviewer. `R1` is the respondent.

```table
| Speaker | Words |
| I | And after that, what happened? |
| R1 | After that... um... I, I did not- I thought I will come next month. [pause, 4 seconds] But my husband, he said, what is the use, you are going there and the weight is not going. |
| I | Mm-hm. |
| R1 | [laughs] So. My son [son's name] also cannot take leave. [inaudible, 00:12:41] and the bus also. |
```

Now the cleaned version of the same twenty seconds.

```table
| Speaker | Words |
| I | And after that, what happened? |
| R1 | After that I thought I will come next month. But my husband said, what is the use, you are going there and the weight is not going. |
| R1 | So my son also cannot take leave, and the bus also. |
```

Read the two side by side and list what the cleaned one dropped.

- The broken-off "I did not-". She started to say what she did, stopped, and moved to a plan instead.
- The four-second pause before her husband comes in.
- The laugh before "So".
- Your own "Mm-hm", a whole turn of yours, gone.
- The place where something could not be heard.

Now ask whether any of that matters for your question. It might. The pause and the laugh sit
exactly where the reason for not coming back moves from her own plan to her husband's words. A
reader of the cleaned version sees a smooth list of reasons. A reader of the full version sees
her hesitate at one of them. McMullin's advice is to keep laughter and other sounds when they
carry meaning.

Look at the marks the full version uses. There is no single standard set, so here is one simple
set to use in your pilot.

```table
| Mark | What it means |
| I, R1, R2 | who is speaking: you, then each respondent by number |
| [pause, 4 seconds] | a silence long enough to notice, with its length |
| [laughs] | a sound that is not a word |
| did not- | a word or sentence broken off |
| [inaudible, 00:12:41] | could not be heard, and the minute and second on the recording where it falls |
| [son's name] | a name taken out, replaced by who the person is |
```

The time on the inaudible mark is what lets you, or anyone checking you, go straight to that
second of the recording. McMullin suggests time marks for exactly this reason: they make it
much easier to check a transcript against the audio. On the recording she said her son's
name. You typed his role instead, as you went. The section on consent and privacy said
why.

Now the cost. McMullin puts transcription at between three hours and over eight hours of work
for one hour of audio, depending on typing speed. Your build target for this rung is five
pilot interviews. Suppose each one runs about an hour. Work out the typing time at each end of
her range.

```working
5 times 3 = 15 hours at the quick end
5 times 8 = 40 hours at the slow end
```

Here is the same sum one interview at a time.

```table
| Interviews, an hour each | Hours at 3 an hour | Hours at 8 an hour |
| 1 | 3 | 8 |
| 2 | 6 | 16 |
| 3 | 9 | 24 |
| 4 | 12 | 32 |
| 5 | 15 | 40 |
```

Forty is not a ceiling. Her words are "over eight hours", so a slow typist with a poor recording
can go past it. Pope, Ziebland and Mays add that one typical interview can fill 20 to 40 pages
of single-spaced text. Plan the hours before you book the interviews, not after.

**Where this picture breaks.** The exchange is made up, and it is written in English. A real recording would be in
Chhattisgarhi. Putting it into English adds a second set of choices. This example does not show
those.

The hours are for one person typing from a recording. They do not tell you how long it takes to
check a draft made by speech-to-text software. McMullin says such software is less accurate with
several voices or with different accents. So do not count on it to save the hours on a
Chhattisgarhi recording made in a busy clinic.

**Must know points for you.**

- A transcript is not the recording. Somebody chose what went in, and two people can hear the same words differently. Keep the recording until your analysis is finished. When a quotation or a reading turns on one line, go back and listen to that line.
- Decide full verbatim or cleaned before you type a word. Write the choice at the top of each transcript, and use the same one for all five pilots. For a pilot, full verbatim is the better choice. The point of a pilot is to hear your own interviewing, and cleaning takes out the fillers, interruptions and half-questions that show it.
- Budget three to more than eight hours of typing for every hour of recording. Five one-hour pilot interviews are 15 to 40 hours before you have read a single line for meaning.
- Speech-to-text software gives you a first draft, not a transcript. McMullin warns that it does worse with several voices or with accents. She also warns that sending audio to an online service raises questions of privacy. Check every line of a machine draft against the recording. Some phones copy recordings to an online store on their own, so find out whether yours does. Before a patient's voice leaves your own recorder, ask your institutional ethics committee where it may be kept.
- A transcript holds only what was said aloud. A glance at the mother-in-law sitting by the door, or the fact that a nurse walked in, is not in it. If the meaning of a passage turns on something seen or on a tone of voice, the transcript alone cannot be trusted for it. Go to the recording and to your field notes, and say in your analysis which one you used.
- Your respondents will speak Hindi or Chhattisgarhi. Putting their words into English is a second round of choices on top of transcription. Keep the transcript in the language spoken. Say which version any count or quotation comes from, so that anyone checking you can find the words.
- Some papers say only "interviews were recorded and transcribed". Ask who transcribed, full verbatim or cleaned, and whether anyone checked the text against the audio. McMullin found that 41 per cent of the interview papers she reviewed in one journal did not mention transcription at all. How to report it properly is taught in the next rung.
