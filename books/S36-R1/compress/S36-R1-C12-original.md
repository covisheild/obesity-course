# S36-R1-C12 · Reading a transcript, and what a code is

**Definition.** Reading a transcript for analysis begins with reading the whole of it, before anything is
marked, and then reading it again line by line.

On the second reading you mark each passage that bears on the study question and write a short
label beside it. That label is a code: a name for what the passage is about, which lets you find
the passage again and set it beside passages from other transcripts that carry the same label.
One passage can carry more than one code. Where the respondent's own words name the thing well,
they can serve as the label.

A memo is a note, written while you read, of what you notice and what you want to ask next.

A passage that runs against the pattern the other passages seem to show is a deviant or negative
case. It is kept and marked, not dropped, because it is what tests and sharpens the pattern.

Reading starts while the interviews are still being done, so what you read in one transcript
changes what you ask in the next.

**In plain terms.** A transcript is the typed record of an interview (the section on recording and transcribing).
Reading it for analysis is slower than reading a letter. It has a set order.

First, read the whole transcript once, start to end, with the pen down. You are finding out what
the person said before you decide what any line means.

Then read it again, line by line. When a passage bears on your question, mark it. Write a short
label in the margin saying what it is about. That label is a code.

This is a second use of the word. The section on consent used "code" for R1, the label that
stands in for a person's name. Here a code labels a passage, not a person. Its job is practical. Later,
you can pull out every passage with the same label, from every transcript, and read them side by
side.

One passage often needs two labels, and that is fine. When the person's own words name the
thing well, use them as the label.

Keep a memo as you go. A memo is a note to yourself: what you noticed, what surprised you, what
you want to ask the next person.

Watch for the passage that does not fit. If four people describe one thing and a fifth describes
the opposite, the fifth is not noise. Mark it and keep it. It tells you where your pattern stops
being true.

Last, you do not wait until every interview is done. You read the second transcript before the
third interview, and what you read changes what you ask.

**Illustration.** Suppose you are running the practice interviews for the clinic question. Why do adult patients
with obesity stop coming back to a district hospital clinic in Chhattisgarh? You have typed up
your second interview. The respondent, R2, is a woman in her fifties who came for a year and then
stopped. The exchange below is made up, and her words are translated from Chhattisgarhi.

```table
| Speaker | Words |
| I | Tell me about the last time you came to the clinic. |
| R2 | It was in the rains. The bus from our village goes only in the morning, so I lose the whole day. My son has to take leave to bring me. |
| I | Mm. |
| R2 | And that time the sugar was fine. The doctor said it is under control. So I thought, if it is fine, why go every month? |
| I | You said the sugar was fine. What was coming every month for, then? |
| R2 | Standing in the line for the weighing. Everybody is there. The sister reads the number out loud. I did not want the whole line to hear it. |
```

Here is how the reading goes wrong first. You start marking on the first line. R2 talks about the
bus, so you write *distance* beside it. By the end of that answer you have a story in your head:
patients stop because the clinic is far. You write *non-compliance* at the top of the page. Every
later line now reads as more of the same.

Look at what that did. The word *non-compliance* is the clinic's word, not hers. It names her as
the problem before you know her reason. And her own account does not end with the bus. She kept
coming on that bus for a year. What changed was that the sugar was "under control", and then the
weighing, read out in front of the line.

Now do it in order. Read all six lines first, pen down. Then go back and label each passage.

```table
| Passage | Code |
| The bus goes only in the morning, so I lose the whole day | a whole day lost to one visit |
| My son has to take leave to bring me | needs family to take time off |
| If it is fine, why go every month? | "if it is fine, why go" |
| The sister reads the number out loud | weighed in front of the line |
```

The first answer carries two codes, because it says two things. The third label is her own
words, because nothing you could write names it better.

Then write a memo. For example: "Distance came up first, but she did not stop when the bus was
hard. She stopped after she was told her sugar was under control. Next time, ask what a visit is
for, in their words. Ask what happens from the moment they arrive, and do not name the weighing
first."

That memo changes your third interview. It adds an open question to your topic guide and keeps
a leading one out of it.

Suppose, last, that R4, a man in his sixties, lives further out than R2 and has not missed a
visit. That is a case that does not fit "patients stop because the clinic is far". Mark it and
keep it. It tells you distance alone does not decide who stops, and it gives you a question: what
does R4 have that R2 does not?

**Where this picture breaks.** A code here is your own label for finding and comparing passages. It is not yet a finding.
Suppose two transcripts out of five carry "weighed in front of the line". That does not tell you
what share of the clinic's patients stop for that reason. The five were chosen, not drawn at
random (the section on who you talk to).

The labels also belong to one reader. Another person reading the same six lines might label them
differently. S36-R2 turns first labels into a set of codes that a second person can apply and
reach the same passages. A first reading does not do that.

**Must know points for you.**

- Coding is not counting. A code is a label for finding and comparing passages. If you tally how many transcripts carry it and report a percentage, you have made a rate out of people who were chosen, not drawn. Write "two respondents described..." and say who they were, not "two in five patients".
- Read the whole transcript once before you mark anything. A label written on the first page bends how you read the third. And the first reason a person gives need not be the one that decided things for them.
- Label with the respondent's words or a plain description of what happened, never with the clinic's verdict. "Non-compliance" names the patient as the problem before you have understood her reason. "If it is fine, why go" keeps her reason on the page, and it is the kind of label a trainee should be corrected towards.
- Keep the case that does not fit, and mark it. If you drop it, your pattern looks cleaner than the data, and you lose the one passage that shows where the pattern stops being true.
- Write a memo after each transcript, before the next interview, and let it change the topic guide. A memo that nobody reads before the next interview is a diary, not analysis.
- Your first codes show what is in these transcripts as one reader saw it. They do not show that a second reader would label the same passages the same way. Until someone else can apply your codes and reach the same passages, do not present them as the study's results. Present them as your working notes.

**Exercise 1** (interpretation). Here is a made-up passage from a practice interview for the clinic question, translated from
Hindi. R5 is a man in his forties.

```table
| Speaker | Words |
| I | What happens on a clinic day, from when you leave home? |
| R5 | I leave before six. At the clinic there is a token, then a long wait. By the time the doctor sees me it is afternoon. The shop stays closed that day. |
| I | Tell me more about the shop. |
| R5 | It is my shop. If it is closed, nobody earns that day. My wife says go, your health comes first. But she is not the one who has to open the shop late. |
```

Read it through once. Then write a code beside each passage that bears on why people stop coming,
using his own words for at least one of them. Then write a one-line memo for your next interview.
