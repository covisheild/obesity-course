# S36-R1-C09 · The interviewer is part of the data

**Definition.** What a respondent says in an interview depends in part on who is asking, and where.

Britten (1995) states the general case. Researchers "need to consider how they are perceived by
interviewees and the effects of characteristics such as class, race, sex, and social distance on
the interview". The problem is sharper when the interviewer is a doctor. A respondent who is, or
may become, a patient "may wish to please the doctor by giving the responses he or she thinks the
doctor wants". The place matters too: "The setting of an interview affects the content".

DeJonckheere and Vaughn (2019) name the same pull as a power imbalance. So does the textbook
Principles of Sociological Inquiry (2012): interviewers "must be aware of and attentive to the
power differential between themselves and interview participants". It suggests letting the
respondent choose the place. In health care the
interviewer "may be a part of the patient's healthcare team or have contact with the healthcare
team".

Mays and Pope (2000) make the effect something a study must report. Reflexivity, in their words,
"means sensitivity to the ways in which the researcher and the research process have shaped the
collected data". The effects of "age, sex, social class, and professional status" on the data are
to be discussed.

None of these sources measures how large the effect is. They describe its direction: towards the
answer the respondent thinks the interviewer wants. An error with a direction, repeated in every
interview the same person runs, is a systematic error in Book 0's sense. It is bias, and more
interviews by the same interviewer in the same setting do not shrink it.

What would overturn this: interviews of the same kind of respondents, on the same questions, by
interviewers who differ only in who they are, giving the same accounts.

**In plain terms.** The last section showed you how to ask without leading. This one is about something no wording
can fix: you.

People answer the person in front of them. A patient talking to a doctor in the doctor's own
clinic knows the doctor has views about weight, tablets and follow-up. Many patients will say what
they think the doctor would like to hear. The same patient, talking at home to someone with no
link to the clinic, may say something quite different.

It is not only doctors. Age, sex, class, the job you do and the clothes you wear all tell the
person something about you. So does the place. A consultation room and a kitchen draw out different
stories.

Book 0's section on random and systematic error gives you the right word for this. The pull
towards pleasing the interviewer has a direction. It works the same way in every interview the
same person runs, in the same room. So it is bias: an error that points one way and does not
shrink when you collect more. Twenty interviews by the clinic's own doctor do not cancel it. They
repeat it twenty times.

You cannot step out of your own interviews. What you can do is make the pull weaker, and make it
visible. Say who you are and who you are not. Promise, truthfully, that nothing said will reach
the clinic. Interview where the person is at ease. Then write down who you were to them, so that
anyone reading your transcripts can judge.

How big is the effect? The papers this section rests on do not measure it. They describe its
direction and tell you to take it seriously.

**Must know points for you.**

- Doing more interviews does not average out the interviewer. If one person runs them all, in the same setting, the same pull is in every one of them. That is bias in Book 0's sense: an error that points one way and does not shrink with more data. To change it, change the interviewer or the setting. Adding interviews will not.
- A run of praise from patients interviewed by their own clinic's staff is not evidence that the clinic is liked. It is what Britten's "wish to please the doctor" would produce. Before you accept such a finding, ask who did the interviews and where.
- Do not interview your own patients for research if you can avoid it. If you cannot, tell them they may say what they really think, and do not correct them.
- Before the first question, say who you are and who you are not. Say that nothing the person says will change their care. DeJonckheere and Vaughn advise the second; in a clinic, it is the sentence that makes the rest of the interview worth having.
- Write field notes about yourself, not only about the respondent. Record who you are to them, where you sat, who else could hear, and any moment you steered. Mays and Pope expect a study to report how the researcher's age, sex, class and profession may have shaped the data. You cannot report what you did not write down.
- In India, power differences make it hard for people to refuse a researcher outright, as the ICMR guidelines note. Disagreeing with one is likely to be hard for the same reason. Expect more agreement than people feel. How hierarchy in Indian clinics and offices shapes interviews is taught in this book's second rung.
- None of the sources this section rests on puts a size on the interviewer's effect. And nothing inside one transcript shows which answers it shaped. The moves here make the pull weaker and visible; they do not measure it. When a finding depends on it, the check is to compare what different interviewers heard, which is second-rung work.

**Exercise 1** (interpretation). A made-up report on the NCD clinic says: "All 12 patients interviewed by the clinic's diabetes
educator said they were satisfied with the service. Missed follow-up is therefore not due to
dissatisfaction." Say what this finding shows, what it does not show, and what you would ask
first.

**Exercise 2** (design). Plan the part of your five pilot interviews that deals with who you are. Write the two or three
sentences you will say about yourself before the first question. Then write the headings of the
field notes you will fill in about yourself after each interview.

**Exercise 3** (teaching). A journalist has three minutes. A hospital has told them: "Our interviews show patients love the
NCD clinic." Help them decide what to ask the hospital, with no jargon.
